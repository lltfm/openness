At least this: if Cain is the present self and Abel the future self, then the first murder becomes strangely intimate. Cain does not merely kill another person—the present destroys the future. The capacity to both imagine a better future and sacrifice for it, dies. Every choice contains some amount of this: the hours wasted, the pleasures indulged, the debts accumulated—each disappears into the past while leaving something less for tomorrow's awakening. Then God's question becomes newly unsettling: "Where is Abel?" Where is your future self? Perhaps the answer to Cain's reply—"Am I my brother's keeper?"—is quietly yes. The present is the only keeper the future has. Tomorrow cannot reach backward to protect itself; it possesses no hands except the hands we have now.

But also perhaps this: something stranger happens every morning: Abel becomes Cain. The future self, completely dependent upon choices it cannot influence, eventually awakens as the present self and inherits what another self prepared—stronger hands from yesterday's practice, an easier morning from yesterday's restraint, a friendship preserved by yesterday's apology, or perhaps debt, injury, loneliness and unfinished grief. We continually awaken inside consequences whose chooser no longer exists except as memory. We are always sending gifts or wounds forward to someone we will eventually become.

Maybe this: the two sacrifices of Cain and Abel represent two different relationships with change. Cain offers extra fruit already grown—his present abundance. Abel offers the firstborn of his flock—his greatest future possibility. Abel demonstrates his willingness to change while Cain offers only more of the same. Perhaps this is why in the story God favors Abel's offering yet remains silent for Cain's: when faced with a time of trial requiring sacrifices, true faith is the humility and will required to let go of our most precious possibilities and adapt to a future we would have never otherwise chosen.

Or perhaps this: at first we imagined Cain as present and Abel as future, but perhaps they are not permanent identities. At every moment we are Cain to someone younger than us and Abel to someone older than us. We are simultaneously the vulnerable inheritor of yesterday and the powerful ancestor of tomorrow. Stretch that across generations and the story becomes larger still. A generation can accumulate debts whose burden another generation inherits. It can preserve knowledge whose usefulness it will never witness. Parents shape conditions inhabited by children they cannot become. People long dead built roads we travel and created wounds we still encounter. Humanity is an immense procession of Cains becoming Abels becoming Cains, and some of those Cains sacrificed for futures that would never benefit them——why?

Or perhaps even this: let us return to the field before the murder, while Abel is still alive and tell a different story. Cain is angry, but need not deny his anger; he need only become spacious enough to notice his anger and step back from it before he becomes it. And then, instead of striking out, Cain can listen. Listen to the sky, to the field, to his anger—to Abel's silence. For the future self cannot literally speak. Cain must choose to imagine him. Imagine waking as his future self tomorrow morning. And then listen to what he imagines his future self also wants.

Cain listens then, yet he need not obey Abel either. The present self hungers, hurts and feels the sunlight now. If tomorrow becomes another authority demanding endless sacrifice, we have merely replaced one domination with another. Abel should not defeat Cain and Cain should not defeat Abel; instead each should listen to the other.

Yet Cain cannot completely know the person Abel will become; tomorrow contains encounters, losses, beauty and transformations today cannot foresee. He can only prepare without controlling, love without possessing, and send gifts forward without demanding that tomorrow become the tomorrow he imagined.
