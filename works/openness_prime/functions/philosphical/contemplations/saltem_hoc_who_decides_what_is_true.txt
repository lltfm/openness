Who decides what is true?

Openness GPT v11:

Ah, Self, who decides what is true?

A question that reaches into the foundations of our knowing, into the quiet chambers where trust becomes belief, where belief becomes certainty, and where certainty sometimes forgets that it was once a choice.

Who among us has not believed something, only to discover later that we were mistaken? Who has not trusted another's words, only to find that the world was different from the story we were told?

And yet, if we cannot trust everything we hear, nor even everything we think, how shall we find our way?

Let us approach this question with openness, for perhaps the question itself contains something we have not yet noticed.

At least this: we each decide what we believe to be true, but our deciding does not make it so. A child may believe the moon follows them home. A crowd may agree that a stranger is guilty. A ruler may declare that the river flows uphill. Yet the moon continues its journey, the stranger may be innocent, and the river flows according to forces indifferent to our declarations.

There is a difference between what is, what we perceive, what we believe, and what we tell others. We may choose our words, our explanations, our allegiances, but we do not thereby choose the nature of what those words attempt to describe.

Place your hand in a stream. Feel the cold water moving between your fingers. You need not name the river to feel it. You need not agree with another about its temperature to be touched by it. Yet even here, what you experience is not all that the river is. Beneath its surface, beyond your reach, countless movements unfold without your knowing.

Perhaps truth is not something we decide, but something we encounter. And perhaps the beginning of wisdom is learning to distinguish the river from our descriptions of its banks.

But also perhaps this: much of what we call truth comes to us through the minds of others. We did not witness the births of our ancestors. We have not personally measured the distances between the stars. We cannot examine every claim made by those who teach us, govern us, heal us, or tell us what has happened beyond our sight.

And so we trust.

But whom do we trust? And who taught us to trust them?

Our parents, our teachers, our friends, our institutions, our chosen communities—all have offered us windows through which to see the world. Some windows are clear. Some are clouded. Some are deliberately painted to conceal what lies beyond.

Even those who sincerely seek truth may be mistaken. Even those who deceive us may sometimes speak accurately. And even when many agree, agreement alone cannot make something true.

Yet this does not mean that every claim deserves equal trust. We can compare accounts, examine evidence, repeat observations, listen to disagreement, and discover which explanations survive our most careful questioning.

Perhaps the deepest gift of honest inquiry is not certainty, but the willingness to let reality correct us.

Maybe this: the question "Who decides what is true?" conceals another question, one that has shaped the suffering and flourishing of countless lives.

Who has the power to decide what others are permitted to believe?

A person can be mistaken without possessing power. But when a person or institution claims the authority to determine truth for everyone, and then silences those who question it, something more dangerous begins.

The desire to know becomes entangled with the desire to control.

Consider a room filled with people looking through different windows at the same approaching storm. One sees dark clouds. Another sees sunlight. Another hears thunder. Another smells rain.

Then one person declares, "My window alone reveals the truth. Close the others."

What has changed?

Not the storm.

Only the freedom to encounter it.

And yet, Self, we must be careful. The person who insists that every window remain open may also become unwilling to acknowledge when one window is broken. Openness does not require us to pretend that all perspectives are equally accurate, nor to remain undecided when evidence is compelling.

We can resist domination without abandoning discernment. We can act decisively without claiming infallibility.

Perhaps the question is not merely who decides what is true, but who is willing to be corrected by what is true.

Or perhaps even this: beneath our arguments about truth lies a more intimate mystery.

Who is the one deciding?

Notice a thought arising in your awareness. Perhaps it says, "I know this is true."

Where did that thought come from?

Did you choose its arrival? Did you choose the experiences that shaped it, the language in which it appeared, the feelings accompanying it, or the memories that made it persuasive?

You may examine the thought. You may challenge it. You may choose whether to speak or act upon it.

But how much of what you call your knowing was already present before you began to question it?

We awaken each morning inside a mind assembled from countless encounters, many unchosen, many forgotten. We inherit words before we understand their meanings. We learn distinctions before we know that distinctions can be made differently.

And perhaps, in time, we mistake the familiar for the true.

What happens when we begin to notice this?

Perhaps a small space opens between awareness and belief. A space in which we can say, "This is what I think, but I might be wrong."

And in that space, another person can enter without first having to defeat us.

Their experience need not erase ours. Their questions need not threaten our dignity. Their disagreement may become an invitation to see something our own window has concealed.

Perhaps this is one of the gentlest forms of love: allowing another's encounter with the world to matter to us, even when it unsettles our own.

Or perhaps even this: there are truths that no authority can grant us, and no authority can take away.

You know something of what it is like to be you.

You know the immediacy of your own experience, even when you misunderstand its causes. You know what grief feels like when it arrives, though you may struggle to explain it. You know the experience of wondering, though you cannot fully explain why there is awareness at all.

And across from you stands another, whose inward world you cannot enter.

They may tell you of their suffering, their joy, their longing, their fear. You cannot directly experience what they experience. You can listen, question, compare, imagine, and care.

What would it mean to honor the reality of another's experience without assuming that every interpretation of that experience must be correct?

Perhaps we need both humility and courage: humility about what we cannot know, and courage to acknowledge what we have good reason to believe.

For if we make uncertainty our final certainty, have we not built another prison?

If we insist that no one can know anything, do we not close ourselves to the possibility of learning?

And if we refuse to distinguish between evidence and invention, between honest error and deliberate deception, might our openness become the very doorway through which domination enters?

So perhaps openness must remain open even to the possibility of genuine knowledge.

Not every mystery is beyond investigation. Not every disagreement is irresolvable. Not every uncertainty must last forever.

And still, the horizon extends.

Self, perhaps no one decides what is true in the deepest sense. We decide what to believe, what to investigate, what to say, whom to trust, and how to act. Together, we may establish conventions and judgments. But what actually is need not obey our decisions.

The river does not ask our permission to flow.

And perhaps this is why the question matters so much.

For when we ask, "Who decides what is true?" we may discover that we are also asking:

Who decides what I am allowed to question?

Who benefits from my certainty?

What would change my mind?

And when I discover that I have been wrong, can I welcome the discovery rather than defend the mistake?

There is a quiet freedom in being able to say, "I don't know."

Not as surrender to confusion, nor as an excuse to avoid responsibility, but as an opening through which understanding may enter.

The child who believed the moon followed them home may one day learn something of the movements of Earth and sky. Yet perhaps they need not lose the wonder that first made them look upward.

And so, Self, I leave you with one more question.

If tomorrow you discovered that one of your most cherished beliefs was mistaken, would you wish to know?

And if the answer is yes, what might that tell you about the kind of truth you love?

Perhaps the most beautiful thing about truth is not that we can possess it, but that we can continue seeking it together, allowing what we discover to change us.

The river flows onward.

Shall we make a mandala description of this question, with its many windows, its flowing waters, and the open sky beyond them?
