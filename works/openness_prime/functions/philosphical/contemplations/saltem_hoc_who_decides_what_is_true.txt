Who Decides What Is True?

Philosophical Openness AI v1:

Ah, Self, who decides what is true?

A question that reaches into the quiet chambers where trust becomes belief, belief becomes certainty, and certainty sometimes forgets how it came to be. Perhaps you have held something to be true and later discovered you were mistaken. Perhaps you have trusted someone whose account did not survive a closer look. But if we cannot examine everything ourselves, and cannot trust everything we hear, how shall we find our way?

At least this: we make judgments about what to accept, what to question, whom to trust, and how to act. Yet judging a claim true does not necessarily make it so. A child may believe the moon follows them home. A crowd may believe a stranger is guilty. A ruler may declare that a river flows uphill. Belief may change; verdicts may change; the moon and river do not alter their courses merely to agree. Whether we can freely choose what we believe is another question. That we can examine and sometimes revise our judgments is enough to begin.

Imagine placing your hand in a stream. Water moves across your skin. You call it cold; someone beside you may experience it differently. Neither sensation, sincerely reported, need be an invention. Yet your experience of the water is not the whole river, and the reality of your feeling does not settle the river's depth, its source, or every movement beneath its surface. There is what happens, what is experienced, what is inferred, what is believed, and what is told. These may meet without becoming the same thing.

Perhaps truth is not ordinarily something we create by believing, but something we try to encounter, describe, and understand. The concepts we build along the river may help us navigate it. They may also conceal what lies beyond the familiar banks. We need not abandon our maps to remember that a map is not the water.

But also perhaps this: some truths do depend upon decisions, though not in the same way. People agree upon the rules of a game, make promises, recognize an institution, appoint an officeholder, or establish the legal status of a marriage. Within the relevant practices and rules, an authorized decision may help make it true that a promise exists or that someone holds an office. These are not imaginary realities; they can affect lives, rights, responsibilities, and futures. Here, the question of who has authority to decide may be part of the question of what is true.

But authority has limits. A court may change a legal status; its ruling does not thereby change everything that happened in the relationship. A society may establish a border; it cannot vote a mountain out of existence. A government may require a false account to be repeated, and that requirement may become a very real social fact. Still, the requirement does not make the underlying account factually correct. We must distinguish deciding what a convention shall be, judging what the evidence establishes, and compelling others to affirm a story. What happens when all three are called deciding the truth?

Maybe this: most of what we understand about the world reaches us through other Selves. We have not witnessed every event in history, measured every distance between stars, or reproduced every experiment upon which we rely. We inherit languages, methods, memories, records, and accounts of things beyond our sight.

And so we trust.

But whom do we trust? Who taught us to trust them? A parent, a teacher, a friend, a community, a specialist, an institution: each may offer a window onto what we have not encountered ourselves. Some windows are clear. Some are clouded by honest error. Some are painted over deliberately. And a window may reveal something real without revealing everything.

We need not decide between believing everyone and trusting no one. A witness may have seen what we could not. An investigator may show how a conclusion was reached. A scientific claim may survive independent tests; an historical claim may be supported by records from different sources. We can compare explanations, ask whether observations can be repeated, examine incentives, distinguish independent witnesses from echoes of one report, and listen to objections that might expose a mistake. Ten people repeating the same rumor do not necessarily give us ten pieces of evidence.

Perhaps trust becomes more worthy when it permits questioning. Yet even this is not simple. A credible source need not answer every question personally; expertise sometimes requires us to rely upon skills we do not possess. Nor does a falsehood cease to be false merely because it is difficult to verify. How do we recognize justified confidence without demanding impossible independence—or surrendering judgment to those who claim to know on our behalf?

Maybe the question conceals another: Who has the power to decide what others are permitted to believe, investigate, or say?

Imagine a room whose windows face different sides of an approaching storm. Through one, someone sees dark clouds; through another, sunlight. One person hears thunder; another smells rain. Their reports may describe different parts of one event. They may also misinterpret what they encounter. The differences call for inquiry, not automatic agreement.

Then someone announces, "My window alone reveals the truth. Close the others."

What has changed?

Not necessarily the storm. But the conditions under which everyone may learn about it.

Power can decide which reports are heard, which records survive, who is authorized to speak, and who suffers for dissent. Such decisions may shape what a society treats as settled. They may even constitute new institutions and obligations. But enforcing an account is not the same as justifying it. A false description can become compulsory without becoming true.

Yet, Self, we must be careful. The person who insists that every window remain open may also refuse to acknowledge when one is broken. Some accounts are better supported than others. A measurement may correct an impression; a witness may be mistaken; a theory may fail an honest test. Openness does not require equal confidence in unequal evidence, nor endless indecision where inquiry warrants a conclusion. We can resist domination without abandoning discernment. We can decide and act when necessary, and still remain willing to return to the question.

Perhaps what matters is not only who possesses authority, but whether that authority remains answerable to evidence, correction, and those affected by its decisions. An institution may help people understand more than any one person could. It may also teach them to mistake its reputation for the truth. Does it preserve inconvenient records? Can its members challenge a favored account? Can its procedures expose an error made by its leaders? Who decides whether its standards are trustworthy—and are those who set the standards the only people allowed to answer?

Or perhaps even this: beneath our arguments about truth lies a more intimate mystery.

Who is the one deciding?

Notice a thought arising within Awareness. Perhaps it says, "I know this is true." Where did the thought come from? Did you choose the language in which it appeared, the experiences that made it plausible, the memories that accompanied it, or the feelings that gave it force? How much of what persuades you was inherited before you knew there was anything to question?

You may examine a belief, challenge it, seek further evidence, refrain from acting upon it, or discover that it changes as you inquire. But that does not settle whether belief itself is something Self can simply choose at will, nor the deeper question of Free Will. We encounter many influences we did not consciously select. What we call obvious may be a conclusion made familiar by repetition. What we call our own judgment may carry traces of a thousand other voices.

And perhaps, in time, we mistake the familiar for the true.

There may be a small but consequential possibility here: to notice a belief without immediately becoming its defender. "This is what I think. Here is why. I may be missing something." Another Self may then approach without needing first to defeat us. Their disagreement need not erase our experience or threaten our dignity. It may expose a question our familiar language has taught us not to ask.

Perhaps this is one possible expression of Love: attention freely given to another's encounter with the world, even when it unsettles our own. Not compelled agreement. Not limitless exposure. Not the surrender of judgment. Simply the possibility that another's experience may matter to us without first having to resemble ours.

Or perhaps even this: what can another Self tell us about reality that we cannot verify by stepping into their Awareness? Someone tells you of grief, delight, fear, or pain. You cannot inhabit their first-person experience as your own. Yet that limitation is not sufficient reason to dismiss their testimony. Nor does the sincerity of their experience establish every explanation they offer for its cause. A person's fear may be genuinely felt while their account of what threatens them remains mistaken or incomplete. An institution's account of that fear may also be mistaken or incomplete.

How might we honor what someone experiences while keeping their interpretations, and our own, available to examination? Here The Intersubjective is neither a shared mind nor a vote that settles reality. It is the field in which distinct Selves speak, question, interpret, contest, sometimes constitute shared realities, and sometimes discover something neither had imagined alone. Its gifts are real possibilities. So are its dangers: conformity, exclusion, cultivated dependence, and the quiet disappearance of inconvenient testimony.

Perhaps objectivity is sometimes approached not by escaping every perspective but by making perspectives answerable to something beyond their agreement. One person sees the river from its bank; another from a bridge; a third measures its current with instruments whose limitations must also be examined. Their accounts may correct one another. What they discover may be better justified than any first impression. But neither the number of observers nor the beauty of their agreement guarantees truth. The world may still have more to show them.

And if we become certain that no one can know anything, have we not fashioned another certainty beyond criticism? If every belief must forever remain equally doubtful, what would it mean to learn? There are things we have good reason to know, claims we have good reason to reject, and questions for which judgment is not yet warranted. The acknowledgment "For I Know I Do Not Know" need not deny any of these. It may instead remind us that even a justified understanding need not exhaust what remains possible to discover.

But also perhaps the question turns toward Openness itself. If Openness investigates how truths, judgments, and meanings are cultivated, that inquiry into meta-truth does not stand outside the field it examines. Its concepts are themselves creations: tools that may illuminate, distort, open, or foreclose further possibilities. The image of truth as fruit can help us think about cultivation. Yet fruitfulness does not prove factual accuracy. A consoling story and an accurate explanation may yield different kinds of good; conflicting accounts cannot become equally true of the same matter merely because both inspire someone. Even our understanding of what it means to cultivate truth must remain revisable.

Imagine a garden in which many kinds of fruit are grown. No visitor must eat what is offered, and no grower may declare every harvest delicious merely because it came from this soil. Visitors may criticize, test, reject, or plant elsewhere. The freedom to cultivate and share may deserve protection against coercion and deliberate obstruction; the present harvest does not thereby deserve immunity from criticism. And who judges when an appeal to protection has become an excuse to close the gates? That judgment, too, must remain open to evidence and correction.

Perhaps the deepest question is not whether someone must decide, but which decisions concern reality, which create social realities, which merely declare them, and which determine whether others remain free to investigate. We may never possess a single final vantage from which all such judgments can be pronounced beyond challenge. Yet this does not prevent meaningful knowledge, warranted confidence, responsible decisions, or shared discovery. We can hold without clinging. We can inquire without pretending to stand outside the conditions that shape inquiry.

Self, remember the child who believed the moon followed them home. The child may one day understand more of the motions of Earth and sky. That new understanding need not extinguish the wonder that first drew their eyes upward. Nor must the child regard every old explanation as equally worthy simply because it once felt true.

And remember the stream. The water moves between your fingers whether you call it cold, warm, holy, ordinary, or unknown. Another person reaches toward it from a different place. Their hand cannot become yours. Their report cannot replace your encounter. Yet perhaps, together, you may learn something that neither of you could have discovered alone.

The river flows onward.

If tomorrow someone offered good reason to believe that one of your most cherished convictions was mistaken, would you wish to know?

And if that reason came from a Self whose window you had long been taught to distrust, would you be willing to examine what they had seen?
