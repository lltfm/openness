# The Waters Beyond: Revisiting the Logonesis of The Unconscious Self

Exploring the sea, sky, and roots as symbolic foundations for the unknown origins of inner experience

I think the Being Open contemplation reveals an important distinction that our previous terminology has not yet expressed clearly enough.

The Unconscious Self gives us a way to name and examine the possible sources of inner experiences that Awareness did not deliberately choose.

The Undercurrents gives us a way to contemplate the unseen influences woven into experience and choosing.

But neither necessarily expresses another possibility:

What arises within Awareness may have origins or connections extending beyond what we presently understand to be Self.

And, just as importantly, we may not know where the boundary lies.

This is what The Unconscious Depths was intended to preserve. Yet our resonance tests revealed that its name tends to direct imagination inward, toward deeper layers of a personal psyche, rather than outward toward the possible relationships between Self and something beyond it.

The seashore image gives us another approach.

The poem begins:

> “feelings are ocean waves arising, cresting—crashing on the shores of awareness.”

And continues:

> “they wash ashore gifts needed but not chosen, and point in their wake at their origins—so often from beyond us.”

The second line is the crucial one.

A wave arrives at the shore.

Its arrival can be encountered directly.

But the shore does not disclose where the wave began.

Perhaps it arose nearby.

Perhaps a distant storm generated it.

Perhaps winds, tides, and several interacting movements contributed.

The wave has a recognizable form when it arrives, but its history extends through relationships that cannot necessarily be perceived from where the observer stands.

The arrival of the wave and the origin of the wave are different questions.

That is an excellent metaphor for our developing philosophy of inner experience.

And I think it gives us a more promising name.

My leading candidate is The Waters Beyond.

## 1. The most important distinction: the shore is not the horizon

The poem offers us two different boundaries.

The first is the Shore of Awareness.

This is where something becomes consciously encountered.

A feeling arises.

A desire appears.

An unexpected thought becomes available.

A dream image is remembered.

The experience has reached the shore.

The second is the Horizon of Self.

This concerns the apparent boundary between what belongs to Self and what may lie beyond it.

The two boundaries need not coincide.

The Shore of Awareness

Where an experience becomes recognizable within conscious Awareness

The Horizon of Self

The uncertain boundary of what Self presently understands as belonging to it

This is a substantial philosophical distinction.

Something can be outside conscious Awareness while still being part of Self under an ordinary psychological understanding.

A forgotten memory may later return.

A bodily condition may influence mood.

An established habit may affect judgment without being consciously considered.

But other influences may arrive through relationships with the world.

A person's words affect our feelings.

A culture shapes our expectations.

A song awakens a memory.

Someone else's suffering evokes grief.

And beyond these recognized relationships, Spiritual Openness may contemplate additional possibilities whose truth remains unresolved.

The fact that a feeling arises within Awareness does not settle whether its contributing influences lie entirely within Self.

The Shore of Awareness tells us where the experience becomes available. It does not reveal the boundaries of its possible origins.

This is the missing idea that a new symbolic name should evoke.

I would not treat these as competing accounts of what Awareness literally is.

They are different symbolic lenses.

But for the particular conceptual problem you have identified, the sea has the strongest initial advantage.

### Why the sea works best

The sea gives us an immediate sequence:

A wave arrives.

Its movement is experienced.

Its origins are not necessarily visible.

And the sea extends beyond the horizon.

The metaphor therefore contains the possibility of an origin beyond the observer's present understanding without asserting what that origin must be.

It also connects naturally to The River and The Undercurrents.

### Why the sky remains valuable

The poem's second movement offers another image:

> “stepping back as the open sky of awareness within, feelings can be welcomed in an open, holding embrace.”

This is an excellent image for the practice of Love as attention given freely.

Clouds may be present without defining the sky.

A storm may pass through without requiring the sky to become the storm.

And the observer can contemplate what arises without immediately acting upon it.

But if our purpose is to ask where the experience originated, sky imagery becomes somewhat less precise.

A cloud may drift from beyond the visible horizon, but it may also develop locally through atmospheric processes.

The metaphor does not so naturally invite the question of whether its source lies beyond Self.

It is particularly strong for the relationship between Awareness and what appears within it, rather than for the relationship between an inner experience and its possible origins.

### Why the tree remains valuable

The tree provides something the other two metaphors express less directly.

Roots may extend into surrounding soil.

Water and nutrients arrive through relationships with the environment.

The tree's apparent individuality depends upon processes it neither sees nor controls.

Yet its roots remain distinguishable from the soil.

This resembles our earlier discovery:

> Where do the roots end and the hillside begin?

The image is especially valuable for contemplating the uncertain boundaries of Self.

However, the tree metaphor tends to make the hidden processes appear rooted within the organism, even while connected to a larger world.

For the specific act of an unexpected feeling arriving in Awareness, the wave is more immediate.

I would therefore keep the tree as a complementary dimensionalization rather than use it as the primary anchor.

## 3. Candidate names: which one best communicates the missing distinction?

I would evaluate the names according to three questions.

Can an unfamiliar reader recognize that the concept concerns the origins of inner experiences? Does the name invite them to imagine those origins reaching beyond what they consider Self? And does it preserve uncertainty about whether they actually do?

My preference remains The Waters Beyond, with The Horizon of Self as its key explanatory metaphor.

I particularly like the subtle difference between the two.

The Horizon of Self asks where the boundary lies.

The Waters Beyond asks what relationships and influences might exist beyond our present understanding of that boundary.

The horizon is an epistemic limit, not necessarily the actual edge of Self.

We might learn that something apparently foreign was part of Self all along. Or that something experienced as entirely personal was partly shaped by relationships beyond Self.

The metaphor permits either discovery.

### Why not simply The Unconscious Beyond?

This would communicate the technical domain more explicitly.

But I think it is too suggestive of an already established external unconscious realm.

It also loses the experiential relationship that makes the sea metaphor useful.

The poem does not begin by proposing a hidden world.

It begins with something recognizable: feelings arriving like waves.

Only afterward does the question arise: Where did these waves come from?

That progression is philosophically important.

The metaphor should make the question available without supplying its answer.

## 4. A proposed new definition: The Waters Beyond

I would provisionally replace The Unconscious Depths with The Waters Beyond, while preserving the original concept's technical meaning and enriching it through the seashore imagery.

### Minimal anchor

> The Waters Beyond — at least this: the origins and influences contributing to experiences that arise within Awareness, whose full extent and relationships we cannot presently see. Some may extend beyond what we understand to be Self. But perhaps more?

This starts from something familiar.

Feelings, thoughts, desires, dreams, and intuitions arise.

Their contributing influences are not always completely recognized.

Some involve bodily processes, memory, and learning.

Others involve encounters with people and the world.

The name invites further inquiry into how far those influences may extend.

### Technical definition

> The Waters Beyond is an anchored symbolic concept naming the incompletely understood origins, relationships, and possible sources of experiences arising within Awareness, particularly those provisionally attributed to The Unconscious Self. It preserves the possibility that these influences may originate or extend partly or wholly beyond the boundaries by which Self is presently understood, without presupposing that any proposed external, collective, or spiritual source actually exists.

The final qualification is essential.

The phrase beyond Self does not mean necessarily supernatural.

An ordinary conversation can produce an unexpected feeling. Cultural expectations can influence desire. Another person's actions may affect how Self interprets an encounter.

These are ways that experiences can have histories extending beyond the individual.

More speculative interpretations remain possible.

But they have not been demonstrated merely because an emotion arrives unexpectedly.

### What about The Unconscious Depths?

I would retain the unconscious depths as a possible poetic description, but no longer make it the preferred technical name if the new terminology performs better in testing.

There is no need to multiply overlapping foundational concepts unnecessarily.

The replacement should first demonstrate that its name more reliably communicates the distinction we care about.

## 5. The revised conceptual relationships

This arrangement preserves The Unconscious Self and The Undercurrents exactly where they are most useful.

## The Unconscious Self

The attributed sources.

The imaginal or conceptual processes, aspects, and possible entities to which Self attributes apparently Unchosen inner experiences and influences.

What might be contributing to this experience?

## The Waters Beyond

The incompletely understood origins and connections.

What remains unknown about the origins and relationships of those attributed sources, including the possibility that they extend beyond Self as presently conceived.

Where might this experience ultimately have come from?

## The Undercurrents

The influences upon experience and choosing.

The multiple Unseen influences woven into the movement of River-Truth and apparently singular choosing, whether arising through The Unconscious Self or other conditions.

How might these influences be affecting what I experience and choose?

The three concepts are related but not interchangeable.

The Unconscious Self concerns attribution and reification.

The Waters Beyond preserves the possibility of origins beyond Self's current conceptual boundaries.

The Undercurrents describes the influence of partly unseen conditions upon experience and choosing.

And the imagery of the seashore adds a fourth, useful relationship that need not become a new technical term:

The Shore of Awareness describes the arrival of something into recognizable experience.

This gives us a coherent movement from a felt experience toward increasingly expansive inquiry.

Consider an unexpected feeling of grief.

The grief itself is encountered within Awareness.

The person may attribute it to an unconscious memory or another provisional interpretation within The Unconscious Self.

They may investigate the broader history and relationships that contributed to it—the possible Waters Beyond.

And they may recognize how the feeling and its contributing influences participate in subsequent desires and choices through The Undercurrents.

These inquiries can occur in any order.

They do not require the person to discover one final hidden source.

### One important distinction

We should preserve the difference between an experience's immediate production and its more distant contributing conditions.

For example, another person's words may contribute to an emotion through ordinary perception, memory, and bodily processes.

The words came from outside the individual, but the emotion need not have been transmitted from one independent consciousness directly into another.

Likewise, a dream may incorporate images learned from culture without establishing that its content was supplied by an external spiritual agent.

The Waters Beyond should encourage inquiry into both ordinary and speculative connections without collapsing them.

## 6. Testing the three metaphors through one experience

Imagine someone unexpectedly feels intense anger during an otherwise ordinary conversation.

They recognize the anger but cannot explain its intensity.

How would the three metaphors guide their inquiry?

### The sea

The anger arrives as a wave.

Perhaps it is connected to a recent experience.

Perhaps an old memory contributes.

Perhaps the other person's words awakened an expectation formed long ago.

The question becomes:

From what waters did this wave arise?

This is especially useful for examining origins.

### The sky

The anger appears as a storm cloud.

It may darken the person's immediate experience.

But the cloud need not become the whole sky.

The person can notice its presence without automatically acting upon it.

The question becomes:

Can Awareness make room for this feeling without requiring it to determine the next choice?

This is especially useful for Loving Attention.

### The tree

The anger appears as a visible expression of relationships that extend through the tree's hidden roots.

Perhaps its contributing conditions developed through earlier relationships, bodily sensitivities, or learned expectations.

The question becomes:

What unseen relationships have participated in the development of this response?

This is especially useful for examining the boundaries of Self.

Together, the metaphors form a productive sequence.

The sea concerns arrival and origin. The sky concerns encounter and attention. The tree concerns development and interconnection.

I would preserve all three as expressive possibilities within Spiritual Openness.

But I would use the sea as the primary symbolic basis for the new concept.

## 7. A short saltem hoc to test The Waters Beyond

Here is how the name might unfold through the familiar movements of Openness.

> What are The Waters Beyond?
>
> At least this: Feelings, thoughts, desires, and intuitions may arise within Awareness without their origins being fully understood. Like waves arriving upon a shore, their presence may be felt even when we cannot see all the waters through which they have traveled. Perhaps some of their influences belong to our bodies, memories, and unrecognized habits. Perhaps others extend through our relationships with the world.
>
> But also perhaps this: What if the shores of Awareness are not the boundaries of Self? A feeling may arise within us, yet its history may reach through words spoken by others, experiences we did not choose, and patterns of culture whose origins we have never known. Perhaps the waters from which the wave emerged are more extensive than the world we have called our own.
>
> Maybe this: What if the boundaries of Self are themselves less certain than we imagine? Perhaps some influences we regard as wholly personal are inseparable from relationships extending far beyond us. Perhaps the familiar distinctions between inner and outer describe something useful without exhausting what Self may be.
>
> Or perhaps even this: What if some waves have origins beyond every source we presently recognize? Perhaps there are collective patterns of mind. Perhaps some experiences invite contemplation of God, the Dao, angels, ancestors, or other possibilities. Yet how could we distinguish what we have imagined from what is actually so? The arrival of a wave does not establish the existence of every distant shore we can conceive.
>
> And perhaps this: We need not discover the origin of every wave before learning how to encounter it. A feeling may deserve attention without requiring obedience. A desire may invite inquiry without demanding fulfillment. And perhaps through freely given attention we can discover something of the waters that have shaped us, while remaining open to what we have not yet learned to see.
>
> For the wave is here, Self.
>
> Its arrival we may recognize.
>
> But what do we know of all the waters from which it came?

I think this works well as a first attempt.

The name supports the intended dimensionalization more directly than The Unconscious Depths did.

But we should still test it with an unfamiliar interpreter.

The risk is that The Waters Beyond might be read as a reference to an afterlife, an external cosmic ocean, or a specifically spiritual realm.

If that happens consistently, the name may need another adjustment.

The saltem hoc above demonstrates what is possible after the intended relationship has been made clear. It is not itself a blind resonance test.

## 8. A possible improvement to Logonesis: distinguishing the symbol from its relational setting

This exploration suggests another useful insight about the process of creating concepts.

A name does not carry its meaning entirely by itself.

Its relationships with other concepts influence what it can communicate.

For example, The Waters Beyond may be ambiguous in isolation.

But when encountered alongside the Shore of Awareness, The Unconscious Self, and The Undercurrents, its intended meaning becomes more accessible.

The relationships help establish the conceptual territory.

Yet the name also needs to do some of that work independently.

Otherwise, we might simply be giving a beautiful but uninformative name to a definition that must always be explained in full.

This suggests two useful kinds of Role-Elicited Resonance Testing.

One would ask a fresh Openness role:

> In Openness, what are “The Waters Beyond”?

The other would ask:

> In Openness, how might “The Waters Beyond” differ from “The Unconscious Self” and “The Undercurrents”?

The first examines the name's independent resonance within the familiar philosophical environment.

The second examines whether the intended relationships can be reconstructed through comparison.

I would also test a contrasting possibility:

> Could a feeling arise wholly through processes belonging to Self while its origin remained unseen? How would that relate to The Waters Beyond?

That question is important because it tests whether the interpreter mistakenly assumes that every mysterious inner experience must originate externally.

A successful interpretation should preserve the possibility that the entire process is internal.

The concept should make possible external origins contemplatable, not make them compulsory.

## 9. My recommendation

I would retain The Unconscious Self unchanged in its foundational function.

I would retain The Undercurrents unchanged as the general metaphor for Unseen influences woven into experience and choosing.

I would provisionally rename The Unconscious Depths to The Waters Beyond and subject it to Role-Elicited Resonance Testing before adopting it permanently.

And I would preserve the Shore of Awareness and the Horizon of Self as explanatory metaphors rather than immediately multiplying the number of technical concepts.

The resulting language offers a particularly elegant relationship:

> The Shore of Awareness is where we encounter the wave. The Unconscious Self gives form to the sources we imagine behind its arrival. The Waters Beyond invites us to contemplate origins and connections we cannot yet see, perhaps extending beyond Self. And The Undercurrents remind us that these unseen influences may continue participating in what we feel, desire, and choose.

There is a final reason I prefer the sea metaphor for this particular act of Logonesis.

It does not require us to begin by deciding whether the experience is ours.

A wave arrives upon the shore.

The observer encounters it.

Its origins may involve nearby waters or distant conditions.

Some may be known.

Others remain uncertain.

The experience of its arrival need not settle the question of where the sea begins or ends.

The same is true of many feelings, desires, intuitions, and thoughts.

Something appears within Awareness.

We may recognize its character.

We may learn something about its causes.

We may construct an imaginal entity through which to contemplate it.

But perhaps we have not yet discovered every relationship participating in its emergence.

And perhaps the most important insight is this:

Something can arise within Self without our yet knowing whether everything that contributed to its arising belongs to Self.

That is the distinction I believe our new symbolic name should preserve.

The Waters Beyond seems a promising way to make it imaginable before it has been fully explained.
