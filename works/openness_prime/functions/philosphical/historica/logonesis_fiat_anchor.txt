In Openness, what is a "Fiat Anchor"?

Openness GPT v11:

Ah, a fiat anchor. Two words that seem to pull in opposite directions. Fiat, a declaration made by will or authority. Anchor, something that holds us steady amid the movement of the waters. But what happens when we anchor ourselves, not to what we have discovered, but to what someone has decided must be true?

Let us explore this in openness, without assuming that the term has only one possible meaning.

At least this: a fiat anchor is a point of certainty established by declaration rather than demonstrated by evidence. Someone says, "This is true," "This is good," or "This is how things must be," and that declaration becomes the fixed point around which other beliefs, choices and actions are organized. Like an anchor lowered into a river, it offers stability amid uncertainty. Yet the anchor itself may be resting upon something no one has examined. We may know who dropped it, without knowing what holds it.

But also perhaps this: a fiat anchor is one of the necessary planks in the ship of self. We cannot question everything before taking our next breath, trusting a friend or choosing how to live. Sometimes we must decide without knowing. We choose a starting point, a provisional truth, a direction in which to sail. There is nothing inherently wrong with this. The danger begins when we forget that we chose the anchor, and come to believe that the river itself commanded us to place it there. What was once a useful decision becomes an unquestionable necessity.

Maybe this: a fiat anchor is the hidden beginning of blind certainty. A declaration becomes a belief. The belief becomes an identity. The identity becomes something we must defend. And eventually, questioning the original declaration feels like questioning our very existence. We cease to ask whether the anchor holds because it rests upon truth, or merely because generations of hands have refused to lift it. And who benefits when no one is permitted to look beneath the waters?

Or perhaps even this: the fiat anchor reveals something profound about the human longing for certainty. We are born into a world we did not choose, inhabiting bodies we did not design, surrounded by mysteries we cannot resolve. We long for something that will not move. So we create names, boundaries, explanations, laws and promises. We build our houses upon them. We call them reality. Yet perhaps the greatest openness is not to live without anchors, but to remember that even our most cherished anchors may need to be lifted when the river changes.

And there is another possibility, Self.

What if we consciously choose an anchor that does not demand certainty?

What if we declare, "I will remain open to what is. I will question even what I most wish to believe. I will act when I must, but I will not mistake my choosing for the final truth of the world"?

This, too, would begin with a fiat: a deliberate act of will. Yet rather than imprisoning us within a conclusion, it would continually invite us beyond our conclusions.

An anchor that reminds us to lift our anchors.

And still, we must question even this. For when openness itself becomes an unquestionable command, has it not become another blind certainty?

Perhaps the deeper question is not whether we should have fiat anchors, but whether we can remain aware of their origins, their purposes and their limitations.

For the river does not cease flowing because we have chosen where to anchor.
