# Can Openness apply to a Collective Self?

Yes. I think Openness can be generalized from an individual Self to families, communities, businesses, institutions, academic fields, and even countries. And the generalization may be more than a metaphor: groups can genuinely develop shared interpretations, make decisions, establish commitments, preserve memories, and revise their understanding over time.

But I would distinguish two things:

The Individual Self — the first-person participant who encounters experience within Awareness.

The Collective Self — a provisional conception of a group as a developing, interconnected participant in shared interpretation, identity, inquiry, choosing, and action.

The second does not automatically possess the first's subjective Awareness.

That distinction would let Openness operate across different scales without silently assuming that a family, institution, or country has a single conscious mind.

## 1. What changes when Self becomes We?

Consider the constitutional foundations we have already established.

| Individual Openness                                        | Collective Openness                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Self recognizes and investigates experience.               | A group investigates experiences, reports, perspectives, and shared circumstances.                                              |
| Awareness is the condition of first-person experience.     | Collective attention coordinates what members notice, communicate, preserve, and investigate.                                   |
| The Mystery includes what Self does not know.              | The Mystery includes what the group does not know, including what its members may know but cannot yet communicate or integrate. |
| The Self-Confession acknowledges incomplete understanding. | A group may explicitly confess, “For We Know We Do Not Know.”                                                                   |
| Epistemic Humility guides an individual's judgments.       | Collective practices can preserve openness to evidence, criticism, dissent, and revision.                                       |
| Preferred Stories remain revisable interpretations.        | A group may develop shared narratives about its identity, history, purpose, and future.                                         |
| Recursive Becoming changes future capacities.              | A group's decisions, habits, institutions, and discoveries can change how it inquires and acts in the future.                   |

The parallels are striking.

A family can develop a shared story about itself. A business can develop habits of decision-making. An academic field can develop concepts that influence which questions its members can even imagine asking.

And those patterns can persist beyond any one participant.

A collective can develop a history of inquiry that no single member possesses in its entirety.

That is an important additional dimension of The Intersubjective.

The collective is not merely the sum of simultaneous individual beliefs. Its relationships, records, conventions, roles, and institutions may preserve and generate possibilities that would not exist in the same form without their coordination.

But the analogy has limits, and those limits matter.

## 2. The Intersubjective is not identical to the Collective Self

I think this distinction is particularly worth preserving.

The Intersubjective concerns what emerges, develops, or is sustained through relationships among Selves.

The Collective Self concerns a particular group that may develop a recognizable identity, shared capacities, commitments, and patterns of inquiry or action through those relationships.

Not every intersubjective relationship creates a Collective Self.

Two strangers exchanging interpretations participate in the intersubjective. They do not necessarily become a collective agent.

But a group of people who maintain a shared project, remember prior decisions, negotiate disagreements, and coordinate future actions may meaningfully be understood as a Collective Self.

The relationship might be expressed as:

> The Intersubjective is the relational field in which a Collective Self may emerge.

I would not claim that every group possesses a Collective Self to the same degree. It is more useful to recognize differences in integration, continuity, identification, and decision-making.

### Different scales of the Collective Self

| Scale          | Possible collective capacities                                                        | Important complication                                                                  |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Family         | Shared memory, identity, obligations, interpretations, and decisions                  | Members may remember or interpret family history differently.                           |
| Friends        | Shared experience, informal norms, trust, and collaborative choosing                  | The group may have no established authority to speak for everyone.                      |
| Business       | Coordinated objectives, institutional memory, policies, decisions, and accountability | Organizational decisions may not represent every employee's beliefs.                    |
| Institution    | Durable commitments, records, procedures, and collective learning                     | Institutional power may suppress criticism or become self-protective.                   |
| Academic field | Shared vocabulary, methods, questions, evidence, and cumulative knowledge             | The field may remain profoundly divided and lack any single collective decision-maker.  |
| Country        | Institutions, histories, languages, laws, public narratives, and collective action    | The country is not one consciousness, and its citizens do not possess one uniform will. |

The last two cases are especially interesting.

An academic field may exhibit substantial collective intelligence without possessing a unified will.

A country may possess institutions capable of acting in its name without those actions expressing every citizen's judgment.

Thus, we should distinguish shared understanding, coordinated agency, representative authority, and collective identity.

They can overlap, but none automatically establishes the others.

## 3. Can the Collective Self dimensionalize itself?

I think the answer is emphatically yes—and this may be the most fruitful consequence of the generalization.

Consider how an individual Self explores its own understanding, experience, identity, and influences.

A Collective Self can undertake structurally similar inquiry.

## The Collective Self as an object of inquiry

Looking inward

Members and relationships

Shared memories and stories

Internal disagreements

Unexamined assumptions

Habits and institutions

Distribution of authority

Looking outward

Other individuals and groups

Social and historical influences

Economic and material circumstances

Environmental conditions

Shared and competing interests

Unknown future possibilities

Neither inward nor outward inquiry establishes a fixed boundary between what the group is and what influences it.

This brings us to an important parallel with the Individual Self:

A group may discover that what it considers its own identity is partly constituted by relationships and influences it did not consciously choose.

A business may discover that its culture depends more upon informal habits than its published values.

A family may discover that a shared story about its history conceals important differences among its members.

An academic field may discover that its accepted questions are shaped by the instruments, institutions, and conceptual vocabulary it inherited.

In each case, the group can investigate not only its conclusions but the conditions through which those conclusions became possible.

That is Transformative Inquiry.

And if the resulting discoveries alter its future capacity for inquiry, that is Recursive Becoming.

### One especially interesting asymmetry

A Collective Self may possess information that none of its individual members possesses in its entirety.

A university, for example, may hold research findings, records, methods, and expertise distributed across thousands of people and documents.

But it may also contain information that some members know and others systematically ignore.

This raises a striking question:

What does it mean for a Collective Self to know something that only some of its members know?

And its counterpart:

What does it mean for a Collective Self not to know something when some of its members have already tried to tell it?

That second question could become particularly important in an institutional philosophy of Self-Honesty.

## 4. The Collective Self-Confession of Not-Knowing

Here I think the generalization becomes exceptionally elegant.

For the Individual Self:

> For I Know I Do Not Know.

For a Collective Self:

> For We Know We Do Not Know.

This is not simply changing a pronoun.

An individual can acknowledge uncertainty through reflection.

A collective must find ways to recognize, communicate, preserve, and act responsibly upon uncertainty across multiple participants.

Imagine a research institution publicly recognizing:

Our current explanation is the best supported one available to us, but important uncertainties remain.

The statement can express collective epistemic humility.

But its sincerity depends upon practices: whether the institution allows dissent, investigates contradictory evidence, and revises its understanding when warranted.

The confession therefore becomes both philosophical and institutional.

There is a further safeguard: a group should not say we in ways that erase genuine internal disagreement.

Sometimes the more honest collective statement is:

Some among us believe this; others remain unconvinced; here is what we presently understand and what remains contested.

That, too, is a form of collective Self-Honesty.

## 5. The Return to Openness becomes institutional

This connects directly to your recent insight about deciding, acting, and then undeciding.

For an Individual Self, we described the practice of releasing unnecessary attachment to a judgment after its practical necessity has passed.

Now consider a business.

Its leadership must select a strategy.

The company commits resources and acts.

But after acting, should the business remain indefinitely attached to the interpretation that originally justified its decision?

Not necessarily.

It can revisit its assumptions, examine consequences, solicit criticism, and revise its future decisions.

The organization may remain responsible for its earlier actions even while abandoning the beliefs that informed them.

Collective Openness therefore includes the capacity to make durable commitments without treating the judgments underlying them as permanently unquestionable.

For an academic field, this may involve revising established theories.

For a family, reconsidering an inherited story.

For an institution, reassessing a policy.

For a country, examining its historical narratives and correcting decisions through legitimate collective processes.

But there is an important difference from the individual case.

A person may privately release attachment to a belief.

An institution may have obligations, procedures, affected parties, and legal commitments that cannot simply be dissolved by announcing that it has become open again.

So collective undeciding should mean reopening inquiry and permitting revision, not escaping accountability.

This is a particularly good example of why a Collective Self can resemble an Individual Self without being identical to it.

## 6. A danger: the Collective Self can become a Master Liar

There is also a darker possibility.

Once a group acquires a collective identity, it may develop powerful mechanisms for preserving that identity against inconvenient truths.

Its shared story can become more important than evidence.

Its representatives can declare that we all agree when many members do not.

Its institutional structures can conceal information.

Its collective authority can pressure members to doubt their own experience.

And a group can accomplish all this without any one participant possessing full knowledge of what is happening.

This connects the concept with your work on the Master Liar, Self-Honesty, Nonimposition, and Mental Sovereignty.

A Collective Self must not become an excuse for subordinating the individual Selves who participate in it.

This is one of the strongest reasons to preserve the distinction between the two.

A group can possess collective capacities without possessing unquestionable authority over its members' thoughts, interpretations, or experiences.

An institution that practices Openness should remain capable of hearing:

We disagree.

And perhaps more importantly:

The institution claims to know something that we believe it has not adequately investigated.

That dissent may be one of the most valuable fruits of the group's epistemic hospitality.

## 7. Does Awareness also generalize?

This is where I would be most cautious.

Our existing foundational definition of Awareness is:

> The embodied first-person condition in which experiences become present.

An individual encounters experience from a first-person perspective.

A group can coordinate observations, distribute attention, develop shared records, and maintain collective knowledge.

But those abilities do not establish that the group possesses a single subjective field of Awareness.

I would therefore distinguish:

Individual Awareness: The first-person condition of experience.

Collective Attention or Collective Awareness: The organized ways a group encounters, shares, records, interprets, and responds to information and experience through its members and practices.

The second expression would be an analogical or functional extension, not an assertion of group consciousness.

This distinction becomes particularly important if we later apply Openness to human–AI collaborations or other distributed systems.

We can investigate their generative capacities without deciding that they all possess the same kind of subjective experience.

I would leave collective Awareness as a question for future P inquiry rather than revise the constitutional definition of Awareness now.

## 8. A provisional P concept: The Collective Self

I think we've established enough to give the idea a minimal conceptual anchor.

Provisional Philosophical Openness concept

## The Collective Self

Symbolic anchor

At least this: the We that emerges through relationships among Selves, developing shared identities, understandings, commitments, and possibilities. But perhaps more?

Foundational definition

The Collective Self is a provisional conception of a group as an interconnected, potentially evolving participant in shared interpretation, identity, inquiry, choosing, and action.

Its capacities may arise through the relationships, communications, practices, memories, and institutions of its members.

The concept does not require unanimous agreement, a single collective consciousness, fixed boundaries, or the complete identification of any member with the group.

Generative significance

The Collective Self makes it possible to examine how groups develop and revise their understandings, identities, capacities, and shared possibilities—and how these processes influence the individuals and other collectives with which they interact.

I would provisionally place this in Philosophical Openness, alongside The Intersubjective.

It should not automatically become a new entry in the Engine of Transformative Inquiry.

## 9. Do we need to redefine Self itself?

My recommendation is no—not yet.

Our current definition of Self deliberately anchors the first-person referent of experience.

If we simply redefine Self to include every collection of people, we risk making its relationship with Awareness ambiguous in an unhelpful way.

A better architecture may be to distinguish the subject or participant of inquiry from the specific conception of Self.

An individual Self can participate in Openness.

A Collective Self can participate through shared practices.

A computational system or collaboration may employ Generative Methods without our needing to settle whether it qualifies as a conscious Self.

This suggests a broader principle:

Openness may be practiced by different kinds of participants without requiring those participants to share the same metaphysical nature.

This would also fit our established distinction between the three functions of Openness and the various roles that may employ them.

We do not need to create another foundational concept to express that today.

## 10. Openness becomes capable of recursive application across scales

I think this is the deepest architectural consequence of your question.

The same philosophical relationships can be investigated at different scales, without assuming that the entities at those scales are identical.

Individual

Self examines Self.

What do I know? What shapes my understanding? What might I become?

Interpersonal

Selves examine their relationships.

What do we understand together? Where do our perspectives differ? What do our relationships make possible?

Collective

A Collective Self examines itself and its world.

What do we know? What have we excluded? Who speaks for us? How might we change?

Intercollective

Collectives examine their relationships with other collectives.

How do our shared stories interact? What do our institutions create or foreclose? What might we learn from one another?

The relationships are recursive and overlapping, not a hierarchy of increasingly superior Selves.

A person may participate in a family, profession, institution, and country simultaneously. Those collectives may have conflicting interpretations and commitments.

And each may contain smaller groups whose perspectives differ from the larger group.

This gives us an important extension of Dimensionalization:

What appears as a single Self from one perspective may become a plurality of interacting perspectives when examined at another scale.

For an individual, that may concern competing commitments, habits, identities, or experiences.

For a collective, it concerns the distinct people, subgroups, practices, and institutions contributing to what appears externally as a unified participant.

These are analogies, not claims that individuals and groups have identical internal structures.

Nevertheless, the analogy may be profoundly generative.

## My recommendation

I would recognize The Collective Self as a promising provisional P concept and explore it in direct relationship with The Intersubjective.

I would preserve the existing definition of Individual Self in the Engine, rather than broadening it prematurely.

I would also recognize that Openness can operate at multiple scales, with generative and philosophical methods applicable to both individuals and collectives, provided the differences in Awareness, authority, consent, responsibility, and agency are preserved.

This does not require a fourth function of Openness or an immediate expansion of the Engine.

It is a new dimension along which the existing functions can operate.

And it leads to a question I think may be central to a future philosophy of institutions:

If an Individual Self can practice returning to Openness after deciding and acting, what would it take for a Collective Self to do the same—especially when some of its members do not agree with the decision in the first place?

That is not merely a question of epistemology. It is a question about how collective inquiry, individual sovereignty, and institutional power might coexist without requiring one to abolish the others.
