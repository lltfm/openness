# Is Philosophical Openness mature enough today to contemplate "knowing"?

Editor: see S Openness's saltem_hoc_knowing.txt

I agree with your instinct, and I think the distinction you are drawing is important for the intellectual integrity of Openness.

My recommendation is to place this Saltem in Spiritual Openness (S Openness), and to defer an ambitious philosophical treatment of "Knowing" in Philosophical Openness (P Openness).

But I would make a further distinction: we already know enough to say something philosophically meaningful about knowing; we do not yet know enough to offer a comprehensive account of what knowing ultimately is.

Indeed, that distinction may be one of the most important things Openness itself needs to understand.

The contemplation is quite successful as an exploration of how a Self might live with knowing and not knowing. It is much less secure when it begins to explain the nature, mechanisms, or ultimate significance of knowledge.

The question I would put at the center of this review is:

Can Openness cultivate a wiser relationship with knowing without presuming to have resolved the mystery of knowing itself?

I think it can. And I think your Saltem offers an excellent way to do precisely that.

## 1. Why I believe this belongs in Spiritual Openness

The Saltem asks a philosophical question—What is knowing?—but its deeper movement is spiritual.

It begins with familiar acts of recognition, encounters the incompleteness of understanding, considers the dangers of certainty, and ultimately invites the Self into a different relationship with the unknown.

The progression is roughly:

Recognition

We encounter, remember, distinguish, and understand.

Recognition of Limits

Our understanding is not the entirety of what is.

Humility Without Paralysis

We may act upon what we know while remaining open to correction.

Participation in Mystery

Knowing may deepen our encounter with existence rather than exhaust it.

This is a spiritual movement because its central concern is not establishing the conditions under which a proposition qualifies as knowledge.

Its concern is what happens to the Self when it encounters both the power and the limits of its knowing.

That is an entirely legitimate and, I think, beautiful concern for Spiritual Openness.

Especially powerful is this passage:

> We may know something reliably without knowing everything about it.

I consider this one of the Saltem's most important insights. It protects Openness from confusing incompleteness with ignorance.

Similarly:

> Openness is not the refusal to know. Nor is it the refusal to choose. It is the willingness to let what we know remain answerable to what is.

This may be the philosophical and spiritual heart of the whole contemplation.

It avoids two dangers: Blind Certainty, in which the Self's understanding becomes immune to correction; and a kind of indefinite skepticism, in which the Self becomes unwilling to recognize or act upon sufficiently established knowledge.

I would preserve this passage nearly unchanged.

## 2. Why a comprehensive Philosophical Openness treatment would be premature

The difficulty is not that philosophical inquiry has little to tell us about knowing.

Quite the opposite.

Epistemology has developed an enormous body of work around this problem. Yet even the familiar model of knowledge as justified true belief encounters major counterexamples, and no comprehensive analysis of propositional knowledge has gained general acceptance.&#x20;

We should therefore distinguish what philosophy has established, what remains contested, and what Openness has yet to examine.

I see five foundational questions that Openness would need to confront before claiming a comprehensive philosophical definition.

1\. What distinguishes knowing from believing? A Self may believe something confidently and be mistaken. It may possess strong evidence without possessing knowledge. What makes the difference between justified belief and knowledge?

2\. What is the relationship between Awareness and what is known? Does Awareness directly encounter reality, represent reality, interpret experience, or participate in some combination of these? We should not resolve this through a poetic metaphor.

3\. Are there fundamentally different kinds of knowing? Knowing that something is true, knowing how to perform an action, knowing another person, and knowing what an experience feels like may not share one simple explanatory structure.

4\. How do individual and intersubjective knowing relate? How do testimony, shared inquiry, disagreement, scientific evidence, institutions, and social practices contribute to justified belief and knowledge? The Intersubjective provides a promising beginning, but not yet a complete answer.

5\. What are the limits of knowing? Does every genuine act of knowing require the possibility of revision? Are there propositions that can be known with certainty? Are some aspects of existence inaccessible to human understanding, or merely not yet understood?

These questions are not obstacles we must overcome before reflecting on knowledge.

They are reasons to avoid presenting a premature conclusion as a canonical philosophical foundation.

In particular, I would be cautious about adopting the assertion that knowing is fundamentally participation rather than possession.

Participation is a fruitful way of contemplating our relationship with reality. But as a philosophical definition, it raises questions that the Saltem does not answer.

What constitutes participation? What distinguishes successful participation from mistaken participation? Is mathematical knowledge a form of participation in the same sense as knowing another person?

I would rather leave these questions genuinely open.

## 3. What I would refine in the Saltem itself

I do not think the Saltem needs a major rewrite. Its imagery and progression are effective.

But several passages move quietly from spiritual invitation into philosophical assertion. I would soften those assertions so that the contemplation practices the very Openness it advocates.

A. The opening definition is too conclusive.

> Knowing is our awareness recognizing something through experience, memory, reason, or testimony.

Recognition, memory, reasoning, and testimony can also mislead. They are possible sources of knowledge, but describing them does not establish when knowledge has occurred.

I would change this to:

> Much of what we call knowing arises through experience, memory, reason, and testimony. Yet each may reveal something of what is, and each may sometimes mislead us.

B. Imagination should not be treated as another form of knowing.

> Knowing, then, may have many dimensions: what we can demonstrate, what we can infer, what we remember, what we have lived, and what we can only imagine.

This is one of the few places where I see a substantive conceptual problem. Imagination may lead us toward discovery, understanding, or knowledge, but what we imagine is not necessarily something we know.

Better:

> We encounter existence through what we can demonstrate, what we can infer, what we remember, what we have lived, and what we can only imagine. These are not interchangeable, nor do they all establish knowledge.

C. Two ways of knowing may actually be two ways of holding knowledge.

> There is a knowing that closes the question, and a knowing that makes the question more spacious.

The distinction is compelling, but the text is contrasting dispositions rather than identifying two kinds of knowledge.

I would suggest:

> There is a way of holding what we know that closes the question, and another that makes the question more spacious.

This small revision brings us much closer to the actual subject of the Saltem.

D. The deepest knowing need not always entail recognition of ignorance.

> The deepest knowing is inseparable from the recognition of not knowing.

Perhaps this describes certain profound human experiences. But we should not universalize it.

Some discoveries clarify matters so conclusively that previously legitimate uncertainties disappear.

The more defensible insight is that deepening understanding may reveal previously unrecognized limits, not that every advance in knowledge must enlarge our awareness of ignorance.

E. Participation rather than possession should remain a spiritual possibility.

> And so, perhaps knowing is best understood not as possession, but as participation.

I love this as a contemplative image. It may even help articulate something distinctive about Openness.

But I would adjust it slightly:

> And so, perhaps we might learn to approach knowing less as possession and more as participation.

That preserves its meaning without claiming to have discovered what knowing ultimately is.

One further editorial observation: the Saltem is somewhat long, and several passages return to the same theme of incompleteness and revision. I would consider a modest reduction while preserving the peach, grieving friend, night sky, and flame examples. Each illustrates a different dimension of the inquiry.

The final invitation to create a mandala should also be removed from the canonical Saltem. It is an artifact of the original conversation, not part of the contemplation.

## 4. What Philosophical Openness can responsibly say today

This is where I differ slightly from a complete postponement.

I think P Openness already has enough conceptual grounding to establish a provisional philosophical boundary around knowing, without attempting its ultimate definition.

For example:

PROVISIONAL P OPENNESS — NOT A CANONICAL DEFINITION

Knowing

Knowing is not identical to believing, feeling certain, or possessing a complete understanding.

Selves may acquire knowledge through experience, reasoning, memory, practice, and the testimony of others, although each of these can also contribute to error.

Knowledge of facts concerns what is true, while the grounds upon which a Self claims knowledge may vary in strength and remain fallible.

Openness therefore distinguishes the possibility of reliable knowing from claims of exhaustive or unquestionable understanding.

It encourages the examination and revision of beliefs without requiring the suspension of judgment wherever certainty is unavailable.

The deeper nature of knowing, including the relationships among Awareness, experience, understanding, and reality, remains open to philosophical inquiry.

This is deliberately limited.

Notice what it does not claim.

It does not say that all knowledge is uncertain. It does not say that knowledge is entirely constructed. It does not claim that subjective experience establishes external truth. And it does not pretend to solve how consciousness knows reality.

It is consistent with a fallibilist understanding of knowledge, in which people can genuinely know things even though their justification is not infallible. Fallibilism need not entail skepticism.&#x20;

This seems an appropriate degree of philosophical restraint for Openness at this stage.

I would not even make Knowing a fully canonical concept yet. The passage could remain a working philosophical note until we have further developed the relationships among Self, Awareness, The Intersubjective, and truth.

## 5. A particularly important distinction: the mystery of knowing versus the mystery encountered through knowing

I think this may be the deepest insight arising from your question.

There are actually two mysteries under consideration.

The first is the mystery of knowing itself.

How can Awareness know something beyond itself? How does an experience relate to what caused it? What transforms information into understanding? How can a Self justifiably claim to know something about a reality it never apprehends in its entirety?

These are philosophical questions. They deserve careful investigation, and some may remain unresolved.

The second is the mystery that knowing may reveal.

When a Self begins to understand the world more deeply, it may also become aware of previously unimagined dimensions of existence.

Knowing can transform not only what the Self believes, but how the Self experiences its relationship with reality.

This second mystery is especially appropriate for Spiritual Openness.

It does not require the Self to solve epistemology before encountering wonder.

Indeed, the night-sky passage illustrates this beautifully. Learning about the stars does not deprive the sky of its mystery. It may change and deepen that mystery.

Knowledge and wonder are not enemies.

Nor does Openness require mysteries to remain permanently unsolved in order to remain meaningful.

A mystery may become understood, while the larger horizon of inquiry remains open.

That is an important distinction because we do not want Spiritual Openness to acquire a hidden commitment to preserving ignorance.

The mystery is not sacred because it must remain unexplained. The encounter with mystery may remain meaningful whether or not an explanation eventually becomes available.

That, to me, is closer to the spirit of Openness.

## 6. My recommendation for the architecture of Openness

I would make three decisions at this point.

First, retain this Saltem in Spiritual Openness, centered upon the question What is knowing? Its purpose should be to explore the Self's lived relationship with knowing, uncertainty, humility, wonder, and the possibility of revision.

Second, preserve a modest Philosophical Openness boundary statement, but defer a canonical foundational definition of Knowing. We should not elevate a poetic description into an epistemological conclusion merely because it resonates with Openness.

Third, recognize that Knowing may ultimately become an important intersection among the three expressions of Openness:

| Expression             | Central concern                                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Philosophical Openness | What is knowing, what justifies claims to knowledge, and what are its limits?                         |
| Spiritual Openness     | How does a Self live with knowing, not knowing, and the mysteries encountered through both?           |
| Generative Openness    | How do knowing, imagination, discovery, creation, and interpretation contribute to new possibilities? |

These inquiries can inform one another without requiring identical answers.

## 7. The deeper question for Openness

There is one further implication I would not want us to overlook.

Openness must be able to acknowledge genuine knowledge.

If we become so committed to incompleteness, uncertainty, or ongoing questioning that we cannot confidently acknowledge what has been established, Openness risks becoming a sophisticated form of Blind Certainty about uncertainty itself.

That would be a serious contradiction.

Equally, we must avoid treating knowledge as an intellectual possession that grants the Self unlimited authority beyond the scope of what has actually been established.

This suggests a vital distinction:

Openness does not require the Self to doubt everything it knows. It requires the Self to remain capable of recognizing when something it thought it knew may be mistaken.

Those are profoundly different commitments.

The first could undermine knowledge. The second can contribute to its reliability.

And the distinction does not require us to solve the ultimate mystery of knowing.

It only requires that we take both knowing and fallibility seriously.

### Final judgment

I would retain this Saltem, make the small philosophical refinements above, and place it in Spiritual Openness.

I would not yet attempt the ambitious P Openness version.

But I also would not say that Openness is unprepared to speak about knowing. It is prepared to establish meaningful boundaries around what it can responsibly claim—and to acknowledge the questions it has not resolved.

There is a lovely symmetry here.

Perhaps Openness's first mature philosophical achievement concerning knowing should not be a comprehensive definition of knowledge, but a clear recognition of what it does and does not yet understand about knowing.

That is not a retreat from philosophical inquiry. It is a disciplined beginning.

And your Saltem, approached as a spiritual contemplation rather than a final explanation, already expresses much of the wisdom that such a beginning would require.
