Certainty

Foundational Definition:

Certainty concerns the degree to which Self regards an understanding, interpretation, proposition, or expectation as settled, and the grounds upon which that confidence rests.

Certainty has distinguishable experiential and epistemic dimensions. Self may feel certain that something is true, yet lack sufficient justification for that confidence. Conversely, Self may possess strong justification for an understanding while continuing to experience doubt.

Situated Certainty, or Situated Confidence, is the confidence Self encounters within Awareness, arising through its present feelings, interpretations, evidence, reasoning, and understanding of what it knows and does not know.

Self may seek increasingly objective understanding through evidence, rational inquiry, and engagement with other Selves. Yet its encounter with that understanding remains situated within Awareness. It encounters its own best-present understanding and assessment of justification, not an infallible perspective outside all experience.

The function of distinguishing these dimensions is to cultivate increasingly discerning confidence: neither mistaking felt conviction for established truth nor diminishing justified understanding merely because further uncertainty remains possible.

Essential Distinctions:

Felt Certainty. The experienced sense that something is true, settled, or beyond reasonable doubt. It may arise through perception, intuition, familiarity, emotion, memory, reasoning, or trust. Its intensity does not establish the truth of what is believed or the adequacy of its justification.

Justified Confidence. Confidence warranted by the available evidence, reasoning, methods of inquiry, and relevant alternatives. Its appropriate strength depends upon the quality and limitations of that justification, not merely upon Self's feeling of conviction. Justification can be examined and challenged by other Selves and need not be confined to Self's own assessment of it.

Situated Certainty / Situated Confidence. Self's presently encountered confidence in an understanding or proposition, within the conditions and limitations of its Awareness, knowledge, experience, and inquiry. It includes what Self feels, the justification Self recognizes, and the degree of confidence Self is prepared to adopt. It may be strongly warranted, poorly warranted, or somewhere between.

Felt Certainty versus Justified Confidence. Feeling certain and having good reasons to be confident are distinguishable. Neither automatically produces the other. Self may feel certain without sufficient evidence, or experience anxiety and doubt despite substantial justification.

Justification versus Truth. Justified confidence does not guarantee truth. An understanding may be well supported by available evidence and subsequently prove mistaken. Conversely, a belief may happen to be true without having been adequately justified.

Situated versus Objective. Situated Confidence does not mean that truth is merely subjective or that objective inquiry is impossible. It recognizes that Self encounters, evaluates, and revises its understanding from within Awareness, while evidence and realities beyond its present understanding may support or challenge its conclusions.

Situated Certainty versus Blind Certainty. Situated Certainty acknowledges the conditions and possible limitations of confidence. Blind Certainty arises when conviction becomes resistant to relevant evidence, questions, or correction. Strong confidence is not Blind Certainty merely because it is strong.

Confidence versus Infallibility. Confidence may be exceptionally high when justification warrants it. Recognizing the possibility of error does not require permanent hesitation, compulsory doubt, or treating well-established knowledge as equivalent to speculation.

Relationship to Openness:

Certainty belongs primarily to Philosophical Openness, which investigates how Self develops, justifies, and revises understanding.

It informs Awareness, Self, The Self-Confession of Not-Knowing, Epistemic Humility, Self-Honesty, The Intersubjective, Situated Certainty, Blind Certainty, Preferred Stories, and The Return to Openness.

The foundational confession—

For I Know I Do Not Know.

—recognizes the incompleteness of Self's understanding without denying that Self may genuinely know particular things or hold exceptionally well-justified confidence.

Epistemic Humility does not require confidence to remain weak. It asks that confidence remain proportionate to justification and responsive to what further inquiry reveals.

The Intersubjective makes additional perspectives, evidence, criticism, and methods of verification available. Other Selves may identify errors or provide justification that Self could not develop alone. Shared inquiry can improve confidence without making consensus itself an infallible authority.

Situated Confidence may therefore become more discerning through continued inquiry, even though Self never ceases to encounter its understanding from within Awareness.

Example:

Self experiences a powerful feeling that another person has deliberately acted against it.

The experience of suspicion is real. Its intensity may create Felt Certainty about the other person's intentions.

Yet the available evidence may be incomplete. Alternative explanations remain possible. Self's Justified Confidence in the accusation may therefore be considerably weaker than its felt conviction.

Through further inquiry, Self discovers information that challenges its initial interpretation.

Its Situated Confidence changes as its understanding of the evidence develops. The earlier feeling remains a genuine experience, but its proposed explanation is revised.

The reverse is also possible.

Self may have strong evidence supporting a conclusion while continuing to feel anxious or uncertain. The absence of Felt Certainty does not negate the justification.

In either case, Self may need to decide and act before every question is resolved.

Boundary:

Certainty is not synonymous with truth, and uncertainty is not synonymous with wisdom.

The experience of being certain cannot establish that a belief is true. Equally, the fact that confidence is encountered within Awareness does not make all beliefs equally justified or reduce objective inquiry to personal preference.

Situated Certainty does not assert that ultimate certainty is impossible, that every established understanding must be continually doubted, or that Self must refrain from action until uncertainty disappears. *(originator: controversial;under discussion)

It recognizes that Self can examine the grounds of its confidence, distinguish conviction from justification, and revise its understanding when warranted.

Self may become increasingly confident in what it understands while remaining open to what it has yet to understand.
