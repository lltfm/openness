Awareness

Fiat Anchored Ambiguity:

Awareness — At least this: the embodied first-person condition in which experience becomes present. But perhaps more?

Foundational Definition:

Awareness is a Fiat Anchored Ambiguity naming the embodied first-person condition in which experience becomes present. Sensation, perception, feeling, thought, movement, recognition and dreams may be encountered without first requiring an explanation of how experiencing occurs or what ultimately makes it possible.

The presence of experience provides a recognizable beginning. Yet recognizing experience does not establish a separate experiencer, an inner observer, or an independently existing substance called Awareness. Nor does it explain why experiencing seems situated within this familiar embodied life rather than another, or why dreams outside the familiar embodied life may be experienced.

Awareness may be recognized in waking experience without establishing its uninterrupted continuity. Sleep, dreams, uncertain memory, and altered experience leave open questions about when and how experience becomes present. The body may appear to continue through periods that cannot be described from within experience; this does not determine whether Awareness continues through those periods.

This minimal anchor makes further inquiry and exploration of possible explanations without first requiring a theory of consciousness, an account of the relationship between Awareness and the body, or an explanation of why experience is encountered from this first-person standpoint.

Essential Distinctions:

Presence of experience versus explanation of experiencing. Experience becomes recognizable in its occurrence. Naming this beginning Awareness does not establish how experiencing arises, what makes it possible, or whether it can be completely described through physical processes. Yet neither does it exclude the possibility that the name captures something about the ultimate nature of reality. Whether Awareness is merely a useful conceptual beginning or names something ultimately real remains unknown.

Awareness versus The Subjective Self. Awareness names the embodied first-person condition in which experience becomes present. The Subjective Self names the familiar embodied life encountered within it as one's own. Distinguishing them does not establish two independent things, an inner observer, or literal ownership of experience.

Experiencing versus an independent experiencer. The presence of sensations, thoughts, or feelings does not by itself demonstrate a separate entity that observes or possesses them. Awareness names a condition of first-person encounter, not a proven witness standing apart from experience.

Present experiencing versus uninterrupted continuity. Experience may be encountered now without establishing that Awareness remained present during unremembered sleep or other intervals. Renewed experience, memory, and bodily continuity do not settle what occurred during periods inaccessible to recollection.

Embodied standpoint versus fixed location. Experience seems situated in relation to this body, yet that seeming does not identify a precise physical location of Awareness, demonstrate its confinement, or establish the ultimate boundaries of embodiment.

First-person recognition versus self-description. Awareness need not be recognized through an explicit statement such as “I am aware.” Experience may become present without a remembered biography, a developed concept of personal identity, or a deliberate act of self-reflection.

Awareness versus consciousness as an explanatory concept. Awareness is the Generative Openness starting point in encountered experience. The term does not settle the scientific, philosophical, or metaphysical meaning of consciousness. Such explanations may be pursued without being built into the minimal anchor.

Encountering another body versus encountering another's Awareness. Another person's gestures, expressions, and bodily life may be observed. Such encounters may support further understanding or inference, but they do not transfer that person's first-person experiencing into one's own. Whether and how another's Awareness can be understood remains a further question.

Relationship to Openness

In Generative Openness, The Mystery, Awareness, and The Subjective Self provide three distinguishable beginnings: what remains unknown, the presence of experiencing, and this familiar embodied life. Their recognition does not establish three separate entities or explain their ultimate relationships.

Within Awareness, the familiar embodied life named My Self may be encountered through sensation, movement, feeling, and renewed recognition. A limit to understanding may also become recognizable, allowing The Self-Confession of Not-Knowing to be expressed: For I Know I Do Not Know.

Awareness makes experience available as a beginning for Poiesis, Fiat Form, Logonesis, and Analogical Projection. Such creation may draw upon what is encountered without resolving how Awareness and embodiment are related.

The Artist need not explicitly name Awareness to work from experience. Its minimal recognition may remain intuitive, arising through attention to materials, sensations, memories, and unfolding possibilities.

Naming and Usage

Awareness — primary Generative Openness name.

Use Awareness without quotation marks when referring to the first-person condition in which experience becomes present:

“Within Awareness, I encounter My Self as this familiar embodied life.”

Capitalization distinguishes the Generative Openness concept from ordinary uses of awareness. It does not establish a separate entity.

“Awareness” — mention of the expression.

Use quotation marks when discussing the term itself:

The expression “Awareness” names a recognizable first-person beginning without providing a theory of consciousness.

awareness, experiencing, and ordinary perception — natural language.

In Transformative Art, creative inquiry, and Saltem Hoc, ordinarily prefer the natural language of experience where a technical term would interrupt the work.

A painter's diary might say:

“I felt the cool handle of the brush against my fingers. The blue looked different in the morning light.”

The Artist need not announce an encounter with Awareness for experiencing to be recognizable.

Awareness and My Self — distinct usage.

Use Awareness for the condition in which experience becomes present. Use My Self or The Subjective Self for the familiar embodied life encountered within it.

Where a passage might imply that Awareness owns the body, that the body owns Awareness, or that one is a separate witness observing the other, choose language that preserves the distinction without asserting the relationship.

Examples

Example — The Brush in Hand (positive bright-line)

A painter feels the weight of a brush, sees morning light cross a canvas, and hears rain against the studio window.

These sensations and perceptions become present in experience.

This provides a recognizable beginning for Awareness without explaining the physical or ultimate nature of experiencing.

Example — Waking Without Recollection (continuity boundary)

A painter wakes after a night remembered only in fragments.

The familiar body and studio are encountered again.

Present experiencing is recognizable. Whether Awareness continued throughout the unremembered intervals remains unanswered.

Example — Becoming Absorbed in Making (nonverbal recognition)

A sculptor works for an extended period without deliberately reflecting on being aware.

Pressure, texture, movement, and emerging form remain available in experience.

Explicit self-description is not required for Awareness to serve as a generative beginning.

Example — Watching Another Painter (boundary)

A painter watches a friend choose colors and move a brush across a canvas.

The friend's movements are visible, and the painter may imagine what the friend experiences.

Such observation and imagination do not establish direct access to the friend's first-person Awareness.

Boundary:

Awareness is minimally anchored in the embodied first-person condition in which experience becomes present. This recognition does not establish that Awareness is a soul, separate inner witness, independent controller, permanently continuous experiencer, universal mind, or something fully captured by a scientific measurement of consciousness. Such interpretations may be explored without becoming requirements of the anchor.

Its recognition does not establish where Awareness resides, whether it remains present during dreamless sleep, why experience seems associated with this body, or what happens to experiencing when the body dies.

The anchor permits questions such as: What does it mean for experience to become present? Why does Awareness seem situated here? Can experiencing occur without memory or self-description? What is the relationship between Awareness and the familiar body? Does Awareness have boundaries?

It claims no final answers.

Awareness names the presence of experiencing without pretending that its familiarity explains its nature.
