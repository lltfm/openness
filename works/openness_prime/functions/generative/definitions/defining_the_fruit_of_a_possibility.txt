The Fruit of a Possibility

Foundational Definition:

The Fruit of a Possibility is what arises, or may arise, through considering, imagining, inhabiting, expressing, communicating, or realizing a possibility.

Fruit may include changes in understanding, feeling, perspective, behavior, relationships, circumstances, and the possibilities available for further inquiry or action.

It may be beneficial, harmful, mixed, insignificant, or uncertain; intended or unintended; immediate or delayed.

Its significance is that a possibility may have consequences simply through being considered or inhabited, even when its literal truth or realization remains unestablished.

Essential Distinctions

- Fruit is not Truth. A fictional, symbolic, or mistaken interpretation may generate meaningful consequences without becoming factually true.
- Fruit is not necessarily beneficial. A possibility may produce insight, confusion, courage, dependence, compassion, distress, or several of these together.
- Imagining is not realizing. Considering or inhabiting a possibility may bear fruit independently of whether it becomes an actual state of affairs.
- Possible fruit is not actual fruit. Anticipated consequences remain provisional until appropriately supported by experience or evidence.
- Fruit is not always attributable to one cause. Changes may arise through multiple interacting influences, and the contribution of a particular possibility may remain uncertain.
- Fruit need not be immediately recognizable. Some consequences emerge gradually or become apparent only through subsequent inquiry.

Function in the Generative Engine:

The Fruit of a Possibility directs attention beyond a possibility's conceptual content toward what engagement with it may bring into being.

It gives Poiesis and Transformative Inquiry a further subject of investigation: not merely what has been created or understood, but what changes through encountering it.

It also provides the broader foundation for examining The Fruit of Further Possibility.

The Engine may therefore investigate the significance of speculative, symbolic, and imaginative creations without confusing their effects with evidence of their literal truth.

Example:

Suppose Self provisionally interprets Cain as the present Self and Abel as the future Self.

Through imaginatively inhabiting this interpretation, Self may recognize how present choices influence the circumstances of future life.

The contemplation might produce increased concern for future needs, a new perspective on responsibility, or a desire to reconsider familiar habits.

These are possible fruits of engaging with the interpretation.

The symbolic interpretation need not be established as the intended historical meaning of the Biblical story for such fruit to arise.

And the fruit need not be wholly beneficial: the same contemplation might lead another reader toward excessive guilt or unrealistic demands for self-sacrifice.

Boundary Case:

Self experiences profound relief through a spiritual contemplation and concludes that the relief proves its metaphysical interpretation is true.

The relief may be genuine fruit. It does not, by itself, establish the interpretation's truth.

Likewise, discovering something beneficial after suffering does not establish that the suffering was good or justified.

The Fruit of a Possibility concerns what engagement brings forth—not an automatic judgment of truth, goodness, or justification.
