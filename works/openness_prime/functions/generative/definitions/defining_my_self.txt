My Self (The Subjective Self)

Fiat Anchored Ambiguity:

My Self — At least this: the embodied life repeatedly encountered within Awareness as “mine,” apparently continuing through sleep and waking. But perhaps more?

Foundational Definition:

The Subjective Self is a Fiat Anchored Ambiguity naming the first-person encounter with a familiar body and its unfolding life. Sensation, movement, feeling, and waking again in a recognizable body make an embodied beginning available without explaining why Awareness appears situated here, in this body rather than another.

The body appears to persist through periods that cannot be recalled or described from within experience. Recognizing its continuity does not establish uninterrupted Awareness, a separate owner of experience, or the ultimate relationship between Awareness and embodiment. Inhabiting, being anchored in, and calling the body mine point toward what seems to be encountered; they do not explain it.

This minimal anchor makes further inquiry and creation possible without first requiring a theory of identity, free choice, the nature of sleep, or what may happen to Awareness when the body dies.

Essential Distinctions:

Embodied referent versus Fiat Anchor. The body can be physically encountered and investigated; The Subjective Self serves as the Fiat Anchor naming this embodied life as mine, without needing to precede the encounter. Giving it a name neither creates another physical object nor demonstrates an immaterial owner. Yet neither does it exclude the possibility that the name captures something ultimately real about selfhood, embodiment, or their relationship. Whether the minimal encounter already reveals something of their ultimate nature remains unknown.

The Subjective Self versus Awareness. Awareness names the embodied first-person condition in which experience becomes present. The Subjective Self names the embodied life encountered within it as one's own. Distinguishing them does not establish two independent things, an inner observer, or literal ownership of experience.

Present encounter versus bodily continuity. The body is encountered now; its persistence through unremembered sleep is recognized through renewed experience, memory, and other evidence. A familiar body upon waking neither proves continuous experiencing nor requires unchanged physical substance.

Felt inhabitation versus explanation. Feeling located in, anchored to, or inseparable from this body is an experiential starting point, not an account of where Awareness is, why it seems associated with this body alone, or whether that association has ultimate boundaries.

Embodied referent versus Fiat Anchor. The body can be physically encountered and investigated; The Subjective Self is the Fiat Anchor that names this embodied life as mine, without needing to precede the encounter. Giving it a name neither creates another physical object nor demonstrates an immaterial owner.

First-person encounter versus self-description. Recognition does not require saying “I,” recalling a biography, or consciously adopting an identity. The concept does not settle its applications to early life, uncertain memory, altered embodiment, or unfamiliar experience merely through language.

The Subjective Self versus The Imaginal Self. The Subjective Self is the Generative Openness starting point in an embodied encounter. The Imaginal Self, usually shortened in Philosophical Openness to Self, is the immaterial conceptual form of selfhood that may first be applied here, then imaginatively developed or analogically projected inward and outward. Its further applications do not establish additional experiencing beings.

Relationship to Openness:


n Generative Openness, The Mystery, Awareness, and The Subjective Self provide three distinguishable beginnings: what remains unknown, the presence of experiencing, and this familiar embodied life. Their recognition does not establish whether these distinctions correspond to separate realities, aspects of one reality, or relationships not yet understood. Neither does it settle whether their names already capture something about ultimate reality correctly.

Within this life, a limit to understanding may become recognizable and The Self-Confession of Not-Knowing may be expressed: For I Know I Do Not Know. From such a beginning, Poiesis, Fiat Form, Logonesis, and Analogical Projection may make further creations possible. "The Artist" need not formally analyze or name these operations to create through them.


Naming and Usage:

My Self — primary Generative Openness name and first-person invitation.

Use My Self without quotation marks where the grammar naturally supports an immediate first-person reference:

“Within Awareness, I encounter My Self as this familiar embodied life.”

The unusual spacing may invite writer and reader alike to turn toward their own experiencing. It should be used deliberately and sparingly—often once in a heading or opening paragraph is enough.


“My Self” — mention of the expression.

Use quotation marks when discussing the name itself or when an unquoted first-person phrase would be grammatically awkward:

The expression “My Self” invites first-person recognition.

Here the quotation indicates a term being discussed, not an asserted separate entity.


The Subjective Self — formal and comparative alternative.

Use the unquoted The Subjective Self when a definition, analysis, or third-person explanation needs a stable name without implying that the author, reader, and subject share one first-person standpoint:

“The Subjective Self remains incompletely understood despite the familiarity of the body.”


Natural language for artistic and inward inquiry.

In Transformative Art, creative inquiry, and Saltem Hoc, ordinarily avoid the technical expression My Self. Prefer myself, or my self and my own self for emphasis; one's own self suits general narration.

A painter's diary might say:

“I recognize myself in these tired hands.”

The Artist need not pause to introduce a technical label. The encounter may be used intuitively without philosophical reification.


Self — different Philosophical Openness meaning.

Capitalized Self, when used as Philosophical Openness terminology, abbreviates The Imaginal Self, not The Subjective Self.

Where a passage could confuse the two, use their full names. Writers retain discretion to choose the expression that best preserves both readability and the intended perspective.


Examples:

Example — Waking in Familiar Hands (positive bright-line)

> A painter awakens and notices familiar hands, an aching shoulder, breath, and yesterday's unfinished canvas.
> Much of the night is not remembered. Still, the body encountered upon waking is recognized as my own.

This is the starting encounter named The Subjective Self. It does not reveal where Awareness was during dreamless sleep.


Example — The Artist's Diary (natural first-person expression)

“I woke with the same stiffness in my hand. Myself, apparently, still here. I do not know why I wake to this body rather than another. The brush is ready; I can begin.”

The experience is recognizable without the Artist naming the concept, deriving a theory of selfhood, or addressing a reader.


Example — A Different Dream Body (ambiguous application)

In a dream, the painter seems to move through an unfamiliar body. Upon waking, a familiar body is encountered again.

The contrast invites questions about what mine means in experience. It does not, on its own, determine the nature of dreams, embodiment, or Awareness.


Example — Another Person (boundary)

The painter sees another friend's hand move across a canvas as they paint.

That person's body is encountered, but not as the painter's own embodied first-person life.

Imagining the other's perspective may generate a further concept of Self; seeing their body alone does not transfer their Awareness.


Boundary:

The Subjective Self is not defined as a soul, permanent personality, independent controller, continuously waking witness, or merely a grammatical I. Bodily change, interrupted memory, sleep, and uncertainty about what mine means do not automatically erase the embodied starting point.

The anchor permits questions such as: Why this body? What does inhabiting mean? Where is Awareness during sleep? Can another's Awareness ever be encountered as one's own? What happens when this life ends?

It claims no final answers.

My Self names a familiar beginning without pretending that familiarity is explanation.
