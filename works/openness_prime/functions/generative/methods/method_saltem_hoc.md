# Saltem Hoc Guidelines

## Purpose

Saltem hoc means "at least this." A contemplation begins from something its writer can honestly acknowledge and opens a sequence of possibilities for a reader to consider. Its purpose is to invite imagination, inquiry, and reflection without demanding agreement, belief, or a final conclusion.

Saltem hoc is a literary form, not a strict logical proof. It welcomes poetry, metaphor, uncertainty, and speculation. Above all, it should be readily understandable and enjoyable for a general reader. A writer may have a preferred story, but should remain honest about what is encountered, what is known, and what is imagined.

## Question, title, and filename

Every saltem hoc contemplation MUST begin, after its document metadata block, with a single plain-language question. That question is both the reader-facing opening title and the document's Title metadata value (in title case).

Avoid a title that implicitly asks two or more independent questions. Split such a draft into separate contemplations, each with one guiding question. Other questions may appear within the contemplation in service of the single guiding question.

By default, derive the .txt filename from the question using lowercase snake_case, removing punctuation: "Where Does Desire Come From?" becomes "where_does_desire_come_from.txt".

If the question is unusually long or awkward as a referent, optionally assign a shorter, non-question Short-Title. This MUST begin with "Saltem Hoc:" and may be used as the work's referent in other texts. The filename then begins with "saltem_hoc_" and uses lowercase snake_case words from the Short-Title following the prefix.

The Title field and reader-facing first line must remain the full question even when a Short-Title is used.

## Default template

[One plain-language question, also the Title]

[Optional attribution, with no "Source" label; e.g. Philosophical Openness AI v1:]

[Introduction: greet the questioner (often "Self") and set the scene, usually through an evocative natural metaphor. Generally one paragraph under 400 characters.]

At least this: [Directly encountered experience, common emotion, available evidence, shared premise, or honestly acknowledged uncertainty. Generally one paragraph under 500 characters.]

But also perhaps this: [A first, often concrete speculation. Usually one paragraph under 500 characters.]

Maybe this: [A second possibility, often more abstract or contrasting. Usually one paragraph under 500 characters.]

Or perhaps even this: [A third possibility, often the broadest, most unexpected or more abstract. It may resolve a narrative arc but need not resolve the question. Usually one paragraph under 500 characters.]

[Optional epilogue/outro: any number of paragraphs, generally under 500 characters each. Return to Self, the opening image, or the writer's preferred story.]

## Flexibility of form

The format is customizable. Writers may add, remove, repeat, or reorder speculative movements, including expressions such as "Or perhaps this." Two competing possibilities may receive parallel development instead of a forced concrete-to-abstract sequence. Character counts are editorial targets, never strict limits. The aim is readable, compelling contemplation, not mechanical conformity.

## Honesty and imagination

The "At least this" movement must not smuggle a disputed interpretation into an apparently shared fact.

## Speculative movements must remain recognizable as speculative.

Give unresolved alternatives genuine imaginative hospitality; avoid quietly tipping the scales through framing, tone, selective skepticism, or unequal development.

Spiritual Openness may disclose existential preferences; Philosophical Openness minimizes compulsory preferences. Either may contemplate spiritual possibilities without requiring belief.

The reader may accept, reject, revise, or indefinitely defer judgment.

## Language, anchored terms, and capitalization

Prefer common, evocative language over specialized Openness terminology. A contemplative piece should be understandable without requiring a glossary.

Anchored-term exception: a specialized Openness term may appear directly when its minimal "at least this" definition aligns closely with an ordinary English meaning understandable in context. Examples include Self, Awareness, The Mystery, and Love. Their complete formal definitions need not be repeated; readers may seek clarification independently.

When a word is being used as an Openness concept rather than only in its ordinary English sense, preserve its distinctive capitalization. When the ordinary English meaning is meant, use ordinary capitalization. Do not let capitalization quietly replace explanation where the meaning would otherwise be opaque.

Translate less accessible specialized vocabulary into everyday imagery where possible:

- The Ship of Self -> "the ship of concepts and beliefs we sail over uncertain seas"
- Faith -> "faith in the journey of life"
- Westward -> "walking westward into the sunset of our lives"

If a contemplation genuinely requires prior familiarity with Openness concepts, add a short audience note in the introduction. Do not add such a warning merely because familiar anchored terms are capitalized.

### Refrains, signatures, and emphatic language

Avoid habitual slogans, signatures, ritual signoffs, foreign-language mottos, or recurring non-plain-language formulas that add little to the particular contemplation. Do not end every contemplation with a stock phrase.

Expressions such as "For I Know I Do Not Know", "Memento Amaris", and "On towards death, Self" may be effective as occasional, purposeful emphases. Use them sparingly so their emotional and poetic force is not diluted by repetition.

A phrase may appear when it contributes meaning (for example, "For I Know I Do Not Know" when explaining The Mystery), rather than functioning as decorative boilerplate. An unadorned question or image may make a stronger ending than a familiar signature.

This is a preference for meaningful variation, not a ban on recurring symbols, intentional literary refrains, distinctive voice, or non-English expression.

## Attribution and metadata

All contemplations must follow Openness Document Guidelines for their single opening metadata block (Title, Authors, Editors, Created, Updated, optional Short-Title, and any additional document properties). Do not place Status, Tradition, Type, or other metadata-like key-value lines beneath the title. The metadata is separate from the optional reader-facing attribution after the title.

If a reader-facing attribution is used, do NOT write "Source:" or "Source" before it. Use these distinct editorial bylines:

Wholly AI-generated Philosophical Openness contemplation: "Philosophical Openness AI v1:"

Wholly AI-generated Spiritual Openness contemplation: "Spiritual Openness AI v1:"

AI-generated and subsequently edited by a human: append x to the version, e.g. "Philosophical Openness AI v1x:" or "Spiritual Openness AI v1x:".

Version N is currently 1 for both branches. The editorial version may be incremented later.

For a human-authored or substantially human/AI coauthored contemplation, the attribution may be omitted or the human author's name used.

These reader-facing labels designate work created using the Openness framework. They are DISTINCT from the existing role-play labels "Openness GPT vN". They do not replace the mandatory record of actual human and AI Authors/Editors in metadata; AI contributors there must identify product, model, and version (for example ChatGPT-GPT-6).

## Guiding principle

Begin with what can be honestly acknowledged. Imagine what more might be possible. Give the reader room to listen, contemplate, and remain free.

The writer offers a journey, not a conclusion that must be reached.
