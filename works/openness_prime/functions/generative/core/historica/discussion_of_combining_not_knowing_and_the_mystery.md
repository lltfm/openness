## Recommendation: Keep Not-Knowing and The Mystery as separate concepts

I recommend retaining both definitions, while making their relationship more explicit and reducing unnecessary repetition.

They arise from the same epistemic relationship but direct attention toward different things:

| Concept                           | What it makes recognizable                                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| My Self-Confession of Not-Knowing | A first-person acknowledgment: For I Know I Do Not Know.                                                                              |
| Not-Knowing                       | The generalized relationship between a standpoint of understanding and a recognizable limit to that understanding.                    |
| The Mystery                       | What remains unknown or incompletely understood relative to that standpoint, including possibilities not yet formulated as questions. |

Consider an unfamiliar sensation.

Not-Knowing makes it possible to acknowledge that its cause is not understood. The Mystery directs inquiry toward the cause or explanation that remains unresolved.

The concepts can therefore be expressed together without being synonymous:

> Not-Knowing makes a limit of understanding recognizable; The Mystery keeps open what that limit leaves unresolved.

### Why I would not collapse them

Combining them into one definition would be economical, but it would obscure three distinctions worth preserving.

First, a limit of understanding is not itself the unknown matter to which that limit relates. Second, The Mystery can include possible unknown unknowns—questions or distinctions not yet recognized—without pretending that their contents are known. Third, their spiritual and philosophical unfoldings differ: Not-Knowing particularly concerns epistemic posture, whereas The Mystery also opens inquiry into what may exist, emerge, or become encounterable beyond present understanding.

A combined definition could still preserve these distinctions through subsections. But once those sections become conceptually independent, the supposed economy largely disappears.

My preferred solution is separate canonical definitions with a shared, explicitly articulated derivation and a concise cross-reference in each. In the README, where brevity matters, they can continue to be introduced together.

One further safeguard is essential: The Mystery does not necessarily identify a real hidden thing. Sometimes the unresolved matter is a mistaken question, an unsupported premise, or an explanation that has yet to be formulated. A conceptual name for what remains unresolved does not establish a new entity in the world.
