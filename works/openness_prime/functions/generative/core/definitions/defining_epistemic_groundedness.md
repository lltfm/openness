# Epistemic Groundedness

## Prelude

I encounter a thought before I can fully explain it. I learn about an animal I have never seen. I recognize a discipline through names, practices, and institutions inherited from others.

Each understanding has a beginning, but the beginnings are not the same. What have I experienced? What have I been told? What have I inferred or imagined? Whose meanings have I inherited—and what might I discover by returning to what can be encountered again?

## Foundational Definition

**Epistemic Groundedness — At least this: the examinable relationships through which a concept or claim becomes recognizable in experience, evidence, Artifice, inference, imagination, or shared practices, and through which its meaning, justification, and possible referent may be reconsidered. But also perhaps more?**

## The Definition Unfolded

**Epistemic Groundedness** makes recognizable how a concept or claim is related to what is encountered, represented, inherited, stipulated, imagined, or inferred. It helps reveal the usually implicit circumstances through which something comes to be understood, the reasons for confidence in it, and the possible reality it purports to describe.

Groundedness is not automatically **good justification**. A claim may originate in a mistaken observation, unreliable testimony, a misleading classification, or an explicitly fictional premise. Examining its grounding may reveal strong support, weak support, a defective inference, or no evidential support for its literal truth. Even a concept with a clear **Fiat Anchor** may refer to something nonexistent or remain uncertain in its application.

**River-Truth** names the reality of what is directly encountered within **Awareness**. Direct experience itself is not **Artifice**. The drawing, word, memory-description, model, or argument through which I represent or interpret that encounter is **Artifice**: necessarily selective, sometimes illuminating, sometimes mistaken, and sometimes deliberately misleading. **Artifice** cannot export the entirety of the encounter. Yet reliable **Artifice** can make realities available to people who have never encountered them directly. Its mediation does not make those realities imaginary.

**Epistemic Groundedness** distinguishes **the experience of encountering**, **the conceptual form by which an encounter is understood**, and **the referent that the form may represent**. My experience of seeing an animal is directly encountered; identifying it as a platypus involves a learned classification and a claim about an animal. A concept's immateriality, or the imaginal character of a projection, neither establishes nor denies the referent's independent existence or its ultimate nature.

The intersubjective dimension is inherently **situated and potentially plural**. Different communities may develop overlapping, conflicting, or unrelated meanings for the same expression. Agreement may be partial, contested, or maintained through recognized practices rather than unanimity. A person may participate in several communities and must sometimes distinguish whose meaning they are using. No one community thereby gains authority over every other community or over all the realities its words attempt to describe.

**Anchor Stewardship** identifies who maintains a particular version of a definition, while **Intersubjective Context** identifies the shared settings in which a meaning is recognized or operative. **Scope of Authority** clarifies what a person or institution can legitimately determine. These are distinguishable from epistemic evidence. An institution may be empowered to set a curriculum, award a credential, or revise its classifications; this does not give it the power to make an inaccurate historical or empirical claim true.

**Openness** practices **Open Stewardship**. **Openness Prime** offers one traceable formulation, not ultimate authority over its readers, independent forks, or future systems of thought. A **Foundational Definition** governs what qualifies as an interpretation **within the identified version of the concept** until that anchor is expressly revised. Individuals may adopt, reject, copy, or fork it, but a private reinterpretation does not silently change the original or another community's agreed meaning.

**Epistemic Groundedness** supports **Epistemic Remembering**: making previously unnoticed experiential, historical, interpretive, and intersubjective relationships available for inquiry. Such remembering may involve reconstructing a plausible path toward a concept, researching its actual history, or reencountering a relevant reality. These are different activities. A plausible experiential reconstruction is not proof of what historically happened; a direct reencounter does not automatically settle every explanation; and a changed social convention is not necessarily a corrected empirical discovery.

## Experiential Derivation

**Experiential Derivation** is not required for **Higher Order Concepts** or meta-concepts, such as **Epistemic Groundedness**, whose purpose is to organize and examine first-order **Openness** concepts such as **The Mind**. For such concepts, an experiential derivation may nevertheless be explored **experimentally and provisionally** to test whether a recognizable path from experience helps clarify their meaning. The following first-person passages offer one such exploration, not a required derivation, a universal learning sequence, or proof of any concept's historical origin.

**1. Direct encounter.** I recognize something becoming present within **My Awareness**: a thought, a sensation, a sound, an animal I appear to see, or a person speaking. This encounter is real as experience even when its source, interpretation, or wider implications remain uncertain.

**2. Encounter with Artifice.** I notice that many of my names and explanations arrived through language, instruction, images, books, institutions, and testimony. A word may shape how I notice an experience, while the experience itself is not identical to that word or explanation.

**3. Examining the grounding.** I ask what I have encountered directly, what I have inferred, what I have inherited, and what I have merely imagined or stipulated. I distinguish the reasons that make a meaning recognizable from the reasons that justify believing a claim about its referent.

**4. Recognizing a general relationship.** I see that different concepts may have different and overlapping grounds. I call this examinable relationship **Epistemic Groundedness**. It helps me investigate my own understanding and compare it with the understandings sustained by others, without assuming that all share one epistemic history.

These movements articulate a possible **Experiential Derivation** of the present concept. They are not a requirement to invent an additional concept called *My Epistemic Groundedness*. They may be revisited through new encounters and through the examination of inherited **Artifice**.

## Grounding Modes

These are **descriptive modes of Epistemic Groundedness**, not separately reified concepts or mutually exclusive categories. Multiple modes may apply to the same concept, and the relevant modes may vary with the person, community, or encounter being examined.

| Grounding mode | Recognizable relationship | Essential qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **First-Person Groundedness** | A beginning in one's own directly encountered experience, such as a present thought or feeling. | Directly encountering an experience does not establish every explanation of it. |
| **Direct-Encounter Groundedness** | Encounter with an apparent worldly referent, such as observing a living animal. | The experience is direct; identification and explanation may involve inference and **Artifice**. |
| **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness** | Recognition through language, testimony, images, records, teaching, or other representations. | Mediation can provide well-justified knowledge without a direct encounter with the referent. |
| **Intersubjective Groundedness** | Recognition through shared meanings, relationships, practices, or institutions. | Specify the relevant communities and conventions; there need not be one consensus or authority. |
| **Inferential Groundedness** | Recognition or confidence developed through reasons and observed effects. | What is inferred need not itself have been directly encountered. |
| **Hypothetical or Imaginal Groundedness** | An explicitly imagined or stipulated premise for inquiry, play, or creation. | An imaginal representation of a real referent is not thereby imaginary or supported only by speculation. |

### First-person and direct encounter

Every direct encounter is experienced from a first-person standpoint. **First-Person Groundedness** emphasizes what can be examined in one's own mental or embodied life; **Direct-Encounter Groundedness** emphasizes the apparent worldly referent encountered. They can overlap without being identical descriptions of what is grounded.

### Imaginal representation and possible reality

An imaginal projection is a representation, not necessarily an imaginary referent. **Hypothetical or Imaginal Groundedness** describes the expressly imagined basis of a premise; it must not be applied merely because a well-supported attribution to something real involves an imaginal representation.

The modes describe **access and grounds**, not an exhaustive inventory of kinds of reality. *Subjectively real*, *intersubjectively constituted*, *independently existing*, and *fictional* can describe different, sometimes overlapping relations to reality. No grounding mode settles the referent's nature by itself.

## Essential Distinctions

**Grounding versus justification.** Discovering how a belief or concept arose does not establish that its claims are true. Testimony can be reliable or unreliable; direct experience can be genuine while its explanation is mistaken. Justification requires examining the reasons relevant to the claim.

**River-Truth versus Artifice.** Direct experience is not its portrayal. **Artifice** is an indispensable means of remembering, communicating, and comparing experiences, but necessarily leaves something out. Selectivity is not automatically error, and error is not automatically deception.

**Direct experience versus direct access to a referent's ultimate nature.** I may directly encounter a feeling, or visual experience of an animal, without thereby knowing its entire cause, constitution, or classification. **River-Truth** is a privileged place of experiential reencounter, not an infallible theory of everything encountered.

**First-Person Groundedness versus Direct-Encounter Groundedness.** Both arise within first-person experience. The former emphasizes experiences recognizable in one's own mental or embodied life; the latter emphasizes encountering an apparent referent in the world. Neither is independent of subsequent interpretation.

**Conceptual form versus referent.** A **Fiat Form** is a conceptual creation or adoption. A **Fiat Anchor** establishes its identifying meaning. The referent may be material, immaterial, socially constituted, hypothetical, fictional, or otherwise understood. The form's existence does not prove the referent's existence.

**Imaginal representation versus imaginary referent.** An **Analogical Projection** is a representation, even when its referent is strongly supported as real. A knowingly imaginary puppet's mind and a strongly justified attribution of another person's mind are both represented imaginally, but they have different referential and evidential standing.

**Artifice-mediated knowledge versus fabricated reality.** Learning about a platypus through a book does not make the animal a fiction. Conversely, a detailed invented history can be intelligible without being historically real.

**Intersubjective recognition versus universal consensus.** Shared meanings belong to identifiable, sometimes overlapping or conflicting communities. Membership need not be unanimous, and the existence of one recognized convention does not erase alternatives.

**Intersubjective constitution versus independent empirical truth.** Shared recognition can help create a degree, office, contract, or disciplinary boundary. It cannot, merely by agreement, establish that an animal has a particular biological trait or that an event occurred as described.

**Anchor Stewardship versus authority over reality.** The steward of a definition can maintain its recognized wording and revision process within a specified version. This does not give the steward ultimate authority over adoption by others or the referent's actual nature.

**Freedom to fork versus unilateral amendment.** Anyone may develop a distinct conceptual version, subject to applicable practical arrangements, and clearly identify its provenance. A person cannot legitimately revise another maintained version or a group's situated definition merely by privately declaring the change already adopted. They may propose, persuade, participate in authorized revision, or build an independent alternative.

**Situated authority versus individual assent.** A group's practices can be operative despite dissent and may affect people who did not personally choose them. An individual's **Interpretive Sovereignty** permits examination and independent adoption, but does not itself alter an established convention or remove its real-world effects.

**Experiential reconstruction versus historical origin.** An **Experiential Derivation** may show an inspectable path from experience to concept without establishing when, where, or by whom that concept was historically invented. Historical claims require their own evidence.

**Ordinary anchored speculation versus anchor revision.** A speculation qualifies under an existing **Anchored Ambiguity** only if it fits its **Foundational Definition**. An important possibility that fails to fit may motivate a separate, exceptional review of the anchor; it is not silently included by reinterpretation.

**Reencounter versus automatic correction.** Returning to experience, observing a referent, or examining social practice can expose a deficient representation. It may also support the existing one, leave the matter unresolved, or reveal that the referent's intersubjective constitution has changed.

## Relationship to Openness

**G — Generative Openness.** **Epistemic Groundedness** belongs provisionally to **G Core**, outside the established 24-concept **Generative Engine**. It complements **Experiential Derivation**, **Experiential Generalization**, **Fiat Form**, **Fiat Anchor**, **Anchored Ambiguity**, **Analogical Projection**, and **Analogical Discernment**. It helps make the normally implicit relationship between **River-Truth** and **Artifice** available for examination, rather than generating a separate compounded **Fiat Anchor** type for each grounding mode.

**P — Philosophical Openness.** **Epistemic Groundedness** supports the inquiry of **The Philomath** into knowing, testimony, evidence, **The Intersubjective**, **Artifice**, **Preferred Stories**, and **Interpretive Sovereignty**. It distinguishes what is experienced, what is inherited, what is justified, and whose meaning is being used. **Epistemic Hospitality** welcomes qualifying possibilities without granting them equal confidence; **Self-Honesty** examines whether claims have been represented faithfully.

**S — Spiritual Openness.** **Epistemic Groundedness** allows personally encountered experiences, inherited spiritual **Artifice**, shared practices, and speculative explanations to be considered without conflating them. The reality of a felt encounter does not by itself establish its spiritual cause; nor does the presence of cultural interpretation prove that every spiritual possibility is false.

**T — Transcendent Openness.** Different standpoints may bring differently grounded meanings to an encounter with **That Which Is Larger**. The **Shadow of That Which Is Larger** and its **Rendering** may disclose genuine effects while leaving their sources uncertain. **Epistemic Groundedness** helps participants distinguish encounter, interpretation, relational emergence, and what remains beyond current understanding.

Across the functions, **Recursive Becoming** permits the examination and explicit revision of definitions, and **Open Stewardship** permits independent adoption, copying, comparison, and further development without making **Openness Prime** the universal judge of what may follow it.

## Naming and Usage

**Epistemic Groundedness** — the canonical capitalized name for the general relationship examined here. Do not use it to mean that a concept has already been proven true.

**Grounding mode** — a descriptive characteristic such as **First-Person Groundedness** or **Intersubjective Groundedness**. The modes are not automatically independent Core Concepts or separate definitions.

**Epistemic Grounding Profile** — an optional descriptive format identifying the relevant grounds of a concept or claim. It may record **Primary Grounding**, **Additional Grounding**, **Possible Referent**, **Intersubjective Context**, **Anchor Stewardship**, **Scope of Authority**, and **Principal Reencounter**. Use only the fields that clarify the inquiry. A profile may be relative to a person, community, time, or particular version of a concept.

**Anchor Stewardship** — identifies who maintains a particular version's defining meaning and how changes may be made. It does not identify an ultimate authority over the real world.

**Intersubjective Context** — identifies the community, tradition, institution, or shared practice in which a meaning is recognized. More than one context may apply, including conflicting contexts.

**Scope of Authority** — identifies what a person or institution may legitimately establish or change within a particular context, distinct from the factual truth of accompanying claims.

**Open Stewardship** — describes **Openness**'s refusal to claim exclusive or ultimate authority over its concepts' adoption and future development. Provenance and fidelity to a stated version remain important.

**Epistemic Remembering** — a useful descriptive expression for recovering hidden or forgotten epistemic relationships; not yet a separately reified Method. Distinguish experiential reconstruction from documented historical recovery.

## Examples

The following examples show **Epistemic Grounding Profiles** as illustrative, situated metadata rather than authoritative classifications. Each table records only the dimensions helpful to its example.

### Example — The Mind (first-person beginning)

A recollection arrives unexpectedly. I experience remembering and the relief that follows, recognize a capacity for memory, and infer that further processes contributed to its arrival. From such encounters I develop the pattern called **My Mind**, then generalize it as **The Mind**.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **First-Person Groundedness** |
| **Additional Grounding** | **Inferential Groundedness** and **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness** |
| **Possible Referent** | Mental activities, capacities, and organization whose ultimate nature remains unsettled |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Inherited meanings of *mind* may differ across traditions |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Situated in whichever version is being used; **Openness Prime** offers one maintainable, adoptable, and forkable definition |
| **Scope of Authority** | The selected anchor governs that version's meaning, not the nature of minds or other versions |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Renewed attention to lived mental activity and relevant evidence about inferred processes |

### Example — Platypus (encounter, inheritance, and classification)

A person learns about platypuses from pictures and books without having seen a living one. Another observes living platypuses and develops a classification from recurring features. The first person's concept is grounded through **Artifice**; the second's also through direct encounter. Both may use evidence and inherited scientific classifications.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness** for one observer; **Direct-Encounter Groundedness** for the other |
| **Additional Grounding** | Testimony, inference, and shared scientific practice |
| **Possible Referent** | Animals whose existence does not depend on their name |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Multiple cultural or scientific communities may maintain overlapping or distinct classifications |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Situated among the communities maintaining their respective classifications |
| **Scope of Authority** | Authority over particular naming or classification conventions, not over the animals or empirical truth |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Observation, comparison, and further scientific investigation |

### Example — Different communities, one participant (plural intersubjective contexts)

Imagine two scientific communities that use *platypus* with different boundaries. One researcher belongs to both and understands that the same word may invoke different classifications in different conversations.

The researcher may use each convention in its context, compare their claims, argue for a change, or construct an independent classification. None of these acts alone rewrites the other's accepted meaning. Whether either classification best represents the animals remains an empirical question.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Intersubjective Groundedness**, relative to each community |
| **Additional Grounding** | **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness**, direct observation, and scientific inference as available |
| **Possible Referent** | Animal populations represented through differently bounded classifications |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Two overlapping but nonidentical scientific communities; one researcher participates in both |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Each community maintains its own situated classification |
| **Scope of Authority** | The researcher may adopt or advocate changes, but cannot unilaterally revise either community's convention |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Compare definitions, membership practices, empirical evidence, and the animals under investigation |

### Example — Computer Science (socially developed field)

I learn programming and encounter algorithms, courses, research papers, and people who call their shared field **Computer Science**. The discipline is not one object I can inspect in a glance; its recognizable identity depends partly on histories of shared inquiry, education, institutions, and practice.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Intersubjective Groundedness** and **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness** |
| **Additional Grounding** | Direct encounters with computational and mathematical work |
| **Possible Referent** | A historically developed discipline with socially maintained boundaries, alongside claims not settled merely by convention |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Departments, traditions, and communities may draw its boundaries differently |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Distributed and situated, with no presumed single universal authority |
| **Scope of Authority** | Communities may govern their own curricula and classifications without determining all mathematical or empirical truths |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Examine actual practices, histories, evidence, and recognized processes of change |

### Example — An Openness fork (open stewardship)

A reader adopts **Openness Prime** and later changes its definition of **The Mind**. They publish the revised definition as a clearly identified personal fork. Another reader keeps Prime's wording; a community develops a third version.

Each may steward a recognizable anchor within its own version. None is required to accept the others' revisions. None can legitimately claim, by mere assertion, that a private revision altered another maintained text or that a community adopted a definition when it did not.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness** and **Intersubjective Groundedness** where versions are shared |
| **Possible Referent** | Distinct, traceable versions of a conceptual definition |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Prime, an individual fork, and a community-maintained version |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Each version has its own steward or stewardship practice |
| **Scope of Authority** | Maintenance of one's own version; no unilateral authority over other versions or readers |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Compare the actual version texts, provenance, adoption records, and revision practices |

### Example — A puppet's mind (imaginal but imaginary)

A child invents a puppet character who remembers yesterday and worries about tomorrow. The child's projection of **The Mind** is imaginal and knowingly imaginary. Its meaning may draw on real experiences of memory and worry, **Artifice**, and play, without providing evidence that the puppet is an independent experiencer.

An imaginal representation of a real friend's mind differs: it too is a representation, but may be strongly supported by testimony, conduct, and shared human capacities. Representation alone does not determine referential reality.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Hypothetical or Imaginal Groundedness** for the fictional puppet |
| **Additional Grounding** | First-person experiences of emotion or memory, inherited stories, and play |
| **Possible Referent** | A knowingly imaginary character, distinct from a real person's potentially well-supported mind |
| **Intersubjective Context** | Shared imaginative play, if other participants recognize the character |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | The story's creators or participants maintain its fictional conventions |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Examine what is being pretended and distinguish representation from evidence of independent experiencing |

### Example — Quietly changed definitions (negative boundary)

A group alters an established definition in an archival document, omits the revision history, and presents the altered wording as though it had always been the recognized version. The new formulation may become widely repeated.

**Epistemic Groundedness** asks what changed, who changed it, under what authority, and what evidence supports the associated claims. Actual adoption may sometimes change a social convention, but it does not rewrite the historical record, make an unsupported factual assertion true, or entitle the group to speak for every other community.

| Metadata | Grounding profile |
|---|---|
| **Primary Grounding** | **Artifice-Mediated Groundedness**, with potentially misleading documentary provenance |
| **Additional Grounding** | **Intersubjective Groundedness** if the altered wording becomes operative in a community |
| **Possible Referent** | The claimed original definition, the altered document, and any convention that actually changed |
| **Intersubjective Context** | The group making the change, its audience, and other communities maintaining the earlier usage |
| **Anchor Stewardship** | Must be established from actual maintenance and revision practices, not asserted retroactively |
| **Scope of Authority** | Legitimate revision procedures, if any, do not confer authority to falsify historical provenance |
| **Principal Reencounter** | Inspect archived versions, revision histories, testimony, and documented adoption |

## Boundary

**Epistemic Groundedness** is not a universal proof procedure, a hierarchy declaring mediated knowledge inferior to direct experience, or an ontology that sorts everything into mutually exclusive kinds of reality.

Its grounding modes describe how meanings or claims become available and supported; they do not alone determine truth. **River-Truth** is the reality of direct encounter, not a guarantee that the immediate explanation of that encounter is correct. **Artifice** is necessarily selective, but may convey knowledge accurately. **Intersubjective recognition** is real in its effects and sometimes constitutive of social facts, but neither universally shared nor automatically authoritative over empirical reality.

No profile establishes universal ownership of a concept. A maintained version's anchor remains binding for interpretations *of that version*; people may choose other versions or create their own. No one can legitimately claim that their private alteration already changed an independently maintained version or a community's recognized convention without the relevant process or practice actually supporting that claim.

Experiential reconstruction must not be presented as verified history. Incompatible speculation must not be silently admitted as a valid interpretation of an unchanged **Foundational Definition**. When reencounter exposes a genuine weakness in an anchor, the possible revision is explicit and exceptional, not an unnoticed expansion of what the anchor already means.

## Summary

**Epistemic Groundedness makes the relationships between experience, Artifice, shared meaning, justification, and possible reality recognizable again. It helps us see whose concept we are using, how it came to be understood, what supports it, and where we might return when its representations deserve reconsideration.**

It invites questions such as: What have I directly encountered? Which meanings have I inherited? What do I infer or imagine? Whose intersubjective convention is operative here? Who may maintain or revise this particular definition? What would count as a meaningful reencounter with the reality it purports to describe?

**A concept may be faithfully maintained without being universally imposed, inherited without being unquestionable, and revised without pretending that the earlier version never existed.**
