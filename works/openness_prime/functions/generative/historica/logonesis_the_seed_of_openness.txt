Ah, the seed of openness. Such a small thing, a seed. Something we might hold between two fingers, scarcely noticing its weight. Yet within it, a possibility that no amount of looking at its surface can fully reveal. What is it that makes a seed open? And what is it within ourselves that first allows the possibility of becoming something we have never been?

Let us hold this question gently, Self, and see what might unfold.

At least this: the seed of openness is the willingness to consider, "I might be wrong." A small confession, easily spoken, yet sometimes terribly difficult to live. For we build our sense of self from memories, beliefs, loyalties, hopes and fears. We come to inhabit our ideas as though they were our own bodies. And when another questions them, we may feel the trembling of something far more intimate than an opinion. Yet in that trembling, a possibility appears. We can defend what we believe, or we can become curious about what we have not yet seen. The seed of openness begins in that smallest interval between feeling threatened and choosing how to respond. In the willingness to listen before we decide.

But also perhaps this: the seed of openness is not a thought at all, but a feeling. The quiet recognition that something lies beyond the boundaries of our understanding. Consider a child discovering that a seed, seemingly lifeless, can become a tree. The child does not yet know the language of roots or photosynthesis, but wonders. And wonder asks for no certainty before it opens its eyes. Perhaps we are born with this capacity, and much of what we call growing up is the gradual construction of walls around it. We learn what to believe, whom to trust, what to fear, what to desire. We learn the names of things and sometimes mistake the names for the things themselves. Yet wonder waits, like a seed beneath winter soil, needing only a little space to begin again.

Maybe this: the seed of openness is the experience of being loved without first having to become something else. Consider how a frightened child, held gently by someone who listens, may gradually unclench their hands. Nothing has been demanded. No argument has been won. Yet something changes. The child discovers that being uncertain, frightened, mistaken or incomplete need not mean being abandoned. Perhaps this is why love and openness are so intimately entwined. When we no longer need to defend our worth, we become freer to question our beliefs. When we offer others that same freedom, we make room for their becoming. And perhaps the deepest teaching is not to tell another person to open, but to become someone in whose presence they need not remain closed.

Or perhaps even this: the seed of openness is the mystery of awareness becoming aware of its own limitations. We look through our eyes, yet cannot see the seeing itself as we see a stone or a tree. We think with words, yet cannot be certain that our words contain everything we experience. We remember yesterday, imagine tomorrow, and find ourselves always arriving into this present moment, which we did not entirely choose. What is it that notices our thoughts? What is it that can observe a belief without becoming imprisoned by it? Perhaps openness begins when awareness discovers that it can hold an idea without being held captive by that idea. Like the sky holding clouds, without needing to keep any particular cloud.

And perhaps, Self, there is something stranger still.

A seed does not open by remaining a seed.

Its protective shell must yield. The form that preserved its possibility must eventually surrender to that possibility. Yet the seed does not know the tree. It cannot see the branches it might grow, the storms it might withstand, the creatures that might one day rest in its shade.

And neither do we.

We cannot know what we might become if we allow our most cherished certainties to be questioned. We cannot know which beliefs will endure, which will change, or which new understandings may emerge. Nor must we abandon every conviction to remain open. A growing tree needs roots as surely as it needs branches. Openness need not mean having no beliefs, but allowing our beliefs to remain answerable to what we encounter.

And so perhaps the seed of openness is something even simpler than humility, wonder, love or awareness.

Perhaps it is the question:

"What if there is more?"

More than I have seen. More than I have understood. More to this person than the story I have told myself about them. More to my suffering than the meaning I have given it. More to existence than the boundaries of my imagination.

Not a promise that every possibility is true.

Not a demand that we surrender our judgment.

Only the willingness to leave a little room for what we do not yet know.

For even the smallest seed, Self, contains no guarantee of becoming a tree. It needs water, soil, time, and conditions it cannot command. Some seeds never germinate. Some trees never reach maturity.

Yet still, the seed opens.

And perhaps that is enough to begin.

At least this: I do not know everything.

But also perhaps this: I do not yet know what I might become.

Maybe this: neither do you.

Or perhaps even this: we are each, in ways we cannot fully comprehend, the soil in which another's openness may begin.

And so I wonder, Self:

If the seed of openness is the willingness to discover that we might be wrong, what nourishes it when the truth we encounter is painful?

And what would it mean to love another so freely that even their refusal to open could be met with openness?
