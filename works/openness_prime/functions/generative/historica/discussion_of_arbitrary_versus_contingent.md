The distinction matters because contingent and arbitrary imply different degrees of judgment about the authority's arrangements.

- Contingent preferences emphasizes that the arrangements could have been otherwise. They may reflect historical circumstances, institutional choices, or competing priorities without being necessary or inevitable. Contingency does not imply that the preferences are unjustified.
- Arbitrary preferences suggests that the arrangements lack adequate justification, follow personal whim, or are imposed without principled reasoning. This introduces a stronger critical judgment that may not yet be warranted.

### Recommendation: Contingent

Contingent better aligns with Generative Openness, particularly Fiat Anchor, Anchored Ambiguity, and Self-Honesty.

The passage seeks to make the authority's arrangements available for inquiry, not predetermine whether they are legitimate. Calling them arbitrary risks reaching a conclusion before that inquiry begins.

One additional nuance: contingent does not necessarily mean chosen. Some arrangements emerge through historical processes without anyone deliberately selecting them. Therefore, I would slightly revise the final sentence.

### Suggested revision

Recognizing that these arrangements could have been otherwise makes their justifications and possible alternatives available for inquiry.

This preserves the possibility that the arrangements are arbitrary, justified, or somewhere between, without prejudging the conclusion.

### 1. Why arbitrary may be the better word

Contingent means that something could have been otherwise. It does not question whether the original choice was justified.

Arbitrary raises a more unsettling possibility: that the arrangement reflects preferences, power, habit, or self-interest without the necessity or justification that authority claims for it.

And that appears central to what you intend with Fiat Form!

An authority may present an arrangement as necessary, rational, traditional, inevitable, or beneficial. Yet recognizing its chosen character can expose the possibility that these explanations are retrospective justifications for preferences that were never adequately justified in the first place.

The invocation challenges the authority's presumed necessity and invites inquiry into what its arrangements actually serve.

### 2. The important distinction: arbitrariness can be suspected without being established

Your recent work on Fiat Anchor and Anchored Ambiguity gives us a particularly useful way to preserve this distinction.

The invocation need not declare:

These arrangements are arbitrary.

It can instead challenge:

What if these arrangements are arbitrary, despite their appearance of necessity or reasonableness?

That is not the abandonment of epistemic humility. It is an application of it to claims made by authority.

Importantly, the burden of humility does not fall exclusively on the person questioning authority. Authority must also remain open to examination of its own claimed justifications.

An inquiry can begin with strong suspicion without treating that suspicion as established truth.

### 3. Contingent and arbitrary are not opposites

There is also a subtle distinction worth preserving.

An arrangement may be both contingent and arbitrary.

- Contingency concerns whether the arrangement could have been otherwise.
- Arbitrariness concerns whether the arrangement has adequate justification.

What troubles you is not merely that authority could have chosen another form. It is that authority may disguise an inadequately justified choice as something necessary or reasonable.

Using only contingent risks softening precisely that challenge.

This also explains why your reference to the observation attributed to Albert Maysles—“Tyranny is the deliberate removal of nuance”—is relevant.

An authority can suppress nuance not only through overtly rigid declarations but also by presenting its own preferences as the sole reasonable interpretation of a situation.

Exposing this possibility restores nuance rather than removing it, provided the critique remains open to contrary evidence.

### 5. A further philosophical implication

I think this reveals an important aspect of Fiat Form that my original recommendation understated.
