# The Ethics of Perspectivity

## Prelude

*Someone tells me what everyone knows. I hear their words. I feel the quick ease of seeing from the vantage they offer.*

*Another person calls the account propaganda. A new vantage arrives, now carrying a verdict about the first.*

*What if I may examine both without granting either the right to decide for me? What if even this question is an invitation I remain free to decline?*

## Purpose and Status

**The Ethics of Perspectivity** gathers **Openness's chosen ethical commitments and available practices** concerning the offering, evoking, inhabiting, affirming, questioning, and declining of perspectives. It examines how [**Imaginality**](../definitions/defining_imaginality.md), [**Real and Reality**](../definitions/defining_real_and_reality.md), [**Intersubjectivity**](../definitions/defining_intersubjectivity.md), and [**Perspectivity**](../definitions/defining_perspectivity.md), including its analytical sub-concept **Perspectival Dimensionalization**, can participate in understanding, persuasion, social cooperation, and possible manipulation.

This is an **ethics document**, not a new [**Fiat Anchor**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_fiat_anchor.md), a revised Foundational Definition, a newly reified [**Generative Method**](../../generative/definitions/defining_generative_methods.md), or an authority empowered to certify which accounts count as propaganda. Its aspirations are offered for independent examination. Understanding the underlying definitions does not require adopting this ethic.

**The central commitment is to cultivate the reader's, listener's, viewer's, and participant's capacity to recognize and choose their relationship to offered perspectives.** Openness values the possibility of stepping back without demanding that everyone perform the same analysis, arrive at the same judgment, or accept Openness's account of the encounter. The capability can be developed, used, neglected, or questioned; its exercise does not guarantee correctness or freedom from consequences.

This ethic extends to people making and circulating perspectives: authors, artists, teachers, journalists, scientists, institutions, communities, and participants who report their own experience. Its interest is in **freedom with discernment**, not the replacement of one imposed interpretation by another.

## The Commitments of Openness

### Freedom to Offer a Perspective

Openness favors the freedom to express ideas, tell stories, testify, argue, imagine possibilities, and advocate strongly. A vivid or one-sided argument can be a legitimate offer of perspective; persuasive purpose alone does not make an act deceptive. The imaginative and emotional powers of art and speech may be valuable precisely because they invite a recipient into an unfamiliar vantage.

An account's accuracy, selectivity, intent, likely effect, and ethical character remain independently examinable. A writer's freedom to offer does not entail another person's obligation to listen, assent, or remain in an encounter. Openness does not seek to prohibit ideas by pronouncing them *propaganda*, *false*, *offensive*, or *dangerous*; criticism and independent verification remain available in the same field of expression.

This commitment relates to [**The Ethics of Closedness**](ethics_of_closedness.md) and **Nonimposition**. The present document applies their established aspirations without reproducing their full ethical arguments.

### Freedom to Encounter Without Assenting

A recipient may hear an argument, enjoy a fiction, follow an unfamiliar belief, inspect an allegation, or provisionally inhabit a viewpoint without endorsing it. **Understanding, evocation, inhabitation, affirmation, and action are distinguishable relations.** A representation may even arise spontaneously before a person notices what prompted it.

Openness values the freedom to continue examining a claim while withholding assent. This includes accounts supported by substantial evidence. The freedom to decline inward adoption does not entail that contrary views have equal evidential warrant, that an independently existing event disappears, or that choices following refusal are free of consequences.

An inquirer may use the same freedom to affirm a perspective confidently, provisionally, or with qualifications. **The ethic protects a space of choice, not an obligation to reject what other people believe.**

### Freedom to Step Back

A reader may notice that an essay has offered a seemingly omniscient standpoint, that a film has made a character's motive feel directly known, or that an institution has spoken as though representing a homogeneous public. The reader may then return attention to their own first-person encounter: the words heard, images seen, thoughts arising, emotions felt, and inferences made.

**Stepping back** does not require denying what was encountered, proving the speaker wrong, or refusing the perspective forever. A person may return to the account with clearer awareness of what it offers. They may also disengage, temporarily suspend assent, compare sources, or accept the account after examination.

Openness treats this possibility as a meaningful aspect of interpretive agency. It does not promise that everyone can always step back easily, that a spontaneous thought can be prevented at will, or that all circumstances afford an unrestricted choice. [**The Unchosen**](../definitions/defining_the_unchosen.md) helps distinguish what first arises without deliberate selection from what responses may later become possible.

### Interpretive Sovereignty and Nonimposition

**Interpretive sovereignty** here names Openness's chosen commitment to people's latitude to decide what interpretations they will provisionally inhabit, prefer, affirm, dispute, or set aside. Another person's expertise, a group's consensus, an institution's power, or the elegance of a representation does not by itself become ownership of someone's inward assent.

The freedom is reciprocal. A writer may advocate; a reader may disagree; a friend may challenge the reader's reasons; a reader may reconsider. Openness aspires to leave all of those possibilities available. A refusal to adopt a perspective does not by itself establish [**Closedness**](../core/defining_closedness.md), [**Blind Certainty**](../core/defining_blind_certainty.md), ignorance, or moral deficiency. Nor does the refusal automatically become evidence of wisdom.

Sovereignty over interpretation does not imply sovereignty over others' bodies, property, safety, or decisions. Ethical questions about resulting action remain distinct, and persons may set boundaries, withdraw, or seek protection. This ethic does not require anyone to become an unwilling audience.

## The Ethics of Disclosure

[**Perspectival Dimensionalization**](../definitions/defining_perspectivity.md#perspectival-dimensionalization) can show where a description moves from a first-person encounter to an [**imaginal perspective**](../definitions/defining_imaginality.md). Openness values the possibility that such movements become recognizable, especially when a hidden attribution could materially change how an account is understood.

The appropriate degree of disclosure varies. A novel may openly invite readers into a fictional omniscient narration. A museum placard may economically summarize strong historical evidence. A scientific paper may state a supported conclusion without repeatedly narrating every researcher's standpoint. A contested claim about a neighbor's private motive may benefit from explicit attribution. **No universal quota of disclaimers follows from this ethic.**

Openness **does** adopt a specific *editorial convention* for its formal definitions: first consequential perspective shifts are to be made recognizable, and the preferred high-precision *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., [**Reality**](../definitions/defining_real_and_reality.md)) is normally introduced before the compressed capitalized term. This is a rule for the accuracy of Openness's own technical writing, not a demand that all speech everywhere follow the same convention. Its operative home is the [**Openness Definition Guidelines**](../../../guidelines/guidelines_definitions.md); [**Real and Reality**](../definitions/defining_real_and_reality.md#naming-and-usage) owns the terminology itself.

The virtue of disclosure is contextual: **make an important inference inspectable without making the text unusable**. Longer phrases may be appropriate where a short form creates false certainty; a brief initial dimensionalization may permit concise ordinary language thereafter. This is [**Semantic Compression**](../../generative/definitions/defining_semantic_compression.md) joined with [**Brevity Without Loss of Essential Meaning**](../../generative/definitions/defining_brevity_without_loss_of_essential_meaning.md).

## Persuasion, Propaganda, and the Freedom to Name

### The Label Is Another Perspective

A speaker declares, “This is propaganda.” The statement itself offers a perspective on the material and may aim to change what the audience believes, fears, or considers legitimate. The label may be illuminating, mistaken, incomplete, carefully justified, self-serving, or deliberately manipulative. **Calling something propaganda does not by itself establish that it is propaganda.**

There is no single authority in this ethic empowered to decide which communications deserve the label. A reader may use the word, reject it, qualify it, or remain undecided. Other people may challenge the reader's characterization and present evidence. Openness **holds the tension** between freedom to criticize and freedom to resist the critic's frame, leaving the classification open to inquiry rather than converting it into a self-validating verdict.

That freedom does not require pretending every allegation is equally justified. Whether a clip omitted key context, whether a speaker misstated a source, whether a campaign knowingly fabricated a quotation, or whether a shared account is strongly corroborated are questions with discoverable grounds. The ethical judgment remains contestable even when some relevant facts are well established.

### Distinguishing Possible Mechanisms

Several different phenomena can produce similar effects:

**Selective representation.** An account includes some information and excludes other information. Selection is unavoidable in finite communication; particular omissions may be harmless, consequential, mistaken, or deceptive.

**Unnoticed perspective shift.** A reader moves into a narrated or attributed vantage without explicitly recognizing the move. This may be a routine feature of language and does not establish the author's intention.

**Implied consensus.** Words such as *we*, *everyone*, *scientists*, *the people*, or *the community* may accurately describe a specified group, summarize a qualified majority, or disguise significant heterogeneity. [**Intersubjectivity**](../definitions/defining_intersubjectivity.md#imaginal-group-attribution-and-heterogeneity) defines the underlying distinctions.

**Omniscient narration.** An author describes motives, fears, intentions, or events as though they were directly available from an unlocated viewpoint. This may be an overt fictional convention, a helpful summary of strong evidence, an unexamined habit, or a misleading attribution.

**Moralized assent.** Wording can invite a reader to believe that declining a claim proves a lack of compassion, intelligence, courage, or loyalty. The relationship between belief and virtue is itself a claim worth examining.

**Intentional deception.** A person may knowingly fabricate or conceal relevant content to induce a response. Claims about such intention require their own evidence; one cannot derive intent solely from the observation that a passage was persuasive or selective.

**Unintended influence.** A writer may offer a viewpoint that recipients understand in ways neither party anticipated. The effects can be beneficial or harmful without being deliberately engineered.

These are **possible analytical distinctions**, not a taxonomy assigning automatic moral guilt. They may overlap, and they may be differently described by competing accounts. They invite further inquiry into evidence, context, power, agency, and consequences.

### Can Calling Something Propaganda Be Propaganda?

Yes, in the sense that **an accusation can itself be used to steer an audience through an unexamined perspective**. A slogan such as “Only fools would believe that propaganda” may pressure a reader to reject an account before its evidence is examined. An institution may use the accusation to suppress criticism. A critic may also use the term in good faith and provide convincing reasons.

This reflexive possibility is central to the ethic. The same analytical questions may be applied to the accusation: What has been encountered? What has been attributed? Which sources are cited? What perspectives are being offered? What alternatives remain? What would change my view? The word *propaganda* cannot exempt its own use from scrutiny.

Nor can the recognition that labels are contestable be used to make evidence permanently irrelevant. A specific false claim may be demonstrated false; a particular omission may be documented; a forged source may be exposed. The larger judgment about the work's character can still remain open to disagreement and further inquiry.

## The Ethics of Group Attribution

A group can be named, can act through recognized practices, can develop consensus, and can remain internally divided. An institution's official statement is not automatically a report of every member's private experience. A survey result, majority vote, unanimous recorded resolution, and claim about *what everyone knows* are not interchangeable.

This matters especially when a public-world account is represented from an apparently collective vantage. A reader may understand the offered account while distinguishing the actual constituency, its authorized speakers, the evidence for consensus, and any voices that are absent or dissenting. A group may be modeled functionally through [**The Mind**](../definitions/defining_the_mind.md) without requiring a conclusion that it possesses one [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md).

Openness values careful representation of others, particularly when a summary may affect their standing, opportunities, or safety. This aspiration is not a rule that every group statement must enumerate every member or that dissent always invalidates institutional decisions. Specific contexts differ. Readers retain their freedom to investigate and assess those choices rather than inherit a predetermined verdict from this ethics.

## Evidence, Consensus, Refusal, and Consequences

Intersubjective inquiry often produces genuine and sometimes durable consensus. Well-replicated research, repeated observation, documented history, and coherent criticism can give strong grounds to prefer a claim. **The fact that an account is imaginally represented is not a reason to discount the evidence supporting its referent.**

A person may nevertheless choose not to affirm an offered account. Openness does not treat evidential strength as ownership of the person's mind. It also does not treat every refusal as equally prudent, caring, or harmless. Decisions made on inaccurate beliefs may cause preventable injuries; acting on a compelling but weakly supported story may harm others; accepting a prevailing account without scrutiny can carry risks of its own.

The ethic distinguishes inward assent, publicly stated judgment, voluntary association, and outward action. A person's freedom to consider an interpretation does not settle obligations to others when acting on it. Questions of consent, confidentiality, reliance, shared resources, institutional authority, and harm may require additional ethical and practical evaluation.

Openness welcomes [**Discernment**](../definitions/defining_discernment.md), [**Epistemic Groundedness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_epistemic_groundedness.md), and [**Epistemic Hospitality**](../../generative/definitions/defining_epistemic_hospitality.md) without requiring readers to manufacture doubt about strong evidence or resolve every question before acting.

## A Noncompulsory Toolkit for Reader Agency

The following are **available moves, not stages, rules for moral worth, or a certification that an account is manipulative**. A reader may use one, several, none, or return to them as their inquiry changes.

1. **Notice** the perspective offered and anything that arises spontaneously while encountering it.
2. **Locate** what is directly encountered in *my-reality*, and what is proposed within *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) or an attributed *imaginal-their-reality*.
3. **Distinguish** an observed statement, an inference, a represented inner life, a claim of collective belief, an aspiration, and an evidential conclusion.
4. **Ask whose vantage** is being offered, and what additional voices or relevant contexts might matter.
5. **Consider the grounds**: evidence, source, coherence, contradiction, corroboration, and uncertainty.
6. **Try on** an unfamiliar perspective provisionally where doing so seems worthwhile; understanding is not endorsement.
7. **Step back** toward one's own first-person encounter when desired or possible.
8. **Choose** among continued inquiry, partial affirmation, suspension, disagreement, participation, withdrawal, or another response.
9. **Revisit** the choice in light of new experience or reasons, including the possible costs borne by oneself and others.
10. **Turn the questions on this toolkit** when its own vocabulary or ethical suggestions feel like an imposed viewpoint.

The toolkit does not promise perfect metacognitive access, the ability to halt every involuntary association, or freedom from social pressure and unequal power. Its value is the possible emergence of additional **recognized choices**, not a demand for uninterrupted surveillance of one's own mind.

## Developed Examples

### Example — The Crowd and the Camera

I watch a short video showing three people applauding a proposed city park renovation. The voice-over states, “The entire neighborhood celebrates the decision.” I can distinguish the applause actually shown from the much broader representation of every resident. A survey might support widespread approval, or it might not; the clip alone does not settle the question.

One viewer calls the video *propaganda*; another calls it a fair shorthand for a widely supported proposal. Both labels offer interpretations. I can examine whether relevant residents were excluded, whether the claim of unanimity was intentional, and what broader evidence exists without treating either label as an automatic verdict.

### Example — A Critic's Counter-Propaganda

An essayist writes, “The documentary is obvious propaganda; only a gullible person could believe it.” I notice that the accusation itself offers a vantage in which disagreement becomes evidence of gullibility. The documentary might indeed be misleading; the essayist might have uncovered decisive evidence. Neither conclusion follows from the insult.

I may ask the critic to identify particular omissions or errors, consider the documentary's sources, or decline the exchange. The possibility of stepping back applies to **both** the original account and its denunciation.

### Example — “Every Caring Parent”

A fundraising appeal declares, “Every caring parent will immediately support this plan.” The wording couples support for a particular proposal with the recipient's moral identity. I may care deeply about the cause while disputing the claim that one policy follows necessarily from that care.

The appeal may be sincere, poorly considered, strategically manipulative, or somewhere between these descriptions. Rather than granting this ethics the power to classify the sender, I can examine the reasons and choose a relationship to the offer.

### Example — A Shared Scientific Account

I read a synthesis of decades of repeatable scientific research. I encounter the words within *my-reality* and consider an account offered through *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**). The report may be supported by unusually strong evidence and broad scientific agreement. I remain free to examine it, though declining its conclusions might produce mistaken decisions with practical consequences.

The imaginal distinction helps me understand **how** the account becomes available; it does not imply that an unsupported alternative deserves equal evidential weight. Neither the ethic nor the text's authority can replace the inquiry into the grounds themselves.

### Example — The Neighborhood Association's Voice

A committee votes seven to four to recommend closing a library. Its newsletter says, “The neighborhood has unanimously spoken.” The first statement may be an accurate record of a valid board decision; the second attributes uniform assent to a much larger population. An individual resident may recognize the group attribution and reject it without claiming the recorded vote never happened.

Whether the newsletter is rightly called propaganda, ordinary exaggeration, a strategic misrepresentation, or an authorized statement with careless wording remains a question readers and participants may dispute. Documented facts, however, can inform that dispute.

### Example — A Fictional Film and the Enjoyment of a Vantage

I watch a fictional detective film whose camera seems to know what every character is thinking. I willingly inhabit that viewpoint and enjoy its dramatic effects. The perspective is an imaginative artistic invitation; the fact that it is not an ordinary first-person witness's standpoint does not render it ethically suspect.

Later I may notice that an editing choice deliberately misled me about a character's motive. I may admire the surprise, criticize the technique, or both. The ethic does not dictate whether that artistic experience ought to count as deception.

### Example — A Friend's Spiritual Testimony

A friend describes a presence they experienced during a difficult night. I hear the testimony directly but consider their reported encounter imaginally. I may provisionally inhabit their interpretation, ask questions about its source, share my doubts, or decline to speculate. The person's experiencing, sincerity, and the ultimate cause of the event remain distinguishable.

My willingness to listen need not imply adopting the explanation; their freedom to report the experience need not impose a belief upon me. Both may continue relating within **Intersubjectivity** despite different interpretations.

### Example — An AI Offers an Apparently Universal Summary

I read an AI-generated summary: “Experts agree that this policy has no disadvantages.” The wording may flatten multiple disciplines, assumptions, and disputed outcomes into one perspective. I can inquire what evidence supports the summary and whose conclusions are included. The fact that the output came from AI neither proves manipulation nor independently validates its universal claim.

I can also examine how the summary's confident tone affects my interpretation, without requiring a conclusion about the AI's subjective intentions or [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md).

### Example — This Ethics Examines Itself

I read that Openness values interpretive sovereignty and the freedom to step back. Those are **Openness's chosen ethical preferences**, not facts compelled by understanding Imaginality. I can notice that this document itself offers a perspective—one that favors greater visibility of perspective shifts—and I may accept, challenge, qualify, or decline that invitation.

If its author calls a critic *closed* or *captured* simply for disagreeing, the same questions about imposed viewpoints apply to Openness's own words. Openness cannot preserve reader agency by reserving immunity from criticism for its preferred ethic.

## Essential Ethical Distinctions

**Freedom of expression versus compelled audience.** One person may speak; another may refuse to listen or disengage. Freedom to offer is not a guarantee of another person's assent or attention.

**Ethical stance versus descriptive anchor.** The fact that a perspective is imaginal, unnoticed, unbound, or imposed does not, by itself, settle whether its use is right or wrong.

**Freedom of assent versus evidence.** A person may refuse a well-supported account; that freedom does not weaken the evidence or remove the possible consequences of acting on the refusal.

**Unchosen response versus considered choice.** A representation may arise before deliberate choice; the possibilities for subsequent reflection or withdrawal vary among people and circumstances.

**Strong persuasion versus covert manipulation.** Both may engage emotions and imagination. Assessing their differences is a contextual, contestable ethical inquiry, not an automatic result of identifying an imaginal perspective.

**Labeling versus demonstrating.** The word *propaganda* can be used analytically, polemically, carefully, or manipulatively. A label is itself an offered interpretation; specific alleged omissions or falsehoods require their own evidence.

**Institutional position versus unanimous inner belief.** A group may validly authorize a statement while its members remain heterogeneous. Accuracy of a group's representation is independently examinable.

**Caring versus compelled agreement.** A recipient may share an aspiration or concern without accepting the particular policy, explanation, or donation demanded by a message.

**Withdrawal versus suppression.** An individual may step back, establish boundaries, or refuse an encounter. Questions concerning organized coercion, censorship, and sanctions connect to [**The Ethics of Closedness**](ethics_of_closedness.md) and warrant separate analysis.

**Epistemic humility versus indiscriminate doubt.** Considering a perspective as represented does not require treating all claims as equally credible; [**Discernment**](../definitions/defining_discernment.md) and continuing inquiry remain available.

## Relationship to Openness

**G — Generative Openness.** Art, narratives, images, and [**Resonant Questions**](../../generative/definitions/defining_resonant_questions.md) can offer unfamiliar viewpoints with great imaginative power. **Nonimposition** supports the aspiration to make participation and assent free rather than covertly manufactured.

**P — Philosophical Openness.** The ethics is a companion to the five conceptual definitions in this family. [**The Ethics of Closedness**](ethics_of_closedness.md), [**Epistemic Groundedness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_epistemic_groundedness.md), [**Blind Certainty**](../core/defining_blind_certainty.md), and [**Suspending Judgment**](../core/defining_suspending_judgment.md) provide related but independently stewarded considerations.

**S — Spiritual Openness.** Listening, care, spiritual testimony, vulnerability, and choosing can involve strong invitations to inhabit another person's interpretation. No one individual's first-person encounter becomes another person's obligatory belief merely by being deeply meaningful.

**T — Transcendent Openness.** [**That Which Is Larger**](../../transcendent/core/defining_that_which_is_larger.md) and [**The Faithful Rendering**](../../transcendent/core/defining_that_which_is_larger.md#the-faithful-rendering) invite fidelity to encounters, epistemic status, and further possibilities while leaving readers free to examine offered interpretations.

## Boundary and Ongoing Inquiry

This ethic does not classify all emotionally powerful art as propaganda, appoint a central authority over the label, prohibit moral disagreement, or demand that every reader adopt Openness's conception of sovereignty. It cannot promise that all manipulative influence will become detectable, that there is always a feasible exit, or that independent judgments are immune to error and social conditioning.

Freedom of inward interpretation does not erase responsibilities arising from outward conduct, shared decisions, consent, confidentiality, and possible harm. Strongly justified interpretations may deserve strong practical preference without becoming mandatory convictions for every mind. The relationships among persuasion, power, evidence, social sanction, and autonomy remain open to further inquiry.

Above all, **calling an account propaganda is itself an offer of perspective**. Openness can hold the tension between that accusation, its contestation, the available evidence, and the reader's unfolding choice without declaring its own labels beyond question.

## Summary

**The Ethics of Perspectivity** offers a toolkit for recognizing, examining, provisionally inhabiting, affirming, declining, and stepping back from perspectives. It protects the reader's agency without seeking control of the reader's conclusions, and welcomes vigorous speech without treating persuasion as automatic wrongdoing. **Its own invitation remains open to the same examination.**
