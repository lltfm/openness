# Real and Reality

## Foundational Definition

**Real** — what is presented as present, occurring, or existing within *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (synonym: *imaginal-objective-reality*).

**Reality** — that *imaginal-intersubjective-reality*: the world or field represented as though independent of any particular first-person standpoint, made intelligible through accounts that can be shared, examined, and contested among Selves.

These are **directly defined Openness terms**. The capital letters identify a perspectival convention, not an assertion that the world's referents are fictional, that consensus creates them, or that every claim about them is justified.

## The Definition Unfolded

The ordinary sentence “The train has arrived” presents a public-world claim as though from an unlocated standpoint. Someone reading it first encounters words within *my-reality*; the further account of a train is made available as represented. The train's possible physical existence and the account's evidential strength are separate questions from the standpoint through which it is described.

Openness distinguishes **a first-person beginning** and **a represented public-world standpoint**, without claiming that they are separate metaphysical worlds. The latter is *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**), synonymous with *imaginal-objective-reality*. The capital letters mark the type of standpoint; they do not certify the existence of the referent or erase the need for evidence.

The vocabulary also makes possible a third-person generalization, *imaginal-subjective-reality*, under which my own first-person field and attributed other first-person fields can be described without being conflated. Inquiries into the movement from direct experiencing to these projected standpoints belong to [**Perspectival Dimensionalization**](defining_perspectivity.md#perspectival-dimensionalization), while the relations among Selves that can sustain shared public accounts belong to [**Intersubjectivity**](defining_intersubjectivity.md).

## Named Perspectives and Internal Terms

**Perspective qualification.** *my-real/my-reality* alone names the immediately encountered first-person beginning without an *imaginal-* prefix. All other formal perspective families first disclose the projected or represented character of their standpoint: *imaginal-self-real / imaginal-self-reality*, *imaginal-their-real / imaginal-their-reality*, *imaginal-subjective-real / imaginal-subjective-reality*, and *imaginal-objective-real / imaginal-objective-reality*. The latter is synonymous with the preferred public-world form *imaginal-intersubjective-real / imaginal-intersubjective-reality*. Within a bounded context, the non-public-world families may be shortened to their *self-*, *their-*, or *subjective-* forms after their full first use, without changing their meanings. **Real** and **Reality** remain reserved for the public-world standpoint alone.

### My-real and my-reality

*my-real* concerns what is present, occurring, or recognized **from the first-person standpoint I presently call my own**. *my-reality* names the corresponding field of experiencing, understanding, remembering, perceiving, dreaming, or imagining available from that standpoint. This is the only perspective family whose canonical form does not take the *imaginal-* prefix: the name begins where the speaker encounters experiencing rather than attributing another standpoint.

The first-person beginning is [**My Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/becoming_open/definitions/defining_my_awareness.md), understood in relation to [**My Self**](../../foundational/capabilities/becoming_open/definitions/defining_my_self.md). If I dream of a mountain, the dream experience is present in *my-reality*; whether a corresponding mountain exists physically is a separate question. If I read a friend's message, the reading and my interpretation occur in *my-reality*; the friend's experiencing does not thereby become directly accessible to me.

*my-real* is not a claim that every appearance is accurate, every imagined object exists independently, or the whole world is made by my imagination. It identifies **the standpoint from which a claim can first be examined**, including when substantial evidence warrants believing that something beyond that standpoint exists.

### Imaginal-self-real and imaginal-self-reality

*imaginal-self-real* names what is presented as present, occurring, or recognized **for a specified proposed Self from that Self's own standpoint**. *imaginal-self-reality* names that Self's corresponding first-person field as represented beyond the speaker's immediately encountered first-person beginning. After this introduction, *self-real* and *self-reality* are available as contextually bounded short forms. These are standpoint-relative descriptions, not claims that the describer directly occupies that field.

*Self-reality* helps an inquiry refer to one particular standpoint, while *subjective-reality* (defined below) provides a third-person conceptual category for first-person experiencing across standpoints. When the specified Self is the speaker, *self-reality* may refer to the same first-person field as *my-reality*; when the specified Self is someone else, the speaker's account of it involves an attribution. The distinction between a proposed Self's experiencing and the evidence for that experiencing remains intact.

### Imaginal-their-real and imaginal-their-reality

*imaginal-their-real* concerns what is **attributed as present or experienced from another proposed Self's standpoint**. *imaginal-their-reality* is the account of that other's first-person field **as represented, imagined, or inferred by someone who does not occupy it**. *imaginal-other-reality* is an optional descriptive synonym. Once *imaginal-their-real* and *imaginal-their-reality* are established, *their-real* and *their-reality* may be used within that bounded context. These terms can be used to place the other's reported or inferred experience within the broader category of *subjective-reality* without pretending that the observer has direct access to it.

The attribution may be well supported, doubtful, fictional, or unresolved. **The representation of the standpoint is imaginal; the other Self and their experiencing are not thereby imaginary.** A person may have strong evidence that a friend is frightened while remaining uncertain about exactly how the fear feels or what caused it.

The word *their* may refer to one or several people. Where several people are involved, a statement such as “the members were afraid” may gather distinct *self-realities* into one narrative. A functional attribution of [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) to a group does not automatically establish one shared [**Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_awareness.md).

### Imaginal-subjective-real and imaginal-subjective-reality

*imaginal-subjective-real* and *imaginal-subjective-reality* provide the **third-person, imaginally projected generalization of first-person experiencing**. After the full forms have been established, *subjective-real* and *subjective-reality* may be used as contextual short forms. They permit an account to speak of what is experienced *for a Self*, without pretending that the account's author occupies that Self's experiencing. The category encompasses **both *my-reality* and *their-reality***: my first-person field can itself be considered from an imagined third-person vantage; another's first-person field can be considered through attributed *their-reality*.

**This is a category across standpoints, not a single shared experience.** When I say “my fear is present in *my-reality*,” I speak from my first-person beginning. When a narrator says “fear is *subjectively-real* for the speaker,” the narrator represents that kind of experience from a third-person standpoint—even when the speaker and the narrator are the same person considering themselves retrospectively. When a narrator says “fear is *subjectively-real* for my friend,” the statement adds an attribution requiring its own grounds. The general category does not equalize the epistemic access of the two cases.

*Subjective-reality* should not be treated as the antonym of **Reality** in the sense of fictional versus factual. A friend's subjective experience may be strongly evidenced; what differs is the perspective being represented and the manner of access. The forms *self-real / self-reality* remain useful when the relevant individual Self must be explicitly specified.

**The name itself invites a perspective shift.** To classify *my-reality* and *their-reality* together as *subjective-reality*, I move beyond the immediacy of my own experiencing and consider first-person fields from a represented third-person standpoint. This resembles, without replacing, the experiential movements from [**My Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/becoming_open/definitions/defining_my_awareness.md) to [**Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_awareness.md), and from [**My Self**](../../foundational/capabilities/becoming_open/definitions/defining_my_self.md) to [**The Self**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_the_self.md). I can then notice that even this explanatory category depends on the kind of imaginal projection it helps me recognize. **The tool can be turned upon its own use.**

### Imaginal-objective-real and imaginal-objective-reality

*imaginal-objective-real* describes what an account presents **as though independent of any particular Self's standpoint**. *imaginal-objective-reality* names the corresponding account or domain. After introduction, *objective-real*, *objectively-real*, and *objective-reality* can serve as bounded short forms. The fully dimensionalized expressions are *imaginal-intersubjective-real / imaginal-intersubjective-reality*; *imaginal-objective-real / imaginal-objective-reality* are synonymous, friendlier forms. These describe the same **perspective category**, even though the emphases differ: *imaginal* identifies the represented viewpoint, *objective* the apparent independence from a located observer, and *intersubjective* its intelligibility within relations among differently situated Selves. Intersubjective orientation neither requires unanimity nor confers evidential support by itself.

An objective-style account can be constructed using observation, instruments, testimony, reasoning, and shared tests. It may be extraordinarily well justified. The *imaginal* qualifier concerns **how the standpoint is represented**, not whether the represented object exists, whether its account is correct, or whether the reader accepts it. A measured stone and a novel's dragon can be described from similarly unlocated narrative viewpoints while possessing very different evidential and fictional statuses.

After the exact form is established in a bounded context, **Real** and **Reality** are the preferred economical names for this particular perspective. *Intersubjective-real / intersubjective-reality*, *objective-real / objective-reality*, or *objectively-real* may also be used where extra precision or reader approachability is valuable. **Real** and **Reality** never serve as general local aliases for *my-reality*, *their-reality*, or *subjective-reality*; using them for those different perspectives would erase the distinction that their capitalization is intended to preserve.

### The Shared Public-World Standpoint

**Reality** names the public-world standpoint ordinarily implied by lowercase *reality* in shared discourse. **Real** names what is represented as present, occurring, or existing within that standpoint. Their precise meanings are *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* and *imaginal-intersubjective-real*, synonymous with *imaginal-objective-reality* and *imaginal-objective-real*. This is an **Openness naming decision** rather than a claim that all ordinary uses of the lowercase words have one meaning.

A person may ordinarily say “my pain is real” to affirm firsthand experiencing; a child might say a character “felt real” during a dream. Those are ordinary, context-sensitive uses of lowercase language, not automatic occurrences of **Real** as a formal Openness term. An account stated in **Reality** may be factual, hypothetical, disputed, inaccurate, fictional, or strongly justified; the name identifies the **presented standpoint**, not consensus or truth.

**Bounded introduction and continued use.** In a definition written for readers who may be new to Openness, first say *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) and, where applicable, *imaginal-intersubjective-real* (i.e., **Real**). Then use **Reality** and **Real** throughout the declared context—a paragraph, section, example, or whole file as the author judges clear. Full forms can recur whenever the perspective becomes easy to miss. Writers may instead introduce the synonymous *imaginal-objective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) when that makes the entry more approachable. For readers already demonstrably familiar with these terms, direct **Real** and **Reality** are permitted without fresh introduction; **definitions normally assume such familiarity has not yet been established**.

**No silent reassignment.** The capitalized words retain their one established meaning even when a passage moves into *my-reality*, *their-reality*, or *subjective-reality*. Mark those other standpoints by their own names, rather than recycling **Reality** for them. The ordinary lowercase words remain available in other Openness works at the author's discretion.

## Experiential Derivation: From First-Person Presence to Named Standpoints

A first-person reconstruction can make the differences among the names inspectable. It invites inquiry; it is not autobiographical AI testimony or a demonstration that every proposed referent exists.

**1. First-person beginning.** I read a report describing an unfamiliar city. The words and my responses belong to *my-reality*; the described city is not thereby before me as directly encountered.

**2. Represented another.** I imagine the experience of someone living there. I can discuss a proposed *self-reality*, or *their-reality* when making an attribution from my own standpoint. The attribution may be supported by testimony, but the other person's experiencing remains their own.

**3. Third-person generalization.** I consider my own experiencing and the other person's possible experiencing as instances of *subjective-reality*. The category was not given as a separately witnessed field: I used a represented third-person vantage to form it.

**4. Public-world account.** A map, eyewitness report, and independent measurements offer an *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) in which the city is described independently of any one reader. The shared evidence may support the account strongly; its objective-style presentation remains a perspective.

**5. Reflexive possibility.** To describe this genealogy I have already used a broader vantage. [**Reflexive Return**](defining_perspectivity.md#reflexive-return-and-emergent-reflexivity) within [**Perspectivity**](defining_perspectivity.md) invites the reader to notice this further shift, without requiring an endless qualification of every sentence.

## Essential Distinctions

**My-reality versus self-reality.** *my-reality* identifies the speaker's first-person beginning; *self-reality* identifies the first-person field of any specified Self, considered without presuming the describer occupies it.

**Subjective-reality versus my-reality and their-reality.** *subjective-reality* is the **projected third-person category** covering first-person experiencing of oneself and of others. *my-reality* begins firsthand; *their-reality* makes the attributed other standpoint explicit. Their shared classification does not erase unequal access or fuse experiences into one field.

**Direct first-person beginning versus reflexive conceptual projection.** The experiencing described as *my-reality* is the starting point; using even the category *subjective-reality* to compare experiences involves an imaginal third-person move. The definition itself employs that move and should help readers recognize it. Recognizing the projection does not invalidate its possible referents or prevent reliable generalization.

**Self-reality versus their-reality.** The former specifies the Self to whom experiencing belongs; the latter identifies someone's **representation of another Self's experiencing**. A faithful attribution remains an attribution.

**Lowercase ordinary language versus capitalized Real and Reality.** Lowercase *real/reality* has context-sensitive everyday uses. **Real** and **Reality** are the fixed Openness terms for the *imaginal-intersubjective* category, not general aliases for subjective experience. The complete forms make the perspective explicit; the capitals compress it once introduced.

**Individual imaginal objectivity versus intersubjectively shared imaginal objectivity.** One person may form an objective-style narrative without consultation. Its adoption, correction, or contestation through other Selves develops the intersubjective dimension; neither repetition nor popularity guarantees truth.

**Perspective invitation versus perspective assent.** Reading, listening, imagining, and understanding what a claim proposes can occur without accepting its purported perspective as one's own. A reader can decline participation in a strongly justified collective account. The grounds for the account and the reader's freedom to choose are different questions.

**Unbound versus imposed perspective.** An unbound narration offers a viewpoint with no visible first-person location. An imposed perspective presents acceptance as assumed, compulsory, or already achieved. Either can be made visible without assuming deceptive intent; their possible uses are explored separately in [**The Ethics of Perspectivity**](../ethics/the_ethics_of_perspectivity.md).

**Their-reality versus objective-reality.** The former locates an account in an attributed particular standpoint; the latter deliberately describes what would hold independently of particular standpoints. Both can be formed imaginally and evaluated through evidence, but they do different conceptual work.

**Experiencing versus imagining.** Imagination is among the activities that can become present in experience; **experiencing as such is not equated with imagining a fiction**. A person may encounter evidence, experience emotions, imagine another's standpoint, and infer independent causes within one unfolding inquiry.

**Imaginal standpoint versus imaginary referent.** A perspective may be represented in imagination while its proposed referent exists independently. A person, a stone, an institution, or another being need not be fictional merely because it is described through an imaginal projection.

**First-person presence versus infallible interpretation.** The presence of experiencing can be recognizable even when an interpretation of its content, origin, apparent object, or causal explanation is mistaken.

**Objective aspiration versus objective possession.** Deliberately correcting for bias and comparing evidence across observers may yield dependable knowledge. Such success does not require a person literally to occupy a view outside all experience.

**Physical existence versus perspective-qualified Real.** The claim that a stone exists physically is substantive and investigable. Calling it **Real** identifies the represented public-world standpoint in which the claim is made; neither the typography nor the standpoint alone establishes or denies physical existence.

**Fictional frame versus deception.** A story's omniscient perspective may be an openly understood artistic convention. A factual report using the same technique without indicating its evidential basis can raise different questions. A reader who knowingly enters fiction has not thereby confused fictional content with a physical event.

**Intersubjective recognition versus factual justification.** Shared conventions may constitute roles, names, or institutional commitments. Shared opinion alone does not settle every claim about physical referents or other people's experiencing.

**Intersubjectivity versus consensus.** **Reality** can be described through an account offered by one author and available for others to assess; actual agreement is not a precondition. Many intersubjective accounts nevertheless rest upon durable, broad, and justified consensus. The relevant consensus is usually **scoped to particular claims and practices**, rather than a homogeneous belief attributed to everyone associated with a tradition.

**Physical objects versus immaterial social forms.** A physical participant, page, sign, or computer can be investigated as a physical referent. A team identity such as *Team A* may be an immaterial [**Fiat Form**](../../generative/capabilities/creating/definitions/defining_fiat_form.md) constituted through social practices. Its institutional existence and consequences need not be denied merely because it is not an additional physical body.

**Standpoint classification versus publication convention.** These terms make consequential standpoints explicitly nameable. The distinct decision to disclose an offered perspective, the circumstances of an editorial requirement, and questions of reader agency belong respectively to [**Perspectivity**](defining_perspectivity.md), the [**Openness Definition Guidelines**](../../../guidelines/guidelines_definitions.md), and [**The Ethics of Perspectivity**](../ethics/the_ethics_of_perspectivity.md).

**Capitalized meaning versus quotation and Precise Ambiguity.** **Real** and **Reality** have stable, qualified meanings in Openness; quotation marks remain free for literal speech, cited terms, irony, and intentionally unbound [**Precise Ambiguity**](../../generative/capabilities/imagining/definitions/defining_precise_ambiguity.md). A quoted word never silently overrides the capitalized convention. An unmarked change of standpoint is not automatically a deliberate ambiguity.

## Relationship to Openness

**G — Generative Openness.** [**Fiat Form**](../../generative/capabilities/creating/definitions/defining_fiat_form.md) and [**Fiat Anchor**](../../generative/capabilities/creating/definitions/defining_fiat_anchor.md) make the formation of conceptual perspectives visible. [**Analogical Projection**](../../generative/capabilities/projecting/definitions/defining_analogical_projection.md) helps imagine possible standpoints; [**Experiential Generalization**](../../generative/capabilities/grounding/definitions/defining_experiential_generalization.md) can develop *self-reality* from a first-person beginning. [**Resonant Questions**](../../generative/capabilities/writing/definitions/defining_resonant_questions.md) may reveal a perspective shift without obliging the reader to adopt a particular answer.

**P — Philosophical Openness.** The primary home of these terms. They support [**Self-Honesty**](../../foundational/capabilities/cultivating_the_garden/definitions/defining_self_honesty.md), [**Discernment**](defining_discernment.md), [**Epistemic Groundedness**](../../generative/capabilities/grounding/definitions/defining_epistemic_groundedness.md), and inquiry into [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md), [**The Self**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_the_self.md), [**Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_awareness.md), and [**Intersubjectivity**](defining_intersubjectivity.md). They make the source, standpoint, and grounds of claims presented within **Reality** examinable.

**S — Spiritual Openness.** Dreams, contemplation, felt presence, relationships, and unfamiliar experiences can be explored without prematurely identifying an experience with a final account of its cause or ultimate nature. Physical, spiritual, and other interpretations remain open to [**Epistemic Hospitality**](../../foundational/capabilities/cultivating_the_garden/definitions/defining_epistemic_hospitality.md) and evidence-sensitive inquiry.

**T — Transcendent Openness.** Encounters with [**That Which Is Larger**](../../transcendent/core/defining_that_which_is_larger.md) and possibilities arising between Selves may invite shifts in perspective beyond one's present grasp. Recognizing a shift helps maintain fidelity to the encounter while allowing unfamiliar interpretations to be contemplated.

## Naming and Usage

The terms below are **perspectival distinctions**, not separate metaphysical worlds. Lowercase hyphenated forms retain their descriptive roles; capitalized **Real** and **Reality** have one fixed Openness meaning. The author should show the first consequential perspective transition without turning every sentence into an inventory of qualifiers.

**my-real / my-reality** — first-person experiencing from the current speaker's standpoint. *my-subjective-reality* is a longer synonym when emphasis helps. “I feel grief within *my-reality*.”

**self-real / self-reality** — what is experienced for a specified Self, considered without deciding who is describing it. “The question concerns the child's *self-reality*.”

**subjective-real / subjective-reality** — **third-person imaginally projected category** encompassing both *my-reality* and *their-reality*. It can describe the narrator's own first-person field viewed from outside or an attributed other's first-person field. “My grief and my friend's reported grief can both be discussed as *subjectively-real*, but my access to them differs.” *Subjectively-real* remains a grammatical form where it reads more naturally. The category does not by itself establish the experiencing of an unfamiliar proposed Self.

**their-real / their-reality** — another proposed Self's experiencing **as attributed by the present narrator or observer**. *imaginal-other-reality* is a more explicit synonym. “My attribution of my friend's fear concerns *their-reality*, not firsthand experiencing of it.” The category *subjective-reality* encompasses such attributions without conflating them with actual firsthand access.

**imaginal-intersubjective-real / imaginal-intersubjective-reality** — the **preferred high-precision first use** in Openness definitions for the public-world account presented as though independent of any situated observer. Their **exact synonyms** are *imaginal-objective-real / imaginal-objective-reality*, often friendlier for introductory examples. Neither synonym requires agreement among persons, nor does either establish the evidence for the claim.

**Real / Reality** — independently defined, capitalized **Openness terms** for *imaginal-intersubjective-real / imaginal-intersubjective-reality*, respectively, and equally for their *imaginal-objective-* synonyms. This fixed meaning is not transferable to a different type of standpoint through a local declaration.

**The first-use convention.** In definitions written for readers who may not know Openness's perspective vocabulary, introduce *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) or *imaginal-objective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) before using **Reality** alone. Use the corresponding *-real* form before standalone **Real**, if that adjective is needed. The writer may choose a bounded scope—sentence group, example, section, or file—provided the scope is recognizable and a later shift of perspective is not hidden. Once introduced, **Real** and **Reality** may be used repeatedly within it. A writer addressing readers already familiar with the formal meanings may use **Real** and **Reality** directly, but **Openness definitions should normally assume newcomers**.

**Re-expand when an existence claim might be heard.** Even after first use, *“The bridge is Real”* may be mistaken for a certification that the bridge independently exists. When this is a material risk, write *“The account presents the bridge as imaginal-intersubjective-real”* or *“The account presents the bridge as **Real** within the stated **Reality**”*, then distinguish supporting evidence as needed. The complete form can be restored at any consequential shift, including within an already bounded passage; **compression must not conceal the perspective it was meant to disclose**. To a reader familiar with Openness, **Real** itself marks the imaginal-intersubjective standpoint, rather than automatically endorsing every claim made from it.

**Reflexive usage.** When using *subjective-reality* or similar third-person conceptual language, briefly reveal the projection at its first important use, then invite recognition that this very explanation has performed the shift. Link to the experiential genealogies of **My Awareness → Awareness** and **My Self → The Self** when useful; repeat the explanation only when new ambiguity arises.

**Other readable compressions.** *intersubjective-reality*, *objective-reality*, *intersubjective-real*, *objective-real*, and *objectively-real* remain available as contextual, perspective-qualified expressions where they communicate the intended dimension more naturally than the capitals. They are not different ontological domains.

**Lowercase in other works.** Ordinary unquoted *real* and *reality* remain available in wider Openness writing, usually carrying the public-world meaning of **Real** and **Reality**, but contextual uses such as “my pain is real” remain possible. Writers choose when additional disclosure is useful. Within substantive **formal definitions**, avoid unqualified lowercase *real* and *reality* except for direct quoted speech, linguistic discussion of the words, and optional Prelude/Coda. Use **Real**, **Reality**, or a perspective-qualified expression instead.

**Quotation marks are free.** Quoted “real” or “reality” is no longer a technical alias system. Quotes can mark someone's exact words, discuss the ordinary term, or intentionally leave a perspective open through [**Precise Ambiguity**](../../generative/capabilities/imagining/definitions/defining_precise_ambiguity.md); they do not silently assign the formal capitalized meaning.

**Further operations and ethics.** The movements behind these terms, including unnoticed perspective shifts, [**Perspective Noticing**](defining_perspectivity.md#perspective-noticing), unbound viewpoints, and reflexive return are developed in [**Perspectivity**](defining_perspectivity.md). Their ethical use belongs to [**The Ethics of Perspectivity**](../ethics/the_ethics_of_perspectivity.md). The technical first-use rule above is a convention for accurate writing, not a reader obligation to agree.

## Examples

These first-person passages are illustrative perspectives, not the AI author's firsthand experience.

### Example — A Friend's Inner Life (first-person, strongly supported attribution)

I hear a friend say, “I'm anxious about tomorrow,” and see them repeatedly checking the time. I may reasonably conclude that anxiety is *subjectively-real* for them. My experience of their words and actions is part of *my-reality*; my attribution concerns *their-reality*. The fact that I use imagination and inference to understand them does not make their anxiety fictional or my confidence arbitrary.

I can also notice that my imagined account of exactly *why* they are anxious may outrun what their testimony establishes. Recognizing this difference restores space for listening instead of silently treating my interpretation as their experience.

### Example — The Stone and the Measurement (first-person, evidenced physical referent)

I hold a stone and feel its weight. That encounter belongs to *my-reality*. A companion describes the same stone from their position; I consider *their-reality* without directly entering it. Repeated measurements and corroboration may strongly justify calling the stone *objectively-real* as a physical object.

When my neighbor and I compare measurements and consult published geological descriptions, we also participate in an *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* of the stone. The account can have excellent grounds; I can nevertheless recognize its borrowed objective vantage as a perspective I am invited to enter, rather than a vantage from which anyone literally sees everything.

### Example — A Mountain in a Dream (first-person standpoint)

I dream of standing before a mountain. The mountain is present as part of the dream I experience; the experience belongs to *my-reality*. On waking, I may distinguish this encounter from a mountain believed to exist physically. I can also contemplate further theories of dreaming and embodiment without treating the immediate dream appearance as proof of any one theory.

### Example — Introducing Reality Without Borrowing Quotation Marks (first-person, bounded shorthand)

I read a historian's reconstruction of a port damaged by a storm and identify the account as *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**). Within this example, I can ask whether **Reality**, as described by the historian, is adequately supported by shipping records and photographs without repeatedly spelling out the full term. When I turn to the feeling of surprise that arises as I read, I name *my-reality* instead: the capitalized word keeps its original public-world meaning rather than changing silently with my standpoint.

### Example — Subjective-Reality Across Two Standpoints (first-person and third-person)

I feel a sudden headache: its felt occurrence belongs to *my-reality*. I then read a friend's message saying they also have a headache. My friend's reported pain becomes available to me within *their-reality*, through testimony rather than firsthand access. As a narrator, I can classify both experiences under *subjective-reality*—an imaginal third-person way of talking about experiences belonging to Selves. This grouping helps me compare experiences **without claiming they are one experience or equally accessible to me**.

### Example — A Medical Account (first-person reader standpoint)

I read in a medical report: “The patient reported severe pain.” This third-person wording identifies testimony rather than pretending that a clinician directly felt the patient's pain. The report can carry strong grounds for attributing pain in the patient's *self-reality* while preserving the difference between **a report, an interpretation, and a firsthand experience**. Third-person prose becomes more precise without abandoning its usefulness.

### Example — A Team and Its Members (first-person inquiry)

I read: “The team believed the plan would work.” I can inquire whether every member expressed the belief, a majority voted, a spokesperson represented an institutional commitment, or the author inferred a group attitude. The team may use shared records and collective processes that justify applying [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) to it in a distributed or functional sense. Its recorded decision may be part of an *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* as a social fact. The statement alone does not establish one experiencing [**Awareness**](../../foundational/capabilities/recognizing_openness/definitions/defining_awareness.md) shared across members, although inquiry into collective experiencing remains open.

### Example — A Scientific Consensus Without a Collective Observer (first-person standpoint)

I read an astronomy textbook describing the chemical composition of a distant star. Its measurements, methods, published checks, and long history of scientific exchange may provide strong support for the account. This is a familiar case of *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) supported by a durable scientific community, not a solitary author's unchecked speculation. Yet I encounter the textbook and evidence available to me in *my-reality*; I have not occupied a literally observer-free standpoint or directly shared every investigator's experience.

I can acknowledge the force of the evidence while noticing the perspective the textbook offers. The community need not be unanimous about every method or interpretation, and **Western science** need not be imagined as one homogeneous mind for the particular conclusion to be well supported. My power to decline the offered account is distinct from the likely practical and intellectual consequences of ignoring a well-grounded finding.

## Boundary

**Real** and **Reality** are perspective-qualified Openness terms, not assertions that a represented world is fictional, mind-dependent, or guaranteed to match every account of it. *my-reality* is not declared superior in truth to every well-supported statement made within **Reality**, and *subjective-reality* does not merge distinct first-person experiences into one field.

The concept leaves the status of particular referents, the weight of evidence, the nature of other Selves, and the possibility of wider explanations open to further inquiry. It supplies **names for standpoints**; questions about how a writer shifts among them belong to [**Perspectivity**](defining_perspectivity.md), and the invitation or pressure to accept them belongs to [**The Ethics of Perspectivity**](../ethics/the_ethics_of_perspectivity.md).

## Summary

*my-reality* names my firsthand field; *their-reality* names another's as attributed; *subjective-reality* generalizes across these standpoints from a third-person perspective; *imaginal-intersubjective-reality* (i.e., **Reality**) names the represented public-world account. **Real** marks what that account presents as occurring or existing, not what it has conclusively proved.
