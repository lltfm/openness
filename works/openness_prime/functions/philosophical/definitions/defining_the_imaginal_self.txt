The Imaginal Self

Fiat Anchored Ambiguity:

Self — At least this: selfhood given imaginal form, first recognizable in "My Self" and available to imagine other Selves within or beyond that life. But perhaps more?

Foundational Definition

The Imaginal Self, ordinarily Self, is an immaterial Fiat Form through which selfhood acquires a recognizable identity: an embodied life, aspect, possible continuation, other being, or collection may be conceived as a Self.

Its minimal starting application is one's Own Self: an embodied life encountered as one's own can be understood as someone, with an identity and history. This is a first conceptual reference, not a claim that a child consciously invents selfhood before knowing others. The form may be learned, inherited, gradually developed, or accepted without any remembered act of creation.

From this beginning, Analogical Projection may extend Self outward toward other people, animals, computational systems, and collectives, or inward toward remembered, anticipated, or contextually distinguished aspects of a life. The same generative operation makes each application possible; Analogical Discernment examines its grounds and mode of application rather than assuming identical selfhood in every case.

An Imaginal Self can organize physically encountered phenomena, established relationships, and consequential commitments while remaining an immaterial identity. Its being imaginable or intersubjectively shared does not establish another physical body, a separately experiencing Awareness, or a metaphysical unity.

Essential Distinctions

Own Self versus Self. Own Self is the embodied first-person starting point. Self is the imaginal conceptual identity of selfhood first applied to that encounter and then available for further application. The physical body is not identical to the immaterial identity through which it is understood.

Imaginal versus fictional. Imaginal concerns conceptual formation through imagination, not necessarily fantasy or error. An Imaginal Self may organize something physically encountered, remembered, inferred, or collectively adopted; it may also be entirely hypothetical. It need not be consciously visualized.

Creation versus inheritance. Selfhood may be shaped by family, language, culture, and experience before it becomes explicitly recognized. Later adopting I am this person need not reveal when the corresponding conceptual form first arose. An inherited Fiat Form may be reformed.

Self versus Awareness and The Mind. Awareness points toward first-person experiencing; The Mind organizes capacities and processes associated with mental activity; Self is an imaginal identity through which an individual or proposed unity is understood as a Self. Applying the concept does not establish Awareness, a particular Mind, or a separate inner owner.

Projection versus discernment. Analogical Projection may extend Self toward any proposed referent. Analogical Discernment examines the supporting relationships, limits, and kind of application—ordinary, comparative, functional, organizational, symbolic, or exploratory. Default Analogical Discernment may suffice where a familiar application is ordinarily well supported; otherwise explicit examination may be needed. These modes are provisional distinctions, not new kinds of projection.

Temporal continuity versus reconstructed perspective. An earlier stage may be identified as belonging to an embodied life by historical continuity. An imagined conversation with a Past Self adds a representation of that earlier perspective. A Future Self is a possible continuation, not a guaranteed future person. Neither application necessarily establishes an additional experiencing subject.

Inner distinctions versus separate agents. Family Self, Professional Self, and other contextual identities may organize observable differences in conduct or interpretation. Their conceptual separateness does not establish multiple independent Selves or multiple independently experiencing subjects within one body.

Collective identity versus collective body. A team identity is formed, adopted, communicated, or imposed among embodied participants. Its members and artifacts may be physically encountered; the identity Team A is not an additional physical object, speaker, or Awareness. Collective Self names an imaginal application to that conceptual unity.

Imaginative significance versus physical existence. An identity may be deeply significant within Own Self Reality or among other Selves without becoming a distinct physical object. Neither significance nor agreement establishes the nature of Awareness or the independence of the imagined unity.

Relationship to Openness

Philosophical Openness uses Self to examine how the embodied starting point of Own Self acquires identity and how that identity can be extended, subdivided, revised, or questioned. Fiat Anchoring makes the form recognizable; Dimensionalization reveals possible aspects; Analogical Projection explores extensions; Analogical Discernment investigates what each extension justifies.

The resulting inquiry can illuminate The Intersubjective, The Unchosen, Preferred Stories, identity, and Recursive Becoming. An Imaginal Self can influence subsequent actions and interpretations without acquiring a separate physical existence. Imagining a more encompassing or non-dual Self remains a contemplative possibility, not a conclusion established by the form alone.

In this definition, capitalized Self is the usual short name for The Imaginal Self. Lowercase self or the self may denote Own Self when that first-person embodied reference is intended and context is clear.

Examples

Example — The First Application: One's Own Life (positive bright-line)

A person awakens in a familiar body and encounters sensations, memories, and an unfolding life as their own: Own Self. The person understands this continuing life as me, a Self with a name, history, and possible future. That conceptual identity is Imaginal Self. The body is physically encountered; the selfhood under which it is understood is a Fiat Form. Its earliest development may be forgotten or never have involved a deliberate declaration.

Example — Another Person or Animal (outward projection)

Someone recognizes another person as a Self, ordinarily with ample justification. A dog may also be understood as a Self on grounds of embodied individuality, behavior, memory, and relationships. The projections are available through the same operation, though what is justified varies. Neither recognition provides direct access to the other's Awareness.

Example — Family Self and Professional Self (inward dimensionalization)

One embodied life exhibits different habits, speech, concerns, and commitments at home and at work. Distinguishing Family Self and Professional Self may reveal tensions or new possibilities for choosing. These Imaginal Selves organize encountered patterns; they need not be two physically existing people or independently experiencing subjects.

Example — Past Self and Future Self (temporal application)

A photograph helps identify a child as an earlier stage of a present life. Imagining a conversation with that Past Self is a further construction, not the original experience recovered unchanged. Imagining a Future Self likewise explores a possible continuation. The distinction between continuity and representation remains important even when the imagined dialogue is fruitful.

Example — Team A (collective application)

Five people adopt the name Team A, coordinate their work, and come to understand themselves as members of a group. Others learn the name. A sign, records, and the five bodies may physically exist; Team A is the immaterial, intersubjectively sustained identity through which their relationship is distinguished. Calling it a Collective Self adds an imaginal selfhood to that identity, without adding a sixth body, an independent embodied speaker, or a unified Awareness.

Example — An LLM's Self (functional or symbolic application)

An LLM uses “I,” maintains conversational context, and generates a self-description. An Imaginal Self may model that conversational identity and its functional organization. The form does not establish that the system experiences an Own Self, possesses Awareness, or has the same kind of identity as a biological individual.

Example — A Non-dual Self (open possibility)

A poem imagines that apparently distinct Selves belong to one encompassing Self. The form may invite contemplation of separateness and unity without establishing a single experiencing subject, a hidden physical organism, or a completed metaphysical account.

Example — A Stone Given a Biography (negative boundary)

A writer calls a stone the lonely Self of the mountain. The personification can be coherent within a story. The identity's creative value does not establish a stone's Awareness or justify treating the stone as another embodied inquirer. A Self can be imaginally formed without every corresponding ontological claim being supported.

Boundary

The Imaginal Self is not by definition a separate body, soul, permanent ego, autonomous controller, or necessarily experiencing subject. Nor does the immaterial nature of the form imply that its physical referent is imaginary or that its influence on embodied lives is negligible.

An imaginative projection may be compelling, culturally binding, or descriptively useful while its extension requires qualification or rejection. Selfhood first conceived for Own Self must not silently transfer every feature of that starting point to an animal, group, machine, temporal stage, or imagined totality.

Self gives selfhood a form that can be inhabited, projected, examined, and revised without mistaking the form for everything it may represent.
