The Intersubjective

Foundational Definition:

The Intersubjective is the relational field among Selves through which meanings, interpretations, understandings, and shared realities are developed, communicated, negotiated, contested, and revised.

Each Self encounters the world through its own Awareness, yet participates in a world inhabited by other Selves whose experiences cannot be directly known. Through language, relationships, shared practices, and institutions, Selves may develop understandings and possibilities that none could have developed alone.

The Intersubjective includes the ways Selves influence one another's understanding of reality, as well as the ways their interactions may constitute shared social realities.

Its generative possibility lies in encounters among differently situated Selves: what one Self cannot see, another may reveal; what one understanding excludes, another may question; and what emerges between them may exceed what either previously imagined.

Essential Distinctions:

Individual Awareness versus The Intersubjective. Awareness is encountered from a first-person perspective. The Intersubjective concerns relationships among Selves whose experiences remain distinct, even when they develop shared meanings and understandings. Communication does not establish direct access to another Self's Awareness.

Intersubjective Constitution. Some social realities, including recognized roles, agreements, conventions, and institutions, exist through shared practices and recognition. These realities may change when the practices sustaining them change.

Intersubjective Interpretation. Language, testimony, culture, history, and relationships influence how Selves understand experience and reality. Different interpretations may coexist, compete, inform one another, or give rise to meanings not previously considered.

Intersubjective Justification. Claims about what is true may be examined, challenged, corroborated, and revised through shared inquiry. Other Selves can contribute evidence, reasoning, perspectives, and criticism unavailable to an individual inquirer.

Constitution versus Interpretation versus Justification. Constituting a social fact, interpreting a claim, and establishing its justification are different relationships. Agreement may create a convention without establishing the factual truth of every belief associated with it.

Consensus versus Truth. Shared agreement may reflect justified understanding, error, incomplete evidence, or coercion. Consensus does not by itself establish truth, nor does disagreement establish that truth is unknowable. Some realities depend upon shared practices; others do not depend upon agreement.

The Intersubjective versus The Collective Self. The Intersubjective is the wider relational field within which particular Collective Selves may emerge. A Collective Self may develop shared identity, memory, commitments, and capacities for action. Neither concept establishes that a group possesses a unified subjective Awareness.

Participation versus Agreement. Intersubjective relationships need not produce consensus. Dissent, unequal influence, exclusion, misunderstanding, and conflict may shape their development. Meaningful participation does not require adopting another Self's interpretation or surrendering independent judgment.

Relationship to Openness:

The Intersubjective belongs primarily to Philosophical Openness, which investigates how Selves develop, justify, and revise understanding through their relationships with one another and the world.

It informs inquiries into Self, Awareness, Artifice, The Collective Self, Interpretive Sovereignty, Epistemic Hospitality, Nonimposition, and Institutional Witness.

Generative Openness may investigate how encounters among creators, works, and readers generate meanings and further possibilities. Spiritual Openness may explore the lived significance of relationships, shared stories, communities, Love, and becoming.

The Intersubjective supports Openness's aspiration to encounter what is with the greatest possible latitude, while recognizing that other Selves may understand, experience, and value possibilities differently.

Example:

Several readers encounter the same poem and offer different interpretations.

Through discussion, they recognize assumptions, distinctions, and possibilities that none had previously considered. Their shared inquiry may produce new understandings without establishing one final interpretation or requiring agreement.

If the readers form a literary society, establish its rules, and appoint a representative, their shared practices may also constitute recognizable social realities.

Yet agreement within the society does not establish the historical intentions of the poet, the factual truth of every interpretation, or the legitimacy of excluding dissenting voices.

The same intersubjective relationships that make shared understanding possible may also support conformity, misleading consensus, or institutionalized Blind Certainty.

Boundary:

The Intersubjective does not imply that all truth is socially constructed, that every interpretation is equally justified, or that collective agreement determines reality.

Nor does it establish a universal shared consciousness or an independently existing collective mind.

Selves may contribute to the constitution of social realities while remaining subject to realities their agreements cannot alter.

The Intersubjective makes shared meaning, understanding, and further possibility available for inquiry without granting any individual or collective unquestionable authority over what is true.
