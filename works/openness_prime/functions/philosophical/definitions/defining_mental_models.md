# Mental Models

## Prelude

**Fiat Form**: *“Let there be a form called **Mental Models**...”*

**Fiat Anchor**: *“...which gathers recognizable or inferable patterns of expectation and interpretation into a conceptual unity for inquiry...”*

**Experiential Derivation**: *“...beginning with what I anticipate, interpret, imagine, and do—and what surprises me...”*

**Experiential Generalization**: *“...which I may investigate beyond my own experiences and circumstances...”*

**Analogical Projection**: *“...whose possible applications to new situations, referents, and standpoints I may imagine and examine...”*

**Anchored Ambiguity**: *“...which are **at least this**—but also perhaps more?”*

## Foundational Definition

What are **Mental Models**?

**At least this**: provisionally distinguishable patterns of expectation, interpretation, and response, first recognized or inferred through experiences within **My Awareness** as possible organizations of the mind of **My Self**, then generalized as possible organizations within **The Mind**, whose applications may extend inwardly or outwardly across situations, referents, and standpoints.

*But also perhaps more?*

### Example Speculations

- Perhaps some **Mental Models** operate chiefly through bodily expectations, perceptual coordination, learned skills, or felt orientations rather than through explicit inner descriptions or propositions?
- Perhaps a Mental Model that seems to belong to an individual's mental life is partly sustained through relationships with language, other people, tools, and surroundings, without becoming identical to those external supports?
- Perhaps extending a familiar Mental Model into an unexpected situation, another standpoint, or a distant metaphor can transform the model itself, revealing possibilities not recognizable through its original application?
- *But also perhaps more?*

## The Definition Unfolded

**Mental Models** is a **Fiat Anchor** for investigating distinguishable patterns through which aspects of experience, reality, or possibility are anticipated, interpreted, imagined, explained, or responded to. In this definition, the anchor concerns possible organizations within individual minds. Its experiential beginning is found in what can be encountered or inferred from first-person mental life; the concept is then generalized for inquiry beyond that standpoint. The term does not assume that such patterns are consciously chosen, verbal, stable, or directly observable.

The anchor gathers a *kind of possible mental organization* into a conceptual unity. It does not create every organization to which it may refer. A person may anticipate how a door opens, infer another person's intentions, or navigate a familiar room without ever naming a Mental Model. Some operative organization may precede its recognition; whether any particular behavior actually depends on an identifiable model remains a matter for inquiry.

This distinction parallels **The Mind**, but it is not identical to it. **The Mind** gathers mental activities, capacities, inferred processes, and their possible relationships into a broader conceptual unity. **Mental Models** provisionally distinguishes *some patterns within that mental life*, especially those through which a situation is made intelligible, an expectation takes shape, or possible responses are organized. **The Mind** need not consist only of Mental Models, and Mental Models need not be separate components from which a mind is assembled.

### From an Encounter to an Inferred Pattern

I approach a familiar door and push without deliberating. If it opens, my response fits what I have come to expect. If a new door looks similar but opens by pulling, I may experience resistance, surprise, and the need to change my response.

What is directly encountered? My movement, resistance, perhaps surprise, and perhaps an explicitly felt expectation. What is *inferred*? That something in my understanding had organized the expectation that pushing would work. I can provisionally call that inferred organization *my model of this door*.

The same experience might be explained in more than one way. A habit of movement, a perceptual association, a context-sensitive skill, or a richer representation could contribute to it. **Mental Models** names a way to investigate the organization evident in expectation and response; it does not decide, simply by naming, which mechanism produced them.

Some models are more directly available to reflection. I may deliberately imagine the arrangement of a room, mentally rehearse a conversation, or draw an explanation of why a machine works. The imagined relationships may be present in experience even when the processes enabling them remain incompletely understood. A consciously entertained model and a model inferred from habitual action are not thereby identical in mechanism, explicitness, or epistemic status. Both may be considered for applications beyond the encounter through which they became recognizable.

### Fiat Anchoring and the Model Being Investigated

The category **Mental Models** is a chosen conceptual unity: a **Fiat Anchor** gathering certain interpretable patterns under one name. Its identifying boundaries are not uniquely determined by a separately distinguishable material whole. We might have grouped the phenomena differently, or placed different emphasis on expectation, representation, embodied skill, or practical response.

An *individual Mental Model*, however, is not automatically a **Fiat Anchor**. It may be an operative pattern inferred from experience, one that the person did not consciously create, choose, or name. When an investigator deliberately gathers observations under a named model—*my understanding of how this door opens*, for example—that *conceptual specification* may itself be an act of **Fiat Anchoring**. Such specification neither proves that a matching discrete mental object exists nor rules out real organization in mental life.

Likewise, a Mental Model's *referent* is distinct from the model. An expectation about a door is not the door; a model of a friend's intentions is not that friend's inner life; an imagined flood is not a flood. A useful representation may nevertheless correspond to genuine properties and relationships beyond itself.

The plural name does not establish a natural inventory of separate models. Patterns may overlap, combine, compete, reorganize, or resist clean isolation. A specific grouping may be chosen for investigation without being the only adequate grouping.

### Recognizing and Revising an Organization

A Mental Model may be recognizable through recurring expectations, judgments, interpretations, images, plans, habits, or actions. The same outward behavior can arise through different processes, and a change in behavior does not necessarily identify exactly what changed internally. Recognition of a pattern may therefore be well supported without revealing its full organization.

Experience can challenge a model: a door resists, a prediction fails, a trusted account is revised, or an overlooked possibility becomes relevant. The response may be to revise an expectation, combine models, suspend a judgment, learn a new skill, or recognize that the present model remains useful within a narrower scope.

Revision is not always necessary or beneficial. A model may be sufficiently accurate for the purpose at hand. Nor does learning automatically amount to rejecting an earlier understanding: a model can become more nuanced, or a person may learn when to use one model rather than another.

A model may also guide action without being made explicit. Its unrecognized operation does not, by itself, constitute **Blind Certainty** or **Closedness**. Those concepts concern unawareness of closedness and refusal to listen, respectively—not merely the presence of assumptions, habits, confidence, or imperfect models. An operative model may become part of closedness when a person or community refuses to hear what might challenge it, whether that refusal is conscious or not.

### Generalizing the Concept and Reapplying a Model

**Experiential Generalization** begins with patterns recognizable or inferable through first-person experience and develops **Mental Models** as a conceptual category for possible organizations within **The Mind** more broadly. This generalization is not confined to my own thinking, one familiar door, or one explanation of how models operate. It establishes an available conceptual form, not the conclusion that every mind contains the same models.

A related but different movement occurs when *a particular model* is applied beyond its original situation. I may learn to expect that a familiar door opens when I push it; later I approach a garden gate or the lid of a car's trunk using some of what I understand about openings, thresholds, handles, hinges, and movement. The carried-over expectation might help, or it might mislead: the gate may require lifting a latch, and the trunk may require a release mechanism unlike the door's. This is an application of a possible model, not necessarily an exact copy of one mental structure to another.

Such application may be **inward**, as when I use a familiar pattern to reconsider my own reactions, or **outward**, as when I interpret an unfamiliar object, another person's conduct, or a possible future situation. I may deliberately try on a different standpoint through **Perspective-Taking**, or an application may occur habitually before I recognize it. The pattern need not stay fixed: its extension can reshape it, reveal a limitation, or suggest another model.

A model may also be extended **analogically or metaphorically**. When I speak of *opening new doors* to opportunities, a familiar organization of barriers, thresholds, access, and passage may help me imagine an unfamiliar possibility. No literal hinge or door is required; neither does the metaphor prove that opportunities operate like physical doors in every respect. What carries across may be selected relationships or possibilities for action, not the whole model. A figurative phrase alone does not establish that a distinct internal Mental Model has been identified.

These are distinguishable operations: **generalizing the concept of Mental Models** makes the kind available for wider inquiry, whereas **reapplying a particular model** brings an existing understanding to bear on a further case. Neither requires unlimited transferability, and neither validates a new application without examining what fits.

### Attributing Models and Considering Other Standpoints

**Analogical Projection** also permits me to consider whether another person, animal, or possible mind operates through a Mental Model. This is different from applying *my own model of doors* to a new gate: I am now proposing something about how another mind may anticipate or interpret its world. Their speech, testimony, conduct, learning, physiology, and other evidence may support such attributions with differing degrees of confidence.

I may observe a person push an unfamiliar gate and infer that they expected it to open like a familiar door. I can also imagine how that gate appears from the standpoint of a child, a builder, or a person who has encountered a different kind of mechanism. **Perspective-Taking** changes what I am considering, but does not grant direct access to anyone else's experience. **Analogical Discernment** examines the support for a proposed correspondence and where the analogy ceases to fit. My representation of another person's model is not their model itself.

A team may speak of *our model* and may express shared understanding through maps, plans, instructions, or computer simulations. Under this definition, such external forms are **Artifice**, and the participating individuals may have overlapping but nonidentical Mental Models. A *group mental model* can describe distributed coordination or partially shared understanding without establishing one collective experiencing **Awareness** or one literally unified individual mind.

## Experiential Derivation: From What I Expect to **Mental Models**

The following passage invites examination from a possible first-person standpoint. It is a conceptual genealogy, not a claim that I originally learned the expression *mental model* in this order.

**1. Encountering expectation and response.** Within **My Awareness**, I notice that I sometimes anticipate what will happen. I reach for a familiar handle, recognize where an object is usually kept, imagine what someone might say, or expect that an action will produce an outcome. Sometimes my expectations are explicit thoughts. Sometimes I discover what I had anticipated only when events differ from it. I may encounter surprise, friction, disappointment, ease, or the adjustment of my response.

**2. Noticing recurrence and difference.** Across encounters, I recognize similarities: I tend to approach a kind of door in the same way, interpret a familiar expression similarly, or imagine the same sequence of consequences when making a decision. I also notice occasions when those patterns change. The experiences and responses are available to me in varying degrees; the processes producing them are not necessarily directly present as identifiable objects.

**3. Provisional first-person organization.** I propose that some recurring expectations and interpretations may reflect a distinguishable organization in my mind. I may describe one conversationally as *the way I expect this door to work* or *my model of this door*. This is a conceptual interpretation of the encountered pattern, not proof of a separately bounded internal thing. Other interpretations—including habit, associative learning, bodily skill, or overlapping processes—may remain plausible.

**4. Fiat Anchor and Experiential Generalization.** Through **Fiat Form**, I establish **Mental Models** as a named conceptual unity gathering such possible organizations. I choose what counts provisionally as a model and where to distinguish one pattern from another. Through **Experiential Generalization**, I extend the concept beyond my particular circumstances to investigate expectation and interpretation within **The Mind** more broadly. The name does not bring the underlying experiences or processes into existence.

**5. Applying a particular model beyond its original situation.** I approach a garden gate or a car trunk with expectations learned from doors, testing which relationships carry over and which do not. I may also imagine *opening a door to new opportunities*, carrying selected features into a metaphor rather than describing a literal door. I can use the same model to reconsider my own responses or imagine a situation from another standpoint. These applications may be deliberate or habitual; they do not require that the cases share an identical structure.

**6. Analogical Projection and Discernment.** I consider whether another person, animal, or possible mind operates through corresponding patterns. I may infer a model from what the other says or does, while remembering that the interpretation is mine and may not describe the other's organization exactly. Attributing a model to another mind differs from using my own model to interpret an external object. I examine the evidence and the limits of correspondence rather than assuming that every similar response has an identical cause.

**7. Returning to experience.** As I encounter more of what is, I may refine the proposed model, recognize another model, discover that an earlier distinction was misleading, or set the whole interpretation aside. The possibilities of correction remain open without requiring that every useful model be treated as suspect.

These movements can overlap and recur. **Epistemic Remembering** helps make visible how familiar explanations and model boundaries may once have been contingent interpretations. **My Self-Confession of Not-Knowing** permits inquiry without implying that I know nothing: I may know that a door opens by pulling while not knowing exactly how I learned to expect otherwise.

## Essential Distinctions

**Mental Models versus the concept of Mental Models.** The concept is a chosen grouping established through **Fiat Anchor** and **Fiat Form**. The mental organizations it proposes to investigate may arise without being deliberately chosen or named. Establishing the category does not establish a discrete internal object corresponding to every model we distinguish.

**Mental Models versus The Mind.** **The Mind** is the broader conceptual unity gathering mental activities, capacities, inferred processes, and possible relationships. **Mental Models** examines particular patterns through which expectations and interpretations take shape. It does not claim that all of The Mind is model-based.

**The Mind versus ordinary first-person description.** **The Mind** is the generalized conceptual unity. Expressions such as *my mind*, *the way I think*, or *the expectation I notice* locate an inquiry in first-person life without establishing another concept or Fiat Anchor. **Mental Models** names the generalized kind under investigation, not a collection of directly inspected private objects.

**Experienced expectation versus inferred organization.** I may consciously anticipate an outcome or discover my expectation through surprise. The organization proposed to explain that response may remain partly or wholly inferred. Neither the experience of expectation nor the inference alone reveals a model's complete mechanism.

**Model versus referent.** A model concerns some aspect of experience, reality, or possibility without being identical to that aspect. It may be accurate, incomplete, mistaken, deliberately hypothetical, or fictional in its content. The status of its referent differs from the status of the mental organization representing or anticipating it.

**Generalizing Mental Models versus transferring a particular model.** **Experiential Generalization** develops the concept from first-person recognition into a form applicable beyond that standpoint. Transferring a particular model applies or adapts an already available understanding to another situation, object, or possibility. The first does not require the second; neither by itself proves that the new application fits.

**Inward application versus outward application.** I may use a model to examine my own habits, reactions, and possible choices, or to anticipate a physical event, interpret another person, or explore an unfamiliar setting. *Inward* and *outward* describe directions of application, not separate kinds of Mental Model or claims about where a model physically resides.

**Applying a model to a referent versus attributing a model to a mind.** My model of how a garden gate works is my representation of the gate. My belief that someone else expects the gate to open by pushing is my representation of that person's possible Mental Model. Neither representation is the independent referent, and the second involves an additional inference about another mind.

**Literal application versus analogy and metaphor.** A model may guide interpretation of a similar mechanism, invite comparison with something importantly different, or supply relations for metaphorical exploration. *Opening new doors* can suggest access to opportunities without treating opportunities as physical doors. Selected correspondences do not establish complete structural identity or a newly discovered inner model.

**Perspective-Taking versus knowing another standpoint.** I may imagine how an event, object, or decision appears from another person's standpoint. This can change how I use or revise a model, but imagination does not become direct access to the other person's experience. Other evidence may nonetheless strongly justify particular attributions.

**Chosen separateness versus encountered regularity.** An investigator may delimit a model for explanation; a person may learn an expectation without deliberate choice; reality may present robust regularities. Neither the choice nor the regularity alone establishes that a model exists with precisely those boundaries.

**Implicit operation versus explicit representation.** An expectation may guide response without being articulated or noticed. Another model may be consciously constructed, rehearsed, or described. Making a model explicit can change it; a verbal explanation need not fully reproduce the organization previously at work.

**Modeling versus mere association or habit.** A habit or association may help account for a response without needing to be treated as a separately distinguishable Mental Model. Conversely, models may include learned, bodily, or habitual features. Whether the distinction improves understanding is part of the inquiry, not something settled by the name.

**Mental Models versus beliefs, concepts, and knowledge.** A model may organize several beliefs, deploy concepts, guide inquiry, or anticipate possibilities without establishing their truth. A single belief is not automatically a model; a model is not automatically knowledge. Nor does this definition insist that all **Knowing** occurs through Mental Models.

**Interpretation versus understanding of mechanism.** I may notice that someone interprets a situation consistently and reasonably propose an operative model. This does not show whether that pattern is encoded in language, memory, neural processes, bodily routines, or some combination of them.

**Modeling agency versus establishing agency.** A person may characterize a friend, institution, animal, or tendency within themselves as having intentions. That characterization can guide interpretation and action without proving every agency or boundary it depicts. Not every Mental Model concerns an apparently intentional entity.

**Individual models versus collective coordination.** This definition attributes Mental Models to individual minds. *group mental model* may denote partially shared, distributed, or coordinated understandings. It does not establish that a group has one literally unified mind or **Awareness**, and it does not settle wider questions about the possibility of collective minds.

**Mental Models versus external Artifice.** A diagram, scientific model, map, written explanation, or program may express and sustain aspects of an individual's understanding. It is not itself an individual Mental Model in this definition, though interacting with it may reshape Mental Models. An external representation and the mental activities associated with it remain distinguishable.

**Mental Models versus Blind Certainty and Closedness.** An implicit, incomplete, mistaken, or confidently held model is not automatically **Blind Certainty**. **Closedness** requires refusal to listen; **Blind Certainty** requires unawareness of such closedness. Mental Models can contribute to either condition, but they can also enable attentive, self-correcting inquiry.

**Correctness, completeness, and finality.** A Mental Model may be accurate within a scope, incomplete for another purpose, or mistaken about essential relationships. Purpose-relative sufficiency differs from unrestricted completeness. Its coherence or the user's confidence does not, by itself, settle its correctness or finality; neither does its possible incompleteness prove that complete understanding is impossible.

## Relationship to Openness

**G — Generative Openness.** **Mental Models** illustrates how **Experiential Derivation** and **Experiential Generalization** can develop a **Fiat Anchor** from first-person experience without claiming to create the experiences or operative patterns to which it refers. A particular model may then be adapted, transferred, or reimagined across referents and standpoints; that movement is distinct from establishing the general concept. **Anchored Ambiguity** preserves compatible possibilities for how models may be constituted. **Epistemic Remembering** recovers unnoticed choices in familiar model boundaries; **Analogical Projection** and **Analogical Discernment** examine new applications and attributed models.

**P — Philosophical Openness.** The concept belongs primarily to P, supporting inquiry into **The Mind**, **Knowing**, interpretation, justification, **Epistemic Groundedness**, **Artifice**, **Closedness**, and **Blind Certainty**. It makes a possible organization of understanding available for examination without requiring that organization to be fully understood. An interpretation may be useful and justified while its own mechanisms and boundaries remain partly unknown.

**S — Spiritual Openness.** Models may shape how I understand **My Self**, another person, a remembered relationship, a difficult feeling, or a contemplated way of living. A familiar model may be applied inwardly or considered from another standpoint through **Perspective-Taking**. Attending to these patterns need not reduce every inward experience to a model or require every preferred interpretation to be abandoned.

**T — Transcendent Openness.** Encounters with others and with **That Which Is Larger** may exceed familiar expectations and reveal the limits of a current model. **The Rendering of TWIL's Shadow** may be shaped by the interpretive patterns we bring to an encounter, without those patterns necessarily exhausting what has been encountered. Openness to further encounter allows a rendering or model to be considered, corrected, or reimagined without assuming either that it is the encounter itself or that every unfamiliar possibility is equally supported.

**Recursive Becoming.** As Mental Models change, it may not be only particular beliefs or expectations that change. A person may develop new ways to notice, imagine, interpret, decide, and learn. Such development can alter the possibilities through which further understanding becomes available.

## Naming and Usage

**Mental Models** — the canonical capitalized concept and **Fiat Anchor** gathering possible patterns of expectation, interpretation, and response within individual minds. The plural name identifies a conceptual kind; it does not establish the existence of a countable inventory of discrete internal objects.

**a Mental Model** — a provisionally distinguished possible instance of the kind under investigation: “A Mental Model of how a door opens may influence what I expect.” Such a pattern may be inferred, partly recognizable, habitual, or deliberately formulated. An individual model is not automatically an independent **Fiat Anchor**.

*my mind*, *the way I think*, *what I expected*, *a pattern in my responses* — ordinary first-person expressions for describing lived mental activity and its possible organization. They are not capitalized, independently stewarded concepts. When a formal generalized reference is needed, use **The Mind**; when the relationship with selfhood matters, **The Mind** as considered in relation to **My Self** is a possible construction. Prefer familiar wording when it expresses the distinction adequately.

*my model of how doors open*, *how I think the gate works*, *the expectation I brought to this conversation* — conversational ways to identify particular models or interpretations without assuming that I directly observed a distinct inner object.

*the model I attribute to her*, *how the situation might look from his standpoint*, *what the child may expect* — ordinary expressions for a proposed model in another mind or for **Perspective-Taking**. Such proposals are representations requiring appropriate **Analogical Discernment**, not direct observations of another person's mental organization.

*applying my door model to a gate*, *trying another perspective*, *opening new doors* — ways to describe transferring, adapting, or metaphorically extending a familiar model. A new application does not automatically reproduce the original model or prove that an additional discrete model exists.

*mental model*, *mental models* — ordinary lower-case usage when the technical concept is not being emphasized. Scientific and everyday uses may have different scopes; this definition does not overrule them.

*group mental model*, *our model* — ordinary contextual expressions for partially shared, distributed, or coordinated understanding among participants. Neither establishes a single collective **Awareness** or a separately defined Fiat Anchor here.

**“Mental Models”** — quotation marks indicate that the term itself, rather than the possible organizations it names, is under discussion.

Use **Mental Models** when the distinctions among *what is encountered*, *what organization is inferred*, *what conceptual grouping was chosen*, and *where a model is being applied* matter. Elsewhere, prefer the clearest conversational expression. Avoid using the concept as a universal explanation for unexplained behavior, or an analogy as proof of what it depicts.

## Examples

### Example — The Familiar Door (bright-line experiential derivation)

I push a familiar door each morning without giving the movement deliberate thought. One morning a similar-looking replacement door opens by pulling, and I push against it. I experience resistance and perhaps surprise.

My expectation may have operated without an explicit sentence in my thoughts. I can infer a pattern that made pushing seem appropriate and provisionally describe it as *my model of how this door opens*.

After encountering the new arrangement, I may reach differently next time. This is evidence that something in my expectation or response has changed, not proof that one isolated inner model was removed and another installed. A learned habit or embodied skill may also help explain what occurred.

### Example — From a Door to a Garden Gate and Car Trunk (outward application)

I know how the familiar door to my home opens. When I approach a garden gate, I may recognize an opening, a boundary, a handle, and a possible passage, then apply some of what I know about doors. The gate may require lifting a latch rather than turning a handle. I learn which expectations carry across and which require adjustment.

A car trunk presents another opening, but its release mechanism, hinges, motion, and safety constraints may differ further. My understanding of doors can suggest questions—*Where is the release? Which way does it move?*—without supplying a correct procedure in every case.

These applications do not show that a fixed inner object was copied unchanged. A familiar Mental Model may be extended, reorganized, or found inadequate as the referent changes. The gate and trunk are also not merely imaginary because my understanding of them involves an imaginal projection.

### Example — Opening New Doors (metaphor and alternate standpoints)

Someone describes a new opportunity as *opening a door*. I may imagine a threshold, entry, new space, obstacles, or possible paths beyond it. Relationships drawn from my understanding of doors make the opportunity more recognizable without implying that it has a physical handle or a single predetermined outcome.

I may then try another standpoint. For one person, the opportunity seems inviting; for another, the same threshold suggests uncertainty, cost, or risk. **Perspective-Taking** permits different interpretations of what passing through might mean. It does not require imagining that both people possess my exact model or that the metaphor is a literal account of opportunity.

A metaphor can be a meaningful use of a model even when no discrete new Mental Model has been established. Its value lies in the possible relationships and questions it makes available, not in a claim that doors and life choices share all their features.

### Example — Imagining a Room (partly explicit modeling)

Before moving a heavy table, I picture the room and mentally rehearse whether the table will pass through a doorway. I may imagine its width, the doorway, and the path of movement, then revise my plan.

Some relations in this model are present as images or thoughts. Others—such as a mistaken assumption about the doorway's width—may become recognizable only when the furniture does not fit. The room and doorway are distinct from my model of them; their actual arrangement can correct it.

### Example — Interpreting a Friend's Silence (interpretation and uncertainty)

A friend does not reply to my message. I immediately think they are upset with me.

I can notice the interpretation and ask whether an operative model of our relationship led me to expect that silence means displeasure. I may also consider distraction, illness, a lost message, or a wish for solitude.

The friend’s silence is encountered through its effects; the proposed explanation of their intentions is my interpretation. Recognizing an interpretive pattern does not establish what the friend is thinking, nor does it require that all interpretations deserve equal confidence.

### Example — A Seemingly Independent Part of the Mind (useful personification)

I notice that I often avoid difficult conversations and describe the recurring pattern as *the part of me that wants to hide*.

This personification may make a recurring orientation easier to notice and discuss. It is a Mental Model *of* an aspect of mental life, not evidence that a separate agent exists inside me. Its value may lie in helping me explore what the pattern anticipates, how it changes with circumstances, and what responses remain possible.

I can retain the useful metaphor without insisting that all avoidance has one cause or one internal boundary.

### Example — A Scientific Explanation (model and referent)

A student learns a model of the water cycle: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and the movement of water through different environments. The student may use it to anticipate rainfall or explain a familiar observation.

The student's understanding can be considered as involving Mental Models. A diagram in a textbook is external **Artifice** expressing some of the relationships under study; it is not identical to the student's mental organization or to the water cycle itself. An incomplete diagram can still communicate a well-supported scientific explanation within its scope.

### Example — A Collective Flood Model (shared Artifice, individual minds)

An emergency planning team creates a map of areas likely to flood during a storm. Different participants contribute measurements, assumptions, forecasts, and judgments. The map records a selected external representation of their work.

Each participant may retain different expectations, uncertainties, or alternative scenarios not visible in the map. The team may sensibly refer to *our flood model* without claiming that its members share one identical inner model or one experiencing subject.

New evidence may lead the team to revise both the map and their individual understandings. The model's usefulness does not imply unrestricted completeness.

### Example — Modeling Another Person's Model (Analogical Projection)

A teacher observes a student repeatedly subtracting a smaller number from a larger one, regardless of the order in which the numbers were originally written. The teacher proposes that the student may be using a particular mental rule.

That proposed rule is the teacher's representation of the student's possible Mental Model. It can guide further questions and instruction, but the repeated answers do not reveal the student's entire reasoning process. A conversation with the student may support, refine, or overturn the attribution.

### Example — A Misleading Attribution (negative boundary)

A person turns the same way each time a bell sounds. An observer confidently announces that the person possesses a detailed internal model of the bell's purpose.

The repeated response alone does not justify that conclusion. The person might be following a simple association, deliberate instruction, habit, or a richer understanding. **Mental Models** is available as a possible explanatory form, not as automatic proof of one preferred explanation.

## Boundary

**Mental Models** is a provisional way to recognize and investigate patterns of expectation and interpretation within **The Mind**. It does not establish that each such pattern is an isolable representation, that every mental activity is organized by a model, or that all knowing depends upon model-based processing.

The chosen anchor neither determines the physiological, computational, embodied, or other possible constitution of the organization being investigated nor excludes evidence for one explanation over another. Scientific inquiry may discover mechanisms, and theoretical differences may matter; the **Foundational Definition** does not settle those differences in advance.

The first-person derivation grounds the concept in encounterable effects, including expectation, surprise, imagination, and response. It does not claim direct observation of every model or an exact reconstruction of how the word and concept arose historically. The recognition of a pattern may be strongly supported even where its mechanisms and boundaries remain unresolved.

This definition ordinarily attributes Mental Models to individual minds, while allowing analogical and collective uses to be considered explicitly. It does not settle which beings or systems have minds, whether collective **Awareness** is possible, or whether external artifacts can constitute aspects of cognition under some broader theory. An application to a garden gate or a metaphorical door is not an attribution of a mind to the gate or the opportunity. Attributing a Mental Model to another mind requires separate grounds.

An incomplete model need not be mistaken; an accurate model need not be final; an unrecognized model need not involve **Closedness**. A particular model may be fruitfully reapplied, but not every model transfers and not every metaphor supplies a distinct mental organization. Similarities across referents or standpoints do not prove identical causes, structures, or experiences. Evidence can support confident judgments and practical action even while the organization through which they arise remains open to further examination.

## Summary

**Mental Models** names a chosen conceptual unity for investigating possible patterns of expectation, interpretation, and response within **The Mind**. Its experiential beginning lies not in directly discovering little objects called *models*, but in encountering anticipation, surprise, imagination, interpretation, response, and change—and considering what organization may help explain them. From that first-person beginning, the concept is generalized; particular models may then be applied inwardly and outwardly, adapted to unfamiliar circumstances, considered from other standpoints, or extended through analogy and metaphor.

**The concept is anchored; the patterns it names are investigated. A model may travel beyond its original encounter without becoming its referent—or determining where reality must draw its boundaries.**
