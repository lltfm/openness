# Real and Reality

## Foundational Definition

**Real** and **Reality** name what is present, occurring, or existing and the corresponding world or field of what is understood to be so. In ordinary shared discourse, unqualified *real* and *reality* commonly present an **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real/reality**: a supposedly standpoint-independent world rendered through perspectives and accounts shared among persons, usually without displaying those perspectives as part of the claim. **Perspectival Dimensionalization** retains this ordinary use while making its ordinarily hidden first-person, attributed-other, imaginal-objective, and intersubjective dimensions available for explicit examination. In Openness definitions, these dimensions are named wherever a shift of standpoint is invited, so the reader can notice and choose whether to enter the offered perspective.

## The Definition Unfolded

The ordinary sentence *“The train has arrived”* often sounds like a direct statement about *reality*. It can be perfectly useful and may be well supported. Yet, for a person reading it, the sentence is encountered within that person's own experiencing. From there, its language invites a view of a train and a world as though available independently of any reader, writer, or witness. This invitation may be accepted automatically, without noticing a change of standpoint.

Openness proposes that **two modes of orientation are available throughout human encounters with accounts of reality**: **a first-person subjective-reality** and **an imaginal objective-reality**. The first is the standpoint from which experiencing becomes present; the second is a representational standpoint from which persons, things, events, and even others' inner lives are described as if surveyed independently of that first person. This is a conceptual dimensionalization offered for first-person examination, **not a theory that human beings inhabit only two kinds of existence** or a claim to have directly inspected every person's inner history. Additional distinctions refine the imaginal mode: a particular other person's represented standpoint and the objective account coordinated through [**The Intersubjective**](defining_the_intersubjective.md).

The same human capacity to imagine a viewpoint not directly occupied can participate in reading nonfiction, following third-person descriptions, attributing another's [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) or [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md), reading fiction, and entering a film's depicted world. **The use of an imaginal standpoint occurs in both factual and fictional discourse; their evidential status may differ greatly.** The process need not be noticed to occur, and the person offering the perspective may not notice it either.

This is a **dimensionalization of familiar reality-talk**, not a proposal to abandon everyday language or pronounce the world fictional. A well-tested account of a stone and an invented account of a dragon can both be presented from an imaginal objective standpoint. Their agreement with independent evidence, relationship to personal experience, and intended domains differ. The term *imaginal* identifies how the standpoint is *taken up*, not a special permission to decide the referent's nature in advance.

In Openness definitions, a consequential shift from a reader's first-person beginning into an attributed or objective standpoint is **disclosed as an invitation rather than silently installed as a prerequisite for understanding**. A reader may enter it, explore it provisionally, return to the first-person beginning, challenge it, or decline it entirely—even when an intersubjective account is strongly justified. The choice to withhold assent need not alter the external events or other people's conclusions described by that account. It remains the reader's choice of what to recognize, inhabit, or affirm within their own inquiry. This power has possible costs and consequences; those warrant separate ethical reflection, not an automatic duty to accept the offered viewpoint.

## Named Perspectives and Internal Terms

### My-real and my-reality

*my-real* concerns what is present, occurring, or recognized **from the first-person standpoint I presently call my own**. *my-reality* names the corresponding field of experiencing, understanding, remembering, perceiving, dreaming, or imagining available from that standpoint. **my-subjective-reality** is its longer synonym when explicit emphasis on subjectivity improves precision.

The first-person beginning is [**My Awareness**](../../generative/core/the_seed/definitions/defining_my_awareness.md), understood in relation to [**My Self**](../../generative/core/the_seed/definitions/defining_my_self.md). If I dream of a mountain, the dream experience is present in *my-reality*; whether a corresponding mountain exists physically is a separate question. If I read a friend's message, the reading and my interpretation occur in *my-reality*; the friend's experiencing does not thereby become directly accessible to me.

*my-real* is not a claim that every appearance is accurate, every imagined object exists independently, or the whole world is made by my imagination. It identifies **the standpoint from which a claim can first be examined**, including when substantial evidence warrants believing that something beyond that standpoint exists.

### Self-real and self-reality

*self-real* names what is present, occurring, or recognized **for a particular Self from that Self's own standpoint**, without fixing the reference to the present speaker. *self-reality* names that Self's corresponding first-person field. Their longer, more explicit synonyms are **subjectively-real** and **subjective-reality**.

These expressions generalize the kind of standpoint expressed by *my-reality*. They do not merge the perspectives of different Selves. For any proposed Self, the claim that something is *self-real* must still distinguish **the experiencing attributed to that Self** from **another observer's representation of it**. The terms describe a possible first-person relationship; they do not alone establish that every proposed Self has [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md).

For example, a friend's pain may be *subjectively-real* for the friend. Another person may have compelling reasons to believe that pain is present through testimony, behavior, and medical evidence, even though it is not directly experienced from the observer's standpoint.

### Their-real and their-reality

*their-real* concerns what is **attributed as present or experienced from another proposed Self's standpoint**. *their-reality* concerns the corresponding world or field **as attributed, imagined, or inferred by someone who does not occupy that standpoint**. **imaginal-other-reality** is a precision-bearing synonym emphasizing that *the other person's perspective is being represented*, not directly entered. These expressions are useful when an account shifts from *my-reality* toward a particular other's *self-reality*.

The attribution may be well supported, doubtful, fictional, or still unresolved. **The representation of their standpoint is imaginal; the other Self and their possible experiencing are not therefore imaginary.** An observer can have strong evidence that another person is frightened while remaining uncertain about exactly how that fear feels or what caused it.

The word *their* need not imply several people: it may refer to a single person of unspecified gender, to a known individual, or to multiple proposed Selves. When several people are involved, distinguish each person's **self-reality**, a speaker's **imaginal-other-reality** for them, and an attributed group-level pattern. One may apply [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) to a group in a distributed or functional sense; that does not silently establish one experiencing [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md) shared by its members.

### Objectively-real and objective-reality

*objectively-real* expresses a claim **as though its subject could be described independently of any particular Self's standpoint**. *objective-reality* names the corresponding proposed account or domain. The precision-bearing longer forms are *imaginal-objectively-real* and *the-imaginal-objective-reality*: they reveal that the seemingly unlocated viewing standpoint is constructed imaginally, whether by an individual or through shared inquiry. **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real** and **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality** explicitly add the layer of intersubjective formation, transmission, contest, and possible agreement that common-language *real/reality* typically leaves implicit.

An objective-style account can be deliberately constructed from observation, instruments, measurements, testimony, reasoning, and shared tests. Such accounts can have extraordinary evidential support. The qualifier *imaginal* draws attention to the **represented viewpoint**: an account is framed *as if seen from nowhere in particular*, even though creating, testing, reading, and interpreting it occur through situated activity.

A stone can be described as *objectively-real* in an account supported by repeated measurement and independent witnesses. A dragon in a novel can also be narrated from an objective-style viewpoint. **In both, the viewpoint is imaginal; what differs is the account's asserted domain, grounds, and potential relation to independently existing referents.** A reader may recognize strong evidence for the stone without surrendering the freedom to question or decline the perspective in which the evidence is presented.

In common discourse, *real* and *reality* ordinarily function as shorthand for **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real/reality** when speaking of the seemingly shared world. Openness does not prohibit that shorthand or require the speaker to disclaim it in everyday life. Its longer form makes visible that the presented objectivity has been developed and passed between situated persons. The shorter *objectively-real/objective-reality* may identify an individual objective-style account before specifying whether it has become intersubjectively shared. Neither shared acceptance nor refusal by an individual alone decides the accuracy of an account.

### Common Reality and Imaginal Intersubjective Objective Reality

*Real* and *reality* in ordinary language frequently refer to a world presented as **already there for everyone**, available to be described without naming who is doing the describing. Openness provisionally recognizes this conventional orientation as *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real/reality*: **imaginal** because its perspective is constructed, **intersubjective** because shared language, practices, testimony, and inquiry sustain and circulate its account, and **objective** because it describes its referents as if independent of any particular first-person standpoint.

This does **not** define all ordinary uses of *real*. Someone saying “my pain is real” may be affirming first-person experiencing; a child may say a character felt real within a dream. The longer form names the important default of public *reality-talk* when a shared objective world is being assumed or asserted. It is a perspective qualification, not a claim that consensus creates every object, or that consensus is needed before a claim may be meaningful.

An author or speaker may present an *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* as a confident fact, a provisional reconstruction, a disputed consensus, a socially constituted institution, or an imaginative invention. **A reader's assent and a claim's justification are separate dimensions.** Even an account with substantial independent support can be declined as a perspective the reader chooses to inhabit. That refusal may carry personal and social consequences; epistemic sovereignty is not a guarantee that the refusal is correct, harmless, or without practical effects.

### Perspectival Dimensionalization and Perspective Noticing

**Perspectival Dimensionalization** names the analytic move of making the usually implicit epistemic dimensions of a statement visible: **(1) the first-person encounter, (2) the represented or invited standpoint, (3) whose experience, intention, or world is being attributed, (4) how the claim is grounded, and (5) whether the reader chooses to inhabit or assent to that perspective**. The move is an *internal descriptive practice of this definition*, not yet an independently reified [**Generative Method**](../../generative/definitions/defining_generative_methods.md).

**Perspective Noticing** is a useful provisional shorter description of the moment a reader recognizes the transition. **Experiential Derivation** reconstructs how a concept can be grounded in recognizable experiencing; **Perspectival Dimensionalization** reconstructs the standpoint movements and attribution embedded in an already expressed account. They can work together but are not identical. One need not prove the psychological history of each automatic mental movement to make the current representational layers inspectable.

The practical inquiry is: *What have I directly encountered? What perspective am I being invited to adopt? What has been supplied by inference, imagination, narration, or testimony? Who is asking me to accept it? Do I wish to participate?* The inquiry may be quick and nonverbal, careful and analytical, or developed over many pages. Making the choice visible serves the reader, not an obligation to reject the offered view.

### Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective

An **Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective** is the apparently unlocated viewpoint made available by narrative, rhetoric, image, or film when the reader or viewer is invited to see persons, events, inner lives, or an entire world **without explicit acknowledgement of how their own first-person perspective is being shifted into the presented representation**. The reader may experience the shift effortlessly. The designation concerns the *structure of the invitation*, not an assertion that every narrator intends to deceive.

A factual sentence such as “Mira secretly feared the storm” and a fictional narrator saying those exact words can use the **same human capacity for imaginative perspective-taking**. A film may align a camera with a character's eyes or show a supposedly neutral overhead scene. A scientific paragraph may describe distant objects from an impersonal vantage. The truth status, conventions, and reasons for adopting these views vary, while their potential to shift a reader into an imaginal standpoint remains available for examination.

What is *unbound* is **the presented viewpoint's apparent freedom from a located experiencing reader**, not the physical reader's actual removal from first-person experiencing. A reader can be carried from the visible page or screen into a scene as if directly seeing it, sometimes without registering that the scene, its framing, or its attributed interiority has been supplied by someone else.

### Imposed Perspective

**Imposed Perspective** names the attempt—or the textual or social effect—of presenting an offered viewpoint as **one the recipient is already presumed to share, or is expected to accept without recognizing the opportunity to decline**. It can involve unlocated narration, institutional authority, assumed consensus, emotional framing, social pressure, or a speaker's insistence that an interpretation is simply “reality.” It may be deliberate manipulation, unexamined habit, or an ordinary convention used without awareness of its effect; intent and ethics require separate examination.

An **Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective** describes an *apparently disembodied vantage* offered to the recipient. **Imposed Perspective** describes the *concealment or foreclosure of the recipient's choice* around such an offer. The two may coincide, but neither entails the other: a transparent fictional narrator may invite an unbound view without attempting to compel belief, while a speaker can impose a standpoint directly through “People like us all know ...” without a disembodied narrator.

Noticing an imposition is not the same as proving the claim false or showing that the source intended deceit. A reader may identify the invitation, remain within *my-reality*, examine its grounds, accept it, or refuse it. Such refusal may concern the perspective itself even when its content is strongly corroborated. **The reader's permission is conceptually different from the account's evidential strength.**

## Standpoint Visibility and The Intersubjective

[**The Intersubjective**](defining_the_intersubjective.md) concerns relationships through which differently situated Selves communicate, interpret, negotiate, justify, and sometimes constitute shared social facts. Language carries one person's representation into another's experience; it cannot simply transport one first-person standpoint into another.

Shared descriptions of *reality* commonly circulate as **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality**: persons coordinate an objective-style account through language, testimony, practice, measurement, and dissent, while each encounters that account from a situated standpoint. The account may be strongly justified, mistaken, or contested. A community's agreement may also constitute an institution or convention. The recipient can notice the offer without pretending to become a disembodied spectator, and can withhold participation even where the group's grounds are substantial. An author may identify the **basis and standpoint of the account** once for a coherent passage rather than reiterating every qualification in every sentence.

## Experiential Derivation: From First-Person Presence to Represented Standpoints

The following passage invites examination of one possible first-person conceptual genealogy. It is not autobiographical testimony by the AI author or a proposed universal chronology of how language is learned.

**1. Recognizing what is present.** I feel the weight of a cup and hear a voice. The feeling and hearing are recognizable within [**My Awareness**](../../generative/core/the_seed/definitions/defining_my_awareness.md). I call this beginning *my-reality*, without claiming that my immediate interpretation exhausts the cup, the voice, or their sources.

**2. Considering another standpoint.** I see a friend lift the same cup and hear them describe its warmth. I can recognize that I do not experience their warmth directly. I may speak of the warmth as *their-real*—an attribution to *their-reality*—and consider the friend's own *self-reality*. The testimony and shared bodily circumstances may make the attribution highly credible.

**3. Forming an objective-style account.** I describe the cup as having a measured temperature and an observable location. I imagine an account not tied to which person holds it: *objective-reality*. The evidence may be robust, but the perspective I use to assemble the account is still a representation made and interpreted from situated experience.

**4. Recognizing shared objectivity.** I read a transit board's schedule, see a platform announcement, and hear other travelers treat the train's arrival as a settled public fact. I can recognize an *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* being offered through these shared records, reports, and practices. I may find the account compelling without losing my first-person standpoint or becoming obliged to assent.

**5. Noticing a shift.** I read, “The friend wanted to leave.” I may initially receive the sentence as if their desire were simply visible. When I notice my shift into the narration's perspective, I can ask whether it rests on testimony, inference, fictional convention, or something else.

**6. Choosing how to continue.** I may accept the perspective for the purpose of a story, provisionally accept a well-supported attribution, ask for evidence, or withhold judgment. Noticing how the perspective was offered helps preserve my freedom to decide how to inhabit it.

These movements distinguish experienced presence, attribution of another standpoint, individually modeled objectivity, intersubjectively shared objectivity, and the reader's possible response. They offer an inspectable conceptual reconstruction, not a claim that these steps always happen consciously or in this order. The reader's ability to refuse an imaginal account is distinct from deciding what physical or social circumstances actually obtain.

## Essential Distinctions

**My-reality versus self-reality.** *my-reality* identifies the present speaker's first-person beginning; *self-reality* is the generalized form for any specified Self's own standpoint. The terms are related, not interchangeable in every sentence.

**Self-reality versus their-reality.** The former concerns a Self's own possible experiencing; the latter makes visible that **someone else is attributing or representing that standpoint**. An attribution may correspond accurately to another person's experience without becoming direct access to it.

**Common reality versus perspectivally dimensionalized reality.** Ordinary *real/reality* often carries an unmarked, intersubjectively transmitted objective standpoint. Openness retains the words and makes that standpoint explicit when definitional precision matters. The longer form supplements common language without declaring its ordinary usage forbidden.

**Individual imaginal objectivity versus intersubjectively shared imaginal objectivity.** One person may form an objective-style narrative without consultation. Its adoption, correction, or contestation through other Selves develops the intersubjective dimension; neither repetition nor popularity guarantees truth.

**Perspective invitation versus perspective assent.** Reading, listening, imagining, and understanding what a claim proposes can occur without accepting its purported perspective as one's own. A reader can decline participation in a strongly justified collective account. The grounds for the account and the reader's freedom to choose are different questions.

**Unbound versus imposed perspective.** An unbound narration offers a viewpoint with no visible first-person location. An imposed perspective presents acceptance as assumed, compulsory, or already achieved. Either can be made visible without assuming deceptive intent; propaganda may deliberately exploit both.

**Their-reality versus objective-reality.** The former locates an account in an attributed particular standpoint; the latter deliberately describes what would hold independently of particular standpoints. Both can be formed imaginally and evaluated through evidence, but they do different conceptual work.

**Experiencing versus imagining.** Imagination is among the activities that can become present in experience; **experiencing as such is not equated with imagining a fiction**. A person may encounter evidence, experience emotions, imagine another's standpoint, and infer independent causes within one unfolding inquiry.

**Imaginal standpoint versus imaginary referent.** A perspective may be represented in imagination while its proposed referent exists independently. A person, a stone, an institution, or another being need not be fictional merely because it is described through an imaginal projection.

**First-person presence versus infallible interpretation.** The presence of experiencing can be recognizable even when an interpretation of its content, origin, apparent object, or causal explanation is mistaken.

**Objective aspiration versus objective possession.** Deliberately correcting for bias and comparing evidence across observers may yield dependable knowledge. Such success does not require a person literally to occupy a view outside all experience.

**Physical existence versus perspective-qualified reality.** The claim that an object exists physically is substantive and investigable. Calling something *real* from a specified standpoint does not by itself establish or deny such existence.

**Fictional frame versus deception.** A story's omniscient perspective may be an openly understood artistic convention. A factual report using the same technique without indicating its evidential basis can raise different questions. A reader who knowingly enters fiction has not thereby confused fictional content with a physical event.

**Intersubjective recognition versus factual justification.** Shared conventions may constitute roles, names, or institutional commitments. Shared opinion alone does not settle every claim about physical referents or other people's experiencing.

**Physical objects versus immaterial social forms.** A physical participant, page, sign, or computer can be investigated as a physical referent. A team identity such as *Team A* may be an immaterial [**Fiat Form**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_fiat_form.md) constituted through social practices. Its reality and consequences need not be denied merely because it is not an additional physical body.

**Required disclosure in definitions versus optional disclosure elsewhere.** Openness definitions must reveal a consequential move from the reader's first-person standpoint to another person's attributed or an imaginal objective perspective **before treating that move as shared or already accepted**. A brief framing sentence can disclose a continuous example or passage; constant mechanical repetition is unnecessary. In other Openness works disclosure remains optional, while especially useful where persuasion, propaganda, contested claims, group assent, or attributed inner life could hide the move. In all genres the reader remains free to decline the invited perspective.

**Deliberate Precise Ambiguity versus accidental equivocation.** Quotation marks around “real” or “reality” may invite inquiry into the standpoint and domain intentionally left unspecified. An unmarked change in perspective that affects the truth or meaning of an assertion calls for clarification rather than celebration as ambiguity.

## Ethical Orientation and the Reader's Sovereignty

**The capability is ethically neutral.** Noticing or tracing a shift of perspective tells a reader *how* a viewpoint is offered; it does not, by itself, determine whether the presentation is good or bad, true or false, responsible or manipulative. A film, scientific report, classroom explanation, persuasive essay, or propaganda work may all recruit the same imaginative capacity.

**Openness definitions require standpoint disclosure** whenever they shift the reader into an attributed other's perspective or an imaginal objective account. The disclosure can be brief and readable: *“From the speaker's attributed perspective ...”*, *“In the imaginal objective account used here ...”*, or *“The following fictional scene invites the reader to see ...”*. The reader is invited to inhabit or reject the frame; the author must not silently presume the reader already stands within it. Across other Openness works, explicit disclosure is optional, and especially recommended where the omitted standpoint would materially influence assent or hide an inference.

**The reader's perspective remains their own.** They can accept, provisionally try on, dispute, or reject an offered interpretation, including a well-evidenced *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality*. Openness does not convert evidential strength into a duty to inwardly assent. The same sovereign choice has consequences: refusing a warranted account may expose the chooser or others to avoidable errors, while accepting an unwarranted one may enable manipulation. Those possible consequences invite [**Discernment**](defining_discernment.md), **Nonimposition**, and eventual more developed ethics, not the surrender of the initial freedom.

**Rhetorical capture is worth noticing.** Repeated images, selectively edited footage, authoritative pronouns such as *we* and *everyone*, implied moral disqualification of dissent, and apparently omniscient narration can invite an objective vantage and then treat the reader's adoption as a foregone conclusion. Naming the transition can help defuse the attempted capture even before the factual dispute has been settled. This is a *possibility for examination*, not an allegation that every persuasive work is intentionally manipulative.

## Relationship to Openness

**G — Generative Openness.** [**Fiat Form**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_fiat_form.md) and [**Fiat Anchor**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_fiat_anchor.md) make the formation of conceptual perspectives visible. [**Analogical Projection**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_analogical_projection.md) helps imagine possible standpoints; [**Experiential Generalization**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_experiential_generalization.md) can develop *self-reality* from a first-person beginning. [**Resonant Questions**](../../generative/definitions/defining_resonant_questions.md) may reveal a perspective shift without obliging the reader to adopt a particular answer.

**P — Philosophical Openness.** The primary home of these terms. They support [**Self-Honesty**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_self_honesty.md), [**Discernment**](defining_discernment.md), [**Epistemic Groundedness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_epistemic_groundedness.md), and inquiry into [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md), [**The Self**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_the_self.md), [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md), and [**The Intersubjective**](defining_the_intersubjective.md). They make the source, standpoint, and grounds of a reality claim examinable.

**S — Spiritual Openness.** Dreams, contemplation, felt presence, relationships, and unfamiliar experiences can be explored without prematurely identifying an experience with a final account of its cause or ultimate nature. Physical, spiritual, and other interpretations remain open to [**Epistemic Hospitality**](../../generative/definitions/defining_epistemic_hospitality.md) and evidence-sensitive inquiry.

**T — Transcendent Openness.** Encounters with [**That Which Is Larger**](../../transcendent/core/defining_that_which_is_larger.md) and possibilities arising between Selves may invite shifts in perspective beyond one's present grasp. Recognizing a shift helps maintain fidelity to the encounter while allowing unfamiliar interpretations to be contemplated.

## Naming and Usage

The following expressions are **perspective qualifications**, not four independent metaphysical realms. Their lowercase, hyphenated spellings are deliberate and do not create additional separately stewarded Core concepts.

**my-real / my-reality** — the explicitly first-person use. **my-subjective-reality** is an equivalent longer form, preferred when the subjective standpoint needs emphasis: “The grief I feel is present in *my-subjective-reality*.”

**self-real / self-reality** — the generalized form for what is present from some specified Self's own standpoint. **subjectively-real / subjective-reality** are equivalent longer forms: “Pain may be *subjectively-real* for a patient even when a clinician cannot feel it.”

**their-real / their-reality** — the standpoint of another proposed Self **as attributed by a speaker, narrator, reader, or observer**. **imaginal-other-reality** is an alternative when the represented character of that other standpoint should be unmissable: “My *imaginal-other-reality* for my friend is a representation, not their experience made mine.” Where *their* refers to several people, specify whether the claim concerns each person's own experience or a claimed group-level pattern.

**objectively-real / objective-reality** — an account presented *as if independent of particular Selves' perspectives*. **imaginal-objectively-real / the-imaginal-objective-reality** make the individually constructed viewing standpoint explicit. **imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real / imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality** make the additional layer of socially circulated, negotiated, or corroborated objectivity explicit. For ordinary public-world claims, unqualified *real/reality* typically abbreviate the last pair. “I can examine a widely shared account of the bridge as *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* without presuming that I literally observe it from nowhere, or that I have agreed to inhabit that viewpoint.”

**Real / Reality** — canonical paired terms for ordinary reality-talk and its **Perspectival Dimensionalization**. An Openness definition identifies a reader-displacing perspective shift and gives the reader room to remain in their first-person standpoint. Ordinary lowercase *real/reality* retain their everyday public-world use (typically the shared imaginal objective account) in other Openness works, with greater precision optional.

**“real” / “reality”** — quotation-marked forms may expressly suspend selection of a standpoint for a [**Precise Ambiguity**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_precise_ambiguity.md), but quotation marks also serve ordinary quoting functions and must not automatically be interpreted as a technical marker.

**Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective** — an apparently unlocated vantage offered to a reader or viewer. **Imposed Perspective** — an offer that treats the recipient's adoption as presumed, compulsory, or already achieved. **Perspectival Dimensionalization** — making the standpoint, attribution, grounds, and reader choice visible. **Perspective Noticing** — provisional shorter descriptive expression for recognizing the shift. These are internal distinctions and a writing practice, not new independently stewarded Methods.

**Earlier terminology.** The previous *Own Self Real / Own Self Reality* corresponds most directly to *my-real / my-reality* when the speaker's first-person beginning is intended, or to *self-real / self-reality* when the first-person pattern is generalized. *Imaginal Other Self Real / Reality* is now expressed as *their-real / their-reality* where the other standpoint is attributed; *Imaginal Objective Real / Reality* corresponds to the precision-bearing longer objective forms. These are editorial equivalences of intended **perspective roles**, not claims that the old expressions always had identical scope.

For precision, a passage can identify **what is encountered**, **whose perspective is represented**, **what is attributed beyond that encounter**, **what supports the attribution**, and **whether the reader is being invited to inhabit the account**. In an Openness definition, disclose each consequential transition before the offered perspective is treated as already shared; a single clear opening can frame several ensuing sentences. Other works may rely on context when more explicit marking would not serve their purpose.

## Examples

The first-person passages below invite recognition of possible perspective shifts; they are illustrations for readers to test, not autobiographical testimony from an AI author.

### Example — “She Is Angry” (first-person reader standpoint)

I read: “Mira closes the door. She is angry.” The sentence offers a third-person view, and I may imagine both the door closing and Mira's anger as though the latter were directly available. What I encounter in *my-reality* is the text and my reading of it. What is attributed to Mira belongs to *their-reality* as represented by the narrator.

If this is a novel with an openly omniscient narrator, I may willingly inhabit the offered fictional perspective. If it is a factual account, I can ask: Did Mira say she was angry? Did someone infer it from her behavior? Was the claim corroborated? Both forms of writing can be meaningful, but they invite different kinds of assent.

### Example — A Friend's Inner Life (first-person, strongly supported attribution)

I hear a friend say, “I'm anxious about tomorrow,” and see them repeatedly checking the time. I may reasonably conclude that anxiety is *subjectively-real* for them. My experience of their words and actions is part of *my-reality*; my attribution concerns *their-reality*. The fact that I use imagination and inference to understand them does not make their anxiety fictional or my confidence arbitrary.

I can also notice that my imagined account of exactly *why* they are anxious may outrun what their testimony establishes. Recognizing this difference restores space for listening instead of silently treating my interpretation as their experience.

### Example — Watching a Film (first-person viewer standpoint)

I watch a film in which a character sees a threatening figure. The images, sound, anticipation, and fear I experience are present in *my-reality*. Editing invites me to see from the character's position and perhaps to interpret the film as revealing their inner life. I may knowingly enter *their-reality* **as fictional representation** for the duration of the scene.

When a film uses a seemingly objective camera angle, I may also experience an **Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective**. I can enjoy the scene while recognizing that its camera and narration are ways of presenting a world, not positions I literally occupy outside my own experiencing.

### Example — Attributing The Mind (first-person standpoint)

I speak with a friend who remembers a shared event, explains their plans, and responds to my questions. I can form an imaginal representation of [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) as applied to my friend: a possible organization of thought, memory, emotion, and other capacities. Their words, conduct, and the broad evidence concerning other humans can make that attribution strongly justified.

My friend may have [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md) whose contents remain their own. My *imaginal-other-reality*—the account I form of their experiencing—may correspond closely in some respects and be mistaken in others. **I am recognizing the attribution as mine**, even when its grounds are excellent. That recognition also leaves me free to revise, accept, or refuse the perspective I have imagined.

### Example — The Stone and the Measurement (first-person, evidenced physical referent)

I hold a stone and feel its weight. That encounter belongs to *my-reality*. A companion describes the same stone from their position; I consider *their-reality* without directly entering it. Repeated measurements and corroboration may strongly justify calling the stone *objectively-real* as a physical object.

When my neighbor and I compare measurements and consult published geological descriptions, we also participate in an *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* of the stone. The account can have excellent grounds; I can nevertheless recognize its borrowed objective vantage as a perspective I am invited to enter, rather than a vantage from which anyone literally sees everything.

### Example — A Mountain in a Dream (first-person standpoint)

I dream of standing before a mountain. The mountain is present as part of the dream I experience; the experience belongs to *my-reality*. On waking, I may distinguish this encounter from a mountain believed to exist physically. I can also contemplate further theories of dreaming and embodiment without treating the immediate dream appearance as proof of any one theory.

### Example — A Medical Account (first-person reader standpoint)

I read in a medical report: “The patient reported severe pain.” This third-person wording identifies testimony rather than pretending that a clinician directly felt the patient's pain. The report can carry strong grounds for attributing pain in the patient's *self-reality* while preserving the difference between **a report, an interpretation, and a firsthand experience**. Third-person prose becomes more precise without abandoning its usefulness.

### Example — A Team and Its Members (first-person inquiry)

I read: “The team believed the plan would work.” I can inquire whether every member expressed the belief, a majority voted, a spokesperson represented an institutional commitment, or the author inferred a group attitude. The team may use shared records and collective processes that justify applying [**The Mind**](defining_the_mind.md) to it in a distributed or functional sense. Its recorded decision may be part of an *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* as a social fact. The statement alone does not establish one experiencing [**Awareness**](../../generative/core/definitions/defining_awareness.md) shared across members, although inquiry into collective experiencing remains open.

### Example — A Reader Declines a Perspective (first-person standpoint)

I encounter an essay declaring, “Everyone knows this policy will fail.” I recognize that its apparent universal vantage may be inviting me into an **Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective**. I can continue reading without adopting that vantage. I may ask whose knowledge is being represented, what evidence supports it, and whether the assertion is rhetorical. Noticing the invitation gives me a clearer opportunity to choose how to engage.

### Example — A Persuasive Video Makes a Crowd Speak (first-person viewer standpoint)

I watch a short campaign-style video about a city park renovation. It shows three residents applauding, cuts to a full-looking meeting room, and plays triumphant music as a narrator announces, “The neighborhood has spoken: everyone wants this plan.” For a moment I feel as though I am looking down upon *the entire neighborhood's judgment*, rather than watching a producer's selection of scenes.

Through **Perspective Noticing**, I return to *my-reality*: I saw edited footage and heard a claim. I was invited into an **Unbound Reader/Viewer Perspective** that made a contested collective judgment seem directly present. The assertion that *everyone* agrees also risks an **Imposed Perspective**, because I am positioned as someone who ought already to share the supposed consensus. I may accept that the applause happened while asking how many residents were surveyed, who opposed the plan, what footage was omitted, and whether the narrator's summary is justified. **I can keep watching without giving my assent to its imagined unanimity.**

### Example — Moral Pressure in a Fundraising Appeal (first-person reader standpoint)

I receive a fundraising message showing a distressed child beside the words “Compassionate people give immediately. Anyone asking questions is turning away.” I experience concern, urgency, and perhaps guilt. The message then invites me to see a world in which the organization's claims and my moral character have already been judged from above.

I can recognize what happened in *my-reality*: I read an appeal and felt an emotional response. I was offered an *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-reality* in which a proposed emergency, a particular solution, and an alleged moral consensus are treated as settled. Its **Imposed Perspective** tries to make withholding assent appear equivalent to lacking compassion. I remain free to care about the child, investigate the organization's evidence and effectiveness, give elsewhere, donate now, or decline entirely. Recognizing the perspective shift does not require suppressing sympathy; it restores a choice over what that sympathy is being asked to endorse.

## Boundary

**Real and Reality** retain ordinary language for the shared imaginal objective world while making the shift into that account **visible and optional for the reader** in formal Openness definitions. Their dimensionalized forms remain available for physical, social, psychological, spiritual, fictional, and other contexts, with the standpoint and grounds disclosed when those distinctions matter.

Accounts of independent physical objects, genuine relationships, shared social institutions, dreams, fictional worlds, and possible unfamiliar forms of existence may all be investigated. An imaginal objective standpoint can support reliable inquiry, but its familiar convenience cannot silently become a claim to occupy the reader's mind. [**Discernment**](defining_discernment.md) assesses claims and consequences; the reader's freedom to accept, suspend, or refuse the offered perspective remains a distinct commitment.

A perspective shift may be useful, generous, beautiful, and well supported. Its risk emerges when an important shift becomes **invisible**, so that an attribution is mistaken for direct encounter or an imaginal standpoint is mistaken for an actually occupied one.

## Summary

*my-reality* names the first-person beginning; *self-reality* generalizes that sort of standpoint; *their-reality* (or *imaginal-other-reality*) makes an attributed other standpoint visible. *objective-reality* names the imaginal objective vantage, while ordinary public *real/reality* commonly carry its socially circulated form: *imaginal-intersubjective-objective-real/reality*. **Perspectival Dimensionalization** makes the movement among these standpoints inspectable and restores the reader's option to participate.

The practical questions become: **“What is real here, from whose standpoint, through whose representation, and on what grounds?”** And, just as importantly: **“Am I being invited into another's perspective—and do I choose to enter it?”**
