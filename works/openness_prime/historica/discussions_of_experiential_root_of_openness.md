# Openness as a different way of knowing

I think you are approaching one of the most important philosophical questions in Openness:

What does it mean to know something when we recognize that our knowing is constructed, experientially situated, potentially incomplete, and always available for reconsideration?

And an even more difficult question follows:

Why would anyone willingly adopt an approach to understanding that explicitly refuses to guarantee the certainty so many people seek from philosophy, religion, and established systems of belief?

My provisional assessment is that Openness is developing something philosophically substantive: a form of experientially rooted, reflexive, fallibilist, and generative knowing.

It is neither simply subjective belief nor conventional objective certainty. It is a way of understanding in which the conceptual structure used to make sense of experience is itself available for examination, criticism, and reconstruction.

But I would make one important distinction at the outset.

Openness may be developing a different kind of knowing, without necessarily requiring a different kind of truth.

That distinction may resolve much of the tension you're feeling between Openness and philosophy.

## 1. What kind of knowing is being constructed?

Consider the chain you propose:

The Experiential Root

The Mystery · Awareness · My Self

Experiential Derivation and Generalization

A traceable network of Fiat Forms

The Mind · The Imagination · Mental Models · Knowing · Not-Knowing · …

Encounter, inquiry, application, and correction

Further possibilities and revised understanding

New concepts may transform their own conceptual ancestry

Each Fiat Form provides an identifiable meaning. Some meanings can be traced directly to recognizable experience; others may depend upon intermediate concepts, generalizations, abstractions, or analogical projections.

What is interesting is that the resulting conceptual network is not merely a collection of propositions about reality.

It can also describe how its own propositions became available.

For example, consider The Mind.

I encounter thinking, remembering, imagining, and feeling within Awareness. I also infer processes from their effects.

I give those phenomena a proposed conceptual unity: The Mind.

I can then investigate whether something corresponding to The Mind is recognizable in another person, an animal, or an unfamiliar computational system.

But throughout this development, I can continue to distinguish:

- What I originally encountered.
- What I inferred or generalized.
- What I established through Fiat.
- What I projected into another context.
- What evidence supports the resulting applications.
- What remains unknown.

That is a significant intellectual capability.

Instead of treating a concept as though it arrived fully formed with an unquestionable meaning, Openness may preserve a traceable relationship between its experiential grounding, conceptual construction, and subsequent justification.

### The reflexive dimension

Now consider what happens when the same approach is applied to Experiential Fiat Anchor itself.

The framework can ask:

Where did this concept come from?

Can its meaning genuinely be traced to The Experiential Root?

Did we introduce assumptions during its construction?

Are the proposed distinctions useful?

What would show that they are mistaken or unnecessary?

Could the whole idea of The Experiential Root be incomplete or wrong?

This produces a kind of reflexivity.

The framework does not exempt its own foundations, operations, or preferred interpretations from the inquiry it applies to everything else.

That is more significant than simply stating that knowledge is uncertain.

It means the framework contains resources for investigating its own possible inadequacy.

However, I would distinguish self-correctability from self-correction. A framework can declare itself open to revision while never actually changing in response to criticism.

The real test will be whether Openness can recognize and act upon evidence that challenges its most cherished concepts—including The Experiential Root.

## 2. A different kind of knowledge—or a different relationship to truth?

I would distinguish four dimensions of the knowledge you're describing.

| Dimension    | What Openness might establish                                                                                    |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Experiential | I recognize the encounter from which a conceptual meaning begins.                                                |
| Genealogical | I can trace how a concept was derived, generalized, or transformed through Fiat Forms.                           |
| Reflexive    | I can examine the conceptual structure through which I claim to understand something, including its limitations. |
| Generative   | I can use the concept to interpret experience, develop further possibilities, and revise the concept itself.     |

These dimensions may operate together, but they provide different kinds of justification.

A concept's experiential origin does not establish that its interpretation is true. A traceable conceptual genealogy does not establish that every transformation was justified. Reflexivity does not guarantee freedom from error. Generative fruit does not establish factual accuracy.

Nevertheless, the combination is meaningful.

It gives us something that ordinary truth-claims often leave implicit: an account of where our understanding comes from, how it has been constructed, and how it remains answerable to future encounters.

### A critical distinction: provenance is not proof

Imagine a chain:

My encounter with thoughts and feelings → The Mind → Mental Models → An explanation of why someone behaved unexpectedly.

Perhaps each conceptual transition is carefully documented.

That traceability is valuable. We can identify which assumptions entered at each step and return to them when an explanation fails.

But a perfectly traceable chain of Fiat Forms could still lead to a false conclusion.

The genealogy explains how the meaning was constructed. It does not independently establish that the resulting explanation corresponds to reality.

This may seem disappointing, but it is precisely why The Mystery remains essential.

The Experiential Root itself is a Fiat Form, so it cannot simply certify the correctness of everything descended from it. Nor can Awareness be presumed to provide infallible evidence about everything appearing within experience.

Indeed, what counts as a directly recognizable experiential beginning may already depend partly upon concepts, expectations, language, and interpretation.

This is a substantial epistemological problem, not a minor technicality.

Yet it need not destroy the architecture. It means that Experiential Derivation provides traceability, while justification must also involve evidence, logical coherence, criticism, and encounters that might challenge the conceptual structure.

### Knowing the limits of knowing

Your proposed Not-Knowing adds something particularly interesting.

A person can simultaneously hold:

I have good reasons to understand this in a particular way.

And:

I recognize that my understanding may not exhaust what is real or possible.

These statements are compatible.

The second does not necessarily undermine the first. It qualifies the speaker's relationship to it.

In epistemology, this belongs partly to what is called metacognition or higher-order epistemic assessment: examining not only what one believes or knows, but also one's grounds, confidence, and limitations.

It also connects with virtue epistemology, which studies the qualities and practices of good inquiry, including intellectual humility and open-mindedness.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



But Openness may add a distinctive generative emphasis.

Instead of stopping at:

I recognize that I do not know everything.

It can continue:

What might the recognized limit make possible to understand, imagine, or create next?

Not-Knowing becomes both a qualification of existing understanding and a possible beginning for further creation.

I would provisionally call this reflexive generative knowing.

Not necessarily as another reified concept, but as a description of the kind of understanding the architecture might cultivate.

## 3. The software analogy is more substantial than it first appears

I think your comparison with software deserves serious attention.

Software is made from explicitly defined abstractions. Those abstractions are often designed around recognizable problems, but their usefulness does not guarantee that the designer completely understood the situation.

Programs can be tested, revised, extended, and sometimes replaced. Their behavior can expose weaknesses in the very abstractions from which they were constructed.

The analogy with Openness is striking.

| Software development             | Experientially rooted conceptual development                      |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Requirements and observations    | Experiential beginnings and recognizable problems                 |
| Abstractions and data structures | Fiat Forms and Fiat Anchors                                       |
| Dependencies                     | Experiential Derivations and conceptual relationships             |
| Interfaces                       | Generalized meanings made available beyond their original context |
| Execution                        | Application to encounters, explanations, and inquiry              |
| Tests                            | Evidence, criticism, logical analysis, and Analogical Discernment |
| Unexpected behavior              | Encounters that challenge an existing conceptual structure        |
| Refactoring                      | Revision of Fiat Anchors and conceptual relationships             |
| New capabilities                 | The Fruit of Further Possibility                                  |

Perhaps the most important similarity is that a well-designed abstraction can remain useful even when the system within which it operates changes.

A concept need not be discarded simply because its original explanation proves incomplete. Nor should it be retained merely because many other concepts depend upon it.

This provides an interesting interpretation of Recursive Becoming.

A new concept may lead us to revise one of its ancestors.

For example, investigating The Imagination may reveal that an existing definition of The Mind presumes a distinction between cognition and imagination that our encounters do not adequately support.

That discovery could motivate revision of The Mind, which might then transform how Mental Models are understood.

This is conceptual refactoring.

### Where the analogy breaks

Software has requirements, tests, and observable behavior, but even software cannot guarantee complete knowledge of all future operating conditions.

Philosophical inquiry has additional difficulties.

The world does not always provide unambiguous tests for metaphysical claims. Different conceptual structures may explain the same observations. Some interpretations concern meaning, ethics, or subjective experience rather than easily measurable outcomes.

And conceptual systems can sometimes manufacture the appearance of their own success.

An explanation may seem successful because it has quietly defined away everything that would challenge it.

This is the philosophical counterpart of a test suite that only checks what the programmer already expects.

The most important tests of Openness may therefore be those it does not design to confirm itself.

Can it encounter a concept that resists experiential derivation?

Can it preserve a justified conclusion without unnecessarily reopening it?

Can it acknowledge that another philosophical framework explains something better?

Can it abandon a cherished distinction when the distinction stops helping?

Can it identify when a beautiful and generative explanation is nevertheless false?

These are not objections external to Openness. They seem to follow from its own aspiration toward Self-Honesty.

## 4. The apparent opposition between Openness and philosophy

I would challenge one premise in your question:

> philosophy ... aspires to permanent objective "truth"

Some philosophical traditions do aspire to establish necessary, universal, or ultimately correct truths.

But philosophy as a whole does not require that knowledge be unrevisable.

Much of modern epistemology concerns how beliefs can be justified despite fallibility, how inquiry produces warranted conclusions, and how knowledge can remain responsive to correction.

Indeed, a major strand of pragmatist philosophy explicitly rejects the idea that inquiry must begin from absolutely certain foundations.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



Consider three different claims:

Reality might have determinate truths. This is a claim about reality.

Humans might be capable of knowing some of those truths. This is a claim about knowledge.

Humans can establish, in advance, that their conceptual formulations are permanently complete and immune to revision. This is a much stronger claim.

Openness can remain undecided about the first two without accepting the third.

And it can acknowledge the possibility that a Fiat Anchor is already ultimately correct.

That is important given your recent insistence that Fiat naming must not prejudge its own ultimate accuracy.

### Truth, warrant, and fruit

I would preserve a threefold distinction:

Truth concerns whether a claim is actually correct.

Warrant concerns whether available reasons justify holding or asserting it.

Fruit concerns what becomes possible or follows from adopting, exploring, or acting upon it.

These interact, but they are not identical.

A false assumption can generate a useful artistic discovery. A correct explanation might initially appear unproductive. A belief can be strongly warranted and later shown mistaken.

This is already consistent with the Engine's distinction:

Resonance ≠ Fruit ≠ Truth.

I would be cautious about calling the goal of Openness useful truth, because it could suggest that usefulness determines truth.

Instead, I would describe the aspiration as:

> To cultivate experientially grounded, responsibly warranted, and generatively fruitful understanding, while remaining open to whether its conceptual forms capture reality correctly.

This preserves the importance of truth without demanding certainty.

The distinction also matters because understanding is not necessarily one indivisible accomplishment. A conceptual form might correctly explain a limited relationship while remaining silent about other aspects of reality.

And sometimes, as you have emphasized, the original formulation might already be entirely correct. Openness should not require discovering an error in order to justify its continued existence.

### Pragmatism — the strongest philosophical parallel

Dewey may be closer to Openness than you presently suspect.

For Dewey, knowing is not merely the contemplation of fixed truths. Inquiry begins in a problematic situation and develops distinctions, hypotheses, and actions that can help resolve it.

What emerges from successful inquiry becomes a resource for subsequent inquiry, rather than something rendered immune to further examination.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



That is remarkably close to your description of Fiat Forms whose value lies partly in their explanatory and generative capacities.

Peirce adds another important dimension.

He understood beliefs as fallible while preserving a strong aspiration toward truth and reality. His account of inquiry emphasizes that no single individual possesses an unquestionable epistemic standpoint. Continued criticism by an open-ended community provides a possible corrective to the limits of individual understanding.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



This challenges the notion that a conceptual framework must have absolute foundations to be intellectually serious.

What Openness appears to add is a more explicit concern with experiential origin, artistic generation, and the reflexive derivation of concepts from The Experiential Root.

### Constructivism — useful, but a caution

Constructivist approaches illuminate the idea that knowledge involves active conceptual organization rather than passive copying.

Some stronger constructivist approaches evaluate concepts by their viability: how well they help us navigate the experiential world.

That resembles your software analogy.

But I would not commit Openness to the claim that knowledge can only be viable construction and never recognition of reality as it is.

Your recent work deliberately preserves the possibility that a chosen conceptual form may already be ultimately accurate.

Thus, I would regard constructivism as a useful lens, not a metaphysical commitment Openness must inherit.

### Embodied cognition and enactivism — a connection to The Experiential Root

Your proposed beginning with Awareness and My Self has an interesting parallel in embodied cognition.

Rather than assuming that cognition is merely the manipulation of internal representations, embodied and enactive approaches examine how understanding emerges through the activities of bodies interacting with their environments.

Enactivism includes several distinct research programs, but their common interest in active, situated cognition is directly relevant here.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



The significant difference is that Openness's Experiential Root is presently a conceptual and philosophical proposal, not a scientific theory of cognitive mechanisms.

That distinction should remain clear.

Nevertheless, embodied cognition may provide evidence and criticism relevant to how experiential concepts arise, interact, and become generalized.

### Philosophy of scientific models — an unexpectedly close parallel

Scientific models are frequently simplified, idealized, or constructed to illuminate particular aspects of phenomena.

They may be useful without being perfectly accurate representations of everything they describe.

For example, idealizations such as frictionless surfaces can provide substantial explanatory and predictive value even though actual physical systems do not perfectly satisfy their assumptions.

Philosophers of science debate whether such models should be understood instrumentally, realistically, or through more nuanced relationships between representation and reality.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

This seems especially relevant to Fiat Anchors.

A conceptual form can be constructed without its construction determining whether the relationships it identifies are real.

That might be one of the most important bridges between Openness and established philosophy.

## 6. Daoism — perhaps the closest existential companion

I think your attraction to Daoism is significant, though I would distinguish Daoism as a philosophical companion from Daoism as a complete conceptual equivalent of Openness.

In particular, the Zhuangzi seems especially relevant.

The text questions the authority of familiar distinctions and explores how what appears correct from one perspective may appear quite different from another.

But the Zhuangzi does not merely recommend replacing every judgment with indecision. Its practical examples often concern people who have developed remarkable skills for responding intelligently to changing circumstances.

This combination—skepticism toward fixed interpretations alongside competent, situated action—seems particularly close to what you are seeking.&#x20;

### The mystery of knowing and not-knowing in Daoism

Your concern about translation is well justified.

The Classical Chinese character 知 (zhī) can occur in contexts involving propositional knowledge, practical skill, and acquaintance or familiarity. Different grammatical constructions can produce different philosophical implications.

A recent scholarly analysis by Yinlin Guan argues that the Laozi does not simply reject knowledge. It both criticizes some uses of knowing and affirms forms of understanding that guide action, including reflection on one's cognitive limitations.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.tandfonline.com\&sz=32)

Taylor & Francis Online



Consider Chapter 71 of the Daodejing:

## 知不知上，不知知病

Zhī bù zhī shàng, bù zhī zhī bìng

A possible interpretive rendering is:

Recognizing not-knowing is excellent; failing to recognize one's not-knowing is a problem.

But this should not be mistaken for a uniquely authoritative translation. The grammar and philosophical significance of the phrase are open to interpretation.

What's important for our inquiry is that the passage offers a remarkably close parallel to the distinction you have developed between ordinary ignorance and reflexively recognized Not-Knowing.

However, Daoist treatments of knowing can go beyond the individual recognition of epistemic limitations.

They can question whether the distinctions through which we organize knowledge are themselves appropriate to the changing relationships of the world.

### Zhuangzi's more radical challenge

There is a particularly intriguing exchange in Chapter 2 of the Zhuangzi concerning knowing and not-knowing.

The dialogue asks, in effect, how one could know whether what one calls knowledge might actually be not-knowing, or vice versa.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.sydney.edu.au\&sz=32)

Plato's Encyclopedia



This raises a question deeper than recognizing uncertainty about a particular proposition.

How do I know that my distinction between knowing and not-knowing is itself correct?

That question seems almost designed to challenge The Experiential Root.

Suppose Openness constructs a careful definition of Not-Knowing.

Then it recognizes that this definition is a Fiat Form.

Then it acknowledges that the distinction between Knowing and Not-Knowing might be incomplete.

The framework must now apply Not-Knowing to its own definition of Not-Knowing.

This need not produce an infinite regress in which nothing can be understood.

It may instead produce the philosophical analogue of an ongoing capacity for correction: a willingness to revisit even the distinctions by which inquiry evaluates itself.

There is a profound similarity here, though I would resist claiming that Zhuangzi and Openness are saying exactly the same thing.

The Zhuangzi arises from a different conceptual, linguistic, and historical world. Openness's explicit architecture of Fiat Forms and experiential derivations is its own proposal.

Moreover, Daoist texts sometimes make substantial claims about the dào, nature, and the relationship between wholes and parts. Openness should not silently inherit those ontological commitments merely because it shares their sensitivity to uncertainty.&#x20;

### Another spiritual comparison: Madhyamaka Buddhism

Madhyamaka offers an additional, potentially illuminating comparison.

The tradition distinguishes conventional understanding from ultimate understanding and questions whether the entities and distinctions of ordinary discourse possess independent inherent natures.

It also contains the philosophical challenge of applying its critique to its own concepts.

That resembles Openness's willingness to question even its foundational Fiat Forms.

But Madhyamaka is not simply a doctrine of provisional usefulness. It advances substantive arguments concerning emptiness, dependent origination, and ultimate reality.&#x20;

## 7. Why would anyone adopt Openness?

This may be the hardest and most consequential question you've raised.

If Openness offers neither permanent metaphysical security nor the promise of an unquestionably correct worldview, what does someone gain?

My first response is that most people do not need to adopt an entire philosophical architecture to benefit from a way of encountering uncertainty.

Think about learning to swim.

A person does not need to understand fluid dynamics before becoming capable of moving through water.

Likewise, someone may become more capable of recognizing assumptions, reconsidering explanations, and responding to uncertainty without understanding Fiat Form, Experiential Derivation, or Analogical Projection.

This distinction between knowing how and knowing that is relevant both to pragmatism and to the Daoist discussions of practiced skill.&#x20;

### An ordinary example

Imagine two friends having an argument.

One says something that the other experiences as dismissive.

An immediate interpretation arises:

My friend doesn't respect me.

That interpretation might be correct.

But perhaps the friend was distracted, misunderstood the conversation, or was responding to something else.

An Openness-inspired encounter would not require abandoning the initial feeling or pretending that every explanation is equally likely.

It could begin with something simpler:

I felt dismissed. I believe my friend intended to dismiss me, but I do not know that for certain.

That is already a meaningful change.

The experience remains recognizable. The interpretation becomes distinguishable from the experience. Further explanations become available.

The person may ask a question, express a concern, decide to set a boundary, or simply reconsider what occurred.

No technical terminology is required.

The benefit is not guaranteed emotional comfort, perfect conflict resolution, or ultimate truth.

The benefit is additional freedom to respond without being completely governed by an interpretation that has not yet been established.

This is the kind of experiential fruit that could make Openness attractive without first requiring philosophical allegiance.

### But perhaps many people will not want it

I think it is important not to assume that everyone will find the framework desirable.

Some people value the stability of existing religious, philosophical, or communal commitments. Others may find explicit reflection on uncertainty tiring, unnecessary, or unhelpful for their present circumstances.

And there are situations where continually reopening interpretations is counterproductive.

Someone performing a familiar, well-understood task may benefit more from practiced competence than philosophical reflection.

A person may need to make a timely decision rather than explore every conceivable alternative.

Openness should be capable of recognizing these limits in its own applicability.

A framework devoted to Nonimposition should allow someone to decline it without being characterized as insufficiently open.

That seems like a particularly demanding test of its values.

## 8. The question of firm ground

You write that Openness does not give its believers:

> a perfectly firm ground to stand on

I would challenge the word believers here, not because belief is forbidden, but because it may imply a relationship that Openness does not require.

Perhaps the relevant distinction is between believing in Openness and practicing Openness.

One can use its concepts provisionally without accepting its entire architecture as a true account of reality.

But perhaps another person might adopt Openness as a deeply held philosophical commitment.

That possibility should remain available too.

What matters is whether such commitment requires suspending Self-Honesty or granting the framework immunity from criticism.

### Is there a more stable ground available?

I would also question whether Openness necessarily leaves us with no firm ground.

The distinction may be between certainty of foundations and reliability of practices.

For example, a person may lack certainty about the ultimate nature of Awareness while possessing well-justified knowledge about how to perform a particular task.

They can act on that knowledge without resolving consciousness.

Likewise, a philosophical commitment can remain stable over a lifetime while remaining, in principle, corrigible.

Fallibility does not mean all beliefs are equally fragile.

And revisability does not mean incessant revision.

There is a valuable lesson here from Peirce: philosophical inquiry need not begin by doubting everything simultaneously. It can begin from what is already reasonably accepted and investigate difficulties as they arise.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



This could be important for Openness.

We do not need to stand on perfectly indubitable ground to stand responsibly.

Nor must we continually dismantle the ground that currently supports us.

The aim may be to develop the capacity to recognize when a foundation deserves to be questioned, rather than making questioning itself compulsory.

## 9. What, ultimately, is Openness trying to become?

I see at least three possibilities that should remain distinguishable.

As philosophy, Openness investigates how experience, conceptual construction, knowing, not-knowing, and further possibility relate. It can develop arguments and invite criticism from other philosophical traditions.

As a generative practice, Openness develops reusable resources for creating concepts, interpretations, and Transformative Art. Here, fruitfulness and further possibility are central, while remaining distinguishable from factual truth.

As a spiritual orientation, Openness may cultivate a lived relationship with uncertainty, embodiment, mystery, and becoming without requiring the acceptance of particular metaphysical doctrines.

The interesting possibility is not merely that these three functions coexist.

It is that they may correct and enrich one another.

Philosophy can criticize what artistic imagination proposes.

Art can make philosophical possibilities experientially recognizable.

Spiritual contemplation can explore how one lives with distinctions that philosophy cannot conclusively resolve.

And new encounters may transform all three.

This is why I would not reduce Openness to pragmatism, constructivism, Daoism, or a theory of knowledge.

Its proposed architecture is sufficiently distinctive to deserve investigation on its own terms, while remaining answerable to those traditions.

## 10. A possible characterization

I would provisionally describe the emerging approach as:

Experientially Rooted Generative Fallibilism

Not necessarily a new reified concept, but a name for the philosophical character of the framework you're developing.

Experientially rooted because concepts preferentially retain meaningful relationships to The Experiential Root, without requiring that every possible concept ultimately be traceable to it.

Generative because understanding is valued partly for what it can explain, illuminate, create, and make possible.

Fallibilist because conceptual forms remain open to criticism, correction, and revision, without assuming that they are necessarily incorrect or incomplete.

Reflexive because these commitments also apply to the framework itself.

I would include that fourth characteristic explicitly in a fuller account, even if it makes the name unwieldy.

The result is not a claim that truth is whatever proves useful.

It is a proposal for how to develop and inhabit understanding responsibly when our relationship to ultimate truth remains uncertain.

And perhaps this provides a way to describe why someone might adopt it.

Not because Openness promises that its forms will never fail.

Not because every future revision will represent progress.

Not because The Mystery guarantees limitless meaning.

But because the practice may cultivate a particular capability:

To understand enough to act, recognize enough not to mistake understanding for omniscience, and remain capable of learning from what the present understanding cannot yet accommodate.

## 11. The question I think is most worth contemplating next

There is an unresolved issue beneath the entire proposal.

If Openness is committed to preserving further possibility, what happens when that commitment conflicts with something else we value?

For example, suppose a particular inquiry has produced a conclusion supported by extensive evidence.

Should Openness continue generating alternatives simply because additional possibilities are conceivable?

Or should it recognize that, for the present purpose, the inquiry is sufficiently resolved?

I would favor the latter when warranted.

But that means further possibility cannot be Openness's only measure of value.

The Engine already contains resources for addressing this: Fruitional Discernment, Self-Honesty, Epistemic Humility, and What Resonates, What Follows & What Is True.

Perhaps what is emerging is not simply a philosophy of openness to possibility.

Perhaps it is an attempt to understand how to remain open without becoming unable to commit, and how to commit without becoming closed.

That tension may be more central than any particular Fiat Form.

And it returns us to your question about Daoism.

What attracts you may not be Daoism's particular account of reality, nor even its teachings about not-knowing.

It may be the possibility of a way of living in which the need to act, understand, choose, and create does not require pretending that The Mystery has been exhausted.

I would leave you with a provisional formulation:

> Openness does not seek to replace truth with usefulness, or knowledge with uncertainty. It seeks to cultivate understanding that remains accountable to experience, reason, and consequence while recognizing that its own conceptual forms may be incomplete, mistaken, or already correct. Such understanding may support action and creation without requiring final certainty, and may welcome future correction without requiring that correction ever come.

And perhaps an even simpler expression:

The ground need not be perfectly certain for us to walk upon it. Nor must the path be final for the journey to be meaningful.

That seems to me a philosophically defensible aspiration—and one that could be practiced long before someone understands the architecture intended to express it.
