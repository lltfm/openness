# Discussing changes to "The Return To Openness" concept

I think the Openness Epitome reveals something important that our previous definition missed: The Return to Openness is not primarily about recovering from Blind Certainty. It is about the recurring rhythm of Openness, decision, action, and renewed Openness that living requires.

The earlier definition made the Return sound like a remedy for a problem: we become closed-minded, recognize our limitations, and reopen ourselves to inquiry. That is one instance of returning, but it is not the central idea you're describing.

Your Epitome suggests something deeper:

We cannot remain indefinitely suspended in Openness because life requires us to decide and act. Yet the necessity of committing ourselves to action does not require us to cling permanently to the judgments that informed that action.

This is the distinctive philosophical tension worth preserving. We want to be fully committed in our actions without becoming blindly certain about the understandings from which those actions arose.

And the passage from Being Open, particularly its image of stepping back into the open sky of Awareness, brings another dimension: we can hold feelings, desires, judgments, and experiences without immediately identifying with them or being compelled to act upon them.

That suggests a concept spanning Philosophical, Spiritual, and potentially Generative Openness.

I would therefore retain The Return to Openness, but redefine it as a recurring practice or rhythm of living, rather than a technical term for reconsideration.

## Proposed canonical definition

# The Return to Openness

## Foundational Definition

**The Return to Openness** is the recurring movement by which a Self, having formed a judgment, made a decision, or committed to action, steps back from the immediacy of that commitment and becomes receptive again to what is, what may be true, and what remains possible.

Life requires Selves to decide and act, often without complete knowledge or certainty. A Self cannot always remain in contemplation, suspend judgment indefinitely, or await the resolution of every uncertainty. Circumstances call for choices, commitments, and actions through which the possibilities of life become actual.

Openness does not require withholding such commitments. A Self may consider what is known, acknowledge what remains uncertain, make the best judgment available, and commit fully to acting upon it.

Yet **commitment to action need not become unquestionable commitment to the understanding that informed it.**

Having acted, a Self may step back, releasing its immediate identification with the decision or course of action, without disowning that action or its consequences. It may listen again, examine what has happened, recognize previously unseen possibilities, and reconsider what remains justified.

This movement need not follow an error or a period of Blind Certainty. Even a well-considered and successful action may be followed by a Return to Openness.

Nor must the Return wait until an action has ended. During an ongoing commitment, a Self may repeatedly recover the capacity to listen, reconsider, and respond to emerging circumstances.

The Return to Openness may occur as an ordinary, largely unnoticed rhythm of living: encountering, considering, deciding, acting, and becoming receptive again. It may also be cultivated intentionally as a practice of Awareness, reflection, humility, and renewed engagement with reality.

Its aspiration is not to escape commitment but to make commitment compatible with continuing Openness.

**To decide and act with conviction when life requires it, while remaining willing to release one's hold upon what one thought one knew, and return again to what is.**

## Essential Distinctions

**Openness versus Indecision.** Openness does not require avoiding decisions or indefinitely suspending judgment. A Self may remain attentive to uncertainty while recognizing that circumstances require action. Indecision may sometimes preserve possibilities, but it may also prevent necessary or meaningful action.

**Commitment versus Certainty.** A Self may commit fully to a chosen action without claiming complete certainty about the circumstances, evidence, or interpretations informing it. Commitment concerns the seriousness with which the Self undertakes an action; certainty concerns the confidence placed in an understanding. The two need not coincide.

**Holding versus Clinging.** Holding a belief, commitment, feeling, or interpretation allows it to remain present and meaningful without making it immune to reconsideration. Clinging arises when attachment interferes with the capacity to recognize change, limitation, or alternative possibilities. The Return does not require abandoning what is held, but loosening the demand that it remain unchanged.

**Stepping Back versus Withdrawing.** Stepping back involves recovering perspective from the immediacy of experience, judgment, or action. It need not mean withdrawing from the world, relinquishing responsibility, or becoming indifferent to consequences. The Self may return to the same commitment with renewed understanding, or discover that another response is appropriate.

**Feeling versus Compulsion.** A feeling may be experienced and attended to without requiring its immediate expression, suppression, or fulfillment. Feelings may reveal needs, desires, concerns, and interpretations worthy of attention, but their presence does not alone determine what action is justified. Holding a feeling in Awareness may allow a Self to choose how to respond rather than react automatically.

**The Return versus Correction.** Returning to Openness does not necessarily involve discovering an error, reversing a decision, or changing a belief. What was previously understood may remain justified. The Return restores receptivity to further experience and understanding without predetermining what will be discovered.

**The Return versus Blind Certainty.** Blind Certainty closes meaningful inquiry around a conviction. The Return to Openness may interrupt such closure, but it is not limited to recovering from it. A Self may return after any consequential commitment, whether or not that commitment involved Blind Certainty.

**A Living Rhythm versus a Fixed Sequence.** Openness, decision, action, and renewed Openness need not occur as separate or strictly ordered stages. A Self may remain receptive while acting, reconsider a judgment during an ongoing commitment, or return repeatedly to reflection as circumstances evolve. The Return names a recurring possibility of living rather than a prescribed procedure.

## Relationship to Openness

The Return to Openness expresses a relationship among Philosophical, Spiritual, and Generative Openness.

Within **Philosophical Openness**, it concerns the relationship between inquiry, judgment, uncertainty, and action. A Self may form conclusions and act upon its best available understanding without regarding those conclusions as beyond reconsideration. The Return preserves the distinction between the practical necessity of deciding and the epistemic justification of what has been decided.

Within **Spiritual Openness**, the Return may be cultivated as a lived practice of Awareness: stepping back from the immediacy of thought, feeling, desire, and action; listening without immediately judging or reacting; and holding experience without clinging to it.

A Self may encounter anger without becoming only anger, experience longing without being compelled to fulfill it, or recognize uncertainty without requiring its immediate resolution. Such experiences need not be rejected or overcome before life can continue. They may be held within an expanding capacity to encounter what is.

The Return may therefore be experienced as a renewed willingness to allow the flow of The Mystery to unfold, without demanding that reality conform to one's expectations, preferences, or desires.

Within **Generative Openness**, stepping back may loosen assumptions or expectations that have narrowed the possibilities available to imagination and action. New understandings, interpretations, or creative directions may become possible when a Self releases its exclusive attachment to a previously chosen course.

The Return also participates in **The Intersubjective**. Through renewed listening, a Self may encounter experiences, evidence, and perspectives that could not be reached through solitary reflection. Acknowledging Preferred Stories, practicing Epistemic Hospitality, and preserving Interpretive Sovereignty may support this process.

Across these expressions, the Return preserves a common aspiration: to engage fully with life without allowing the necessary commitments of living to foreclose further encounter with reality.

## Examples

### Example One: The Rhythm of Decision and Action

Person A is responsible for leading a project that requires an important decision.

The available information is incomplete. Different participants offer competing interpretations, and postponing the decision indefinitely would carry consequences of its own.

Person A listens, examines the available evidence, considers alternatives, and acknowledges what remains uncertain.

Eventually, a decision must be made.

Person A chooses a course of action and commits to carrying it out. Rather than acting halfheartedly because uncertainty remains, Person A gives the decision the attention, energy, and determination necessary to make it effective.

Later, after the initial action has been undertaken, Person A steps back.

They consider what happened, listen to those affected, examine new evidence, and reflect upon assumptions that may have influenced the original judgment.

Perhaps the decision was sound. Perhaps it was mistaken. Perhaps the circumstances have changed, or new possibilities have emerged.

The Return does not require Person A to regret the original decision or withdraw their commitment to the project.

It allows them to recognize that having committed fully to an action does not require defending its original justification indefinitely.

They may continue, adjust, or choose another course.

The decision was real. The commitment was genuine. The consequences remain.

And yet Openness remains possible.

### Example Two: Holding a Feeling Without Being Governed by It

Person A experiences anger after a difficult encounter with Person B.

The anger arises with a desire to respond immediately, perhaps to accuse Person B of unfairness or demand acknowledgment of an injury.

Rather than acting upon that impulse, Person A pauses.

They allow themselves to experience the anger without dismissing, suppressing, or immediately attempting to satisfy it.

As Person A attends to the feeling, they may recognize hurt, fear, disappointment, or an unmet need beneath the initial anger. They may also notice a Preferred Story about what occurred or what Person B intended.

Person A need not determine immediately which interpretations are correct.

They may simply hold the experience with enough openness to distinguish what they feel, what they believe, and what they wish to do.

Eventually, Person A may decide that a response is needed. They might explain their experience to Person B, establish a boundary, seek reconciliation, or take another appropriate action.

They may act firmly and with conviction.

Later, Person A may step back again, reflecting upon the encounter, their response, and whatever the experience has revealed.

Their anger need not have disappeared. Nor must they conclude that their original understanding was mistaken.

The Return lies in their continuing ability to encounter the feeling and the situation without being compelled to treat their first reaction as the final truth of the experience.

The feeling was allowed. The action was chosen. Openness remains available.

## Boundary

The Return to Openness does not require perpetual self-examination, hesitation, or an inability to sustain commitments.

It does not require withholding necessary action until uncertainty has been resolved, nor abandoning responsibilities whenever new possibilities arise.

Returning to Openness does not erase the consequences of action. A Self remains responsible for what has been done, including any resulting need for repair, accountability, or continued commitment.

The Return also does not require emotional detachment, suppression of feeling, passive acceptance of harm, or the suspension of justified boundaries.

Stepping back may be useful, but it is not always immediately possible or appropriate. Circumstances may require sustained attention, urgent action, rest, protection, or support from others.

Nor does the Return guarantee that reflection will reveal truth, resolve uncertainty, transform difficult feelings, or produce new creative possibilities.

It does not establish a final state of enlightenment in which Blind Certainty, Preferred Stories, or mistaken judgment become impossible.

The Return to Openness is a recurring possibility rather than a permanent achievement.

Its significance lies in the recognition that **the necessity of deciding and acting does not require us to cease being open to what our decisions and actions have yet to reveal.**

We may commit ourselves fully to living, while ever returning to Openness.

## Where this belongs in the architecture of Openness

I see three distinct but complementary dimensions of the concept.

Philosophical Openness — the principle

The necessity of deciding does not confer certainty upon the beliefs informing our decisions. We can commit to action while preserving the possibility of revising our understanding.

Spiritual Openness — the lived practice

We can step back, listen, hold experience without clinging, and allow life to unfold without requiring it to conform to our desires. This gives the Return its experiential and contemplative depth.

Generative Openness — the possibility

Stepping back can reveal creative alternatives that commitment to a single interpretation or course of action had obscured. The Return may generate further possibilities, though it need not always do so.

I would give the concept its primary conceptual grounding in Philosophical Openness, while recognizing that its fullest expression as a practice belongs equally to Spiritual Openness.

The distinction matters because the philosophical principle does not depend upon any particular contemplative practice or spiritual understanding. A person can embrace it entirely through reasoning, reflective judgment, and responsible action.

Spiritual Openness explores how that same principle can be lived and experienced.

Generative Openness concerns what may become possible as a result.

## What Being Open contributes

The passage How do we get rid of bad feelings? is especially important because it broadens the Return beyond beliefs and judgments.

The ocean-wave metaphor depicts feelings as experiences that arise without necessarily being chosen. The subsequent image of stepping back into the open sky of Awareness suggests that a Self need not become wholly identified with each feeling or compelled by its immediate desires.

Three insights are relevant.

First, allowing is not the same as acting. Feeling anger does not require expressing it destructively; feeling longing does not require fulfilling it. Nor does Openness demand suppression. A Self may attend to the feeling and then decide whether and how to act.

Second, stepping back is not escaping experience. The passage presents a welcoming relationship to feeling rather than an attempt to become untouched by it. This is why the Return should not be defined simply as detachment.

Third, the aim is not necessarily to eliminate the feeling. The ending of the passage is particularly relevant: transformation may occur through presence without requiring the fulfillment of desire.

That insight supports a subtle distinction: we can return to Openness while still feeling angry, afraid, disappointed, or uncertain. We need not wait until those experiences have disappeared.

It also suggests that the Return may occur before action, during action, or afterward. Its deepest quality is not its position in a sequence but the renewed capacity to hold experience without being governed entirely by our first response to it.

## Does The Return to Openness deserve its own canonical definition?

I think it does, with one qualification.

You are right that merely defining it as stepping back and becoming open again adds little. Neither phrase is especially ambiguous, and neither requires specialized terminology.

What makes the concept philosophically meaningful is the apparent tension between two desirable commitments:

- To encounter reality openly, admitting uncertainty and remaining receptive to what may be true.
- To participate fully in life, making consequential decisions and committing ourselves to actions that cannot always await certainty.

The Return to Openness is a proposed way of living with both commitments without sacrificing either.

That is a substantive principle, rather than an elaboration of an intuitively obvious phrase.

The other consideration is its status. I would not present the Return as an independently existing structure or metaphysical entity. It is more naturally understood as a named practice, recurring movement, or rhythm of living. That distinction gives us room to preserve the idea without over-reifying it.

One last formulation captures what I think is most distinctive:

> We may commit fully to what we choose to do without clinging to what we believed when we chose it.

This also explains why the Epitome concludes not with understanding, knowledge, or permanent serenity, but with:

> Deciding & acting when we must, but ever returning to Openness.

I would preserve that line unchanged. The proposed definition gives it philosophical substance, while the Epitome expresses it in a form that can be remembered and lived.
