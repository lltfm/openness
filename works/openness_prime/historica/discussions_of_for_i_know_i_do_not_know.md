Q: should "For I Know I Do Not Know" be more accurately stated as "For I Know I Do Not Ultimately Know"? It is loses a lot of its poetic resonances but I think it includes a perhaps important distinction

I think you've identified an important distinction, but I would retain “For I Know I Do Not Know” as the foundational confession and incorporate ultimately into its explanation.

The difference is more philosophically significant than it first appears.

## 1. What “ultimately” adds

For I Know I Do Not Know expresses epistemic humility. It acknowledges the limits of present understanding without specifying where those limits lie.

For I Know I Do Not Ultimately Know makes a more specific claim: even when we possess genuine knowledge, we may lack an ultimate understanding of its foundations, causes, significance, or consequences.

Consider Awareness.

I know that I am experiencing something. I can investigate my perceptions, body, and experiences. I can develop justified explanations.

Yet I cannot presently demonstrate why subjective Awareness exists at all, why it is experienced from this particular perspective, or what ultimately becomes of it.

The addition of ultimately captures this distinction beautifully.

It also connects with the Chinese Farmer's “Maybe”: we may understand a particular event without knowing the ultimate fruit of its consequences.

Knowing something does not mean knowing all that it is, why it is, or what may follow from it.

## 2. But “ultimately” introduces another problem

It can be understood in two ways.

Epistemically modest: My present understanding does not establish ultimate understanding.

Epistemically absolute: Ultimate understanding is impossible.

The first is consistent with Openness.

The second is a much stronger philosophical claim, and potentially a Blind Certainty of its own.

We do not know whether some apparently ultimate mysteries may eventually become understandable. Nor must every subject possess an endlessly inaccessible explanation.

There is another subtle change: the original confession invites Self to recognize specific things it does not know, whereas the revised confession directs attention toward a particular kind of knowing.

That makes it more precise in one respect, but less encompassing in another.

## 3. Why I prefer the original

I think the original's ambiguity is unusually productive.

> For I Know I Do Not Know.

It invites several interpretations without requiring any single one:

- I know some things, but not everything.
- I may be mistaken about what I think I know.
- My present understanding may conceal further possibilities.
- I do not know the ultimate origins, purposes, or consequences of existence.
- Even my understanding of what it means to know remains open to inquiry.

This is an example where Anchored Ambiguity genuinely serves the concept.

The minimal confession is recognizable and defensible. Its further dimensions can unfold through Philosophical and Spiritual Openness.

Adding ultimately privileges one of those dimensions at the expense of the others.

And because the phrase is foundational, I think that loss matters.

## 4. My suggested refinement

Rather than change the confession, I would strengthen the accompanying explanation.

The foundational confession of Openness is:

**For I Know I Do Not Know.**

This does not deny knowledge or justified certainty. It recognizes that what we know may not exhaust what is, that our understandings remain open to revision, and that even genuine knowledge need not establish ultimate understanding.

To know something is not necessarily to know all that it means, why it is, or what may yet follow.

The Mystery remains open.

The last sentence is optional, but I like how it returns us from an epistemological distinction to the wider posture of Openness.

## 5. An even deeper implication

Your proposed revision reveals a distinction that may eventually matter more than the wording of the confession:

The distinction between knowing what is true and knowing the ultimate significance of what is true.

These are not the same.

A person may know they have suffered a loss without knowing what that loss will eventually mean to their life.

A philosopher may establish a justified conclusion without knowing every consequence of it.

A Noble Heart may know that it has made The First Crossing without knowing its ultimate fruit.

And a spiritual practitioner may cultivate genuine wisdom without knowing whether their preferred metaphysical explanation is true.

This is almost exactly the distinction we have been developing between philosophical truth, wisdom, and the Fruit of Further Possibility.

It is also why Openness need not diminish actual knowledge to preserve The Mystery.

Knowledge and Mystery are not opposites. Knowledge may reveal further Mystery.

My recommendation, then, is to preserve the original as an Epitome-like foundational confession and use your new distinction to deepen its philosophical explanation.

“For I Know I Do Not Know” is more than an admission of ignorance.

It expresses the possibility that whatever we know, there may be more to understand.

And crucially, it does not insist that the more must remain forever beyond our reach.

Q: I think what I hope that "For I Know I Ultimately Do Not Know" makes explicit is the idea that all knowing is provisional -- but perhaps even this idea needs to be open to "not knowing"? -- stating the principle of not knowing I think requires a lot of care since it is very easy to create a well-meaning phraseology that ends up closing certain possible interpretations or implying possibilities that aren't intended

I agree, and I think you've identified a subtle danger: a philosophy devoted to preserving possibility can unintentionally close possibilities through the very language it uses to express that devotion.

Consider the statement:

“All knowing is provisional.”

It beautifully expresses epistemic humility. But taken as an absolute claim, it raises a question: Is that statement itself provisional?

And if it is, must we remain open to the possibility that some knowledge is not provisional?

I think the answer, within Openness, is yes.

This leads to a distinction worth preserving:

Fallibility is not the same as uncertainty.

We may be justified in knowing something with great confidence while still acknowledging that our understanding, the grounds of our confidence, or the implications of what we know could be reconsidered. Revisability does not require treating every conclusion as doubtful.

More importantly, Openness need not establish the universal provisionality of knowledge as a truth in order to remain open to revising what it knows.

It is an aspiration and practice rather than a metaphysical conclusion about all possible knowledge.

This makes the original confession particularly effective:

> For I Know I Do Not Know.

It doesn't specify what I don't know.

It doesn't declare that ultimate knowledge is impossible.

It doesn't require that everything I believe be regarded as equally uncertain.

And importantly, it doesn't establish that my present understanding of knowing and not-knowing is itself complete.

That final point may be its most profound recursive possibility.

The confession opens the possibility of questioning even our understanding of the confession.

I think that is where your principle of Anchored Ambiguity is especially well employed—not by making the sentence more explicitly qualified, but by allowing a defensible minimal meaning to invite further inquiry.

There is also a distinction between semantic precision and semantic closure. A more precise statement can prevent misunderstanding, but excessive qualification may narrow the possibilities the expression was intended to invite.

The challenge is not to make every foundational expression immune to misinterpretation. That is probably unattainable.

It is to say enough to make honest inquiry possible without pretending to have settled what inquiry may yet reveal.

This returns us to a maxim we've already developed:

> Define precisely enough to make inquiry possible. Explore openly enough to make further meaning possible. Revise honestly when further inquiry reveals a better distinction.

I would leave the confession unchanged, as you've chosen, and resist burdening it with an elaborate set of qualifications.

Perhaps the simplest insight to preserve from this discussion is:

Even our understanding of what it means to not know remains open to inquiry.

And I think the remarkable thing about For I Know I Do Not Know is that it already allows that possibility without needing to say it.
